This guide covers the grammar and punctuation skills tested in the SAT Reading and Writing section. It focuses on sentence boundaries, punctuation, verb forms, agreement, modifiers, parallel structure, pronouns, and usage.

The material is organized around two skill areas: Boundaries (punctuation and sentence structure) and Form, Structure, and Sense (grammar and word forms).
---

# Part 0 — THE MOST IMPORTANT IDEA: STOP USING “WHAT SOUNDS GOOD”

This is the biggest shift.

On SAT grammar, don't ask:

> “Which answer sounds natural?”

Ask:

> **“What grammatical structure do I have?”**

Because SAT choices are intentionally written so that multiple wrong answers **sound fine**.

You want rules.

For punctuation:

1. Find clauses.
2. Determine whether each side is complete.
3. Pick the legal punctuation.

For grammar:

1. Identify what is being tested.
2. Find the two things that must agree.
3. Match them.

That is the whole game.

---

# Part 1 — BEFORE PUNCTUATION: FINITE VERBS

This is the foundation.

You cannot identify a complete sentence until you understand what counts as a real verb.

This guide calls this the prerequisite to clause identification.

---

## 1.1 What is a finite verb?

A **finite verb** is a verb that actually carries tense and can power a clause.

Examples:

> She **runs**.

> She **ran**.

> She **is running**.

> She **has studied**.

> The samples **were analyzed**.

All of these can function as the main verb.

---

## 1.2 What is NOT automatically a finite verb?

These can look like verbs but do not independently power a sentence:

### Participles

\[
\text{running, collected, written, analyzed}
\]

Example:

> **Running across the street**

Not a sentence.

There is no subject + complete finite verb.

---

### Infinitives

\[
\text{to run,\ to study,\ to analyze}
\]

> To finish the experiment.

Not a complete sentence.

---

### Gerunds

An \(-ing\) form acting as a noun.

> **Swimming** is healthy.

Here “swimming” is the subject.

The finite verb is:

> **is**

---

## 1.3 Compare these carefully

> **The researchers studying the samples.**

Wrong as a sentence.

“studying” is non-finite.

But:

> **The researchers are studying the samples.**

Now:

\[
\boxed{\text{are studying}}
\]

is finite.

---

Another:

> The data **collected** yesterday.

Fragment.

But:

> The data **were collected** yesterday.

Complete sentence.

---

## 1.4 The SAT finiteness trick

Suppose:

> The sculpture, ___ from marble, weighs over two tons.

Choices:

- carved
- was carved
- carving
- to carve

Find subject:

> sculpture

Does it already have a finite verb?

Yes:

> **weighs**

Therefore you do **not** need another finite verb.

You need a modifier:

> The sculpture, **carved** from marble, weighs over two tons.

Correct:

\[
\boxed{\text{carved}}
\]

Not:

> sculpture, **was carved** ..., weighs

because now you have two competing finite verbs without proper clause structure.

The source's decision rule is essentially:

\[
\boxed{\text{Already has finite verb? then use verbal}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{No finite verb yet? then need finite verb}}
\]

# Part 2 — INDEPENDENT VS DEPENDENT CLAUSES

THIS is the foundation of punctuation questions.

---

## 2.1 Independent Clause (IC)

An independent clause needs:

1. subject
2. finite verb
3. complete thought

Example:

> The experiment failed.

Subject:

> experiment

Finite verb:

> failed

Complete thought:

yes.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{IC}
\]

---

## 2.2 Dependent Clause (DC)

A dependent clause can have a subject and verb but still cannot stand alone.

Example:

> Because the experiment failed

Subject:

> experiment

Finite verb:

> failed

But:

> Because the experiment failed...

Your brain asks:

> What happened because it failed?

Incomplete.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{DC}
\]

---

## 2.3 Subordinating words

These often create dependent clauses:

- because
- although
- since
- while
- if
- when
- unless
- whereas
- after
- before
- though
- even though

So:

> The team left.

IC.

But:

> Although the team left

DC.

The manual defines IC/DC exactly this way.

---

# Part 3 — THE COVER TEST

This should become automatic.

Suppose:

> The experiment failed ___ the team revised its hypothesis.

Ignore punctuation.

Cover the right side.

Read:

> The experiment failed.

Complete?

Yes.

\[
IC
\]

Now cover left.

Read:

> The team revised its hypothesis.

Complete?

Yes.

\[
IC
\]

So structure is:

\[
\boxed{IC\quad IC}
\]

Now punctuation rules become mechanical.

---

# Part 4 — JOINING TWO INDEPENDENT CLAUSES

If you have:

\[
IC + IC,
\]

there are several legal constructions.

---

## 4.1 Period

> The experiment failed. The team revised its hypothesis.

Correct.

Pattern:

\[
\boxed{IC. IC}
\]

---

## 4.2 Semicolon

> The experiment failed; the team revised its hypothesis.

Correct.

Pattern:

\[
\boxed{IC; IC}
\]

A semicolon behaves grammatically like a period.

---

## 4.3 Comma + FANBOYS

Memorize:

\[
\boxed{\text{FANBOYS}}
\]

**F**or  
**A**nd  
**N**or  
**B**ut  
**O**r  
**Y**et  
**S**o

Example:

> The experiment failed, **so** the team revised its hypothesis.

Pattern:

\[
\boxed{IC,\ FANBOYS\ IC}
\]

---

## 4.4 What is NOT legal?

> The experiment failed, the team revised its hypothesis.

That is:

\[
\boxed{IC,\ IC}
\]

This is a **comma splice**.

Wrong.

This is probably the single most important punctuation error to recognize.

---

## 4.5 SAT reflex

Whenever you see a comma:

Ask:

> Is there a complete sentence on BOTH sides?

If yes:

\[
\boxed{\text{comma alone = wrong}}
\]

unless a FANBOYS immediately participates in joining them.

This guide's master rule sheet gives these same legal IC–IC patterns.

---

# Part 5 — DEPENDENT CLAUSE FIRST VS SECOND

These behave differently.

---

## 5.1 DC first

> Because the equipment was faulty, the experiment failed.

Structure:

\[
DC,\ IC
\]

Rule:

\[
\boxed{\text{DC first then comma}}
\]

Examples:

> Although she was tired, she continued.

> When the study ended, the researchers published their findings.

> If the treatment succeeds, the trial will expand.

---

## 5.2 DC second

> The experiment failed because the equipment was faulty.

Structure:

\[
IC\ DC
\]

Usually no punctuation when the dependent clause is essential.

\[
\boxed{\text{IC + essential DC then usually nothing}}
\]

So:

> The experiment failed, because the equipment was faulty.

Usually unnecessary/wrong for SAT purposes in this ordinary essential construction.

---

## 5.3 Easy memory

\[
\boxed{\text{Because X, Y}}
\]

comma.

But:

\[
\boxed{\text{Y because X}}
\]

usually no comma.

---

# Part 6 — DIRECT VS INDIRECT QUESTIONS

This is easier than it looks.

---

## 6.1 Direct question

Actually asks something.

> Why did the expedition turn back?

Question mark.

---

## 6.2 Indirect question

Reports that somebody asked something.

> The researcher asked whether the expedition had turned back.

This is a statement.

No question mark.

---

Compare:

> She asked, “Will the experiment continue?”

Quoted words are a direct question:

\[
?
\]

But:

> She asked whether the experiment would continue.

Indirect:

\[
.
\]

This guide explicitly warns that **asked whether / asked if** is indirect and does not take a question mark.

---

# Part 7 — FANBOYS VS CONJUNCTIVE ADVERBS

This is a HUGE SAT distinction.

Meaning may be similar.

Grammar is different.

---

## 7.1 FANBOYS

Again:

\[
\boxed{\text{for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so}}
\]

They can actually join two independent clauses with a comma:

> The theory was promising, **but** evidence was limited.

Correct.

---

## 7.2 Conjunctive adverbs

Examples:

- however
- therefore
- moreover
- nevertheless
- consequently
- furthermore
- thus
- meanwhile
- otherwise

These are **not** FANBOYS.

So this is WRONG:

> The theory was promising, however, evidence was limited.

That gives:

\[
IC,\ however,\ IC
\]

Still basically a comma splice.

---

## 7.3 Correct structure

Use:

> The theory was promising; however, evidence was limited.

Pattern:

\[
\boxed{IC;\ however,\ IC}
\]

or:

> The theory was promising. However, evidence was limited.

This guide explicitly distinguishes FANBOYS as actual clause “glue” from conjunctive adverbs, which require a stronger boundary before them.

---

## 7.4 SAT reflex

See:

> however

Immediately think:

\[
\boxed{;\ however,}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{.\ However,}
\]

Not:

\[
\boxed{,\ however,}
\]

between two ICs.

Same for:

> therefore, moreover, consequently, nevertheless

---

# Part 8 — COLONS

Colon questions become extremely easy with one rule.

## 8.1 LEFT SIDE MUST BE COMPLETE

Before a colon:

\[
\boxed{\text{must be an IC}}
\]

After a colon:

it can be almost anything as long as it explains the left.

---

## 8.2 Correct examples

> The team needed three things: funding, equipment, and staff.

Left:

> The team needed three things.

Complete.

Therefore colon legal.

---

> The team had one option: start over.

Left complete.

Right is just a phrase.

Still legal.

---

> The result was clear: the hypothesis was wrong.

Left complete.

Right also complete.

Still legal.

---

## 8.3 Illegal colon

> The team's only option was to: start over.

Check left:

> The team's only option was to.

Incomplete.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{colon illegal}}
\]

---

## 8.4 “Such as” trap

Don't write:

> The museum contains artifacts such as: pottery and tools.

“such as” already introduces examples.

Correct:

> ...artifacts such as pottery and tools.

Your source states this exact left-side completeness test.

---

# Part 9 — DASHES

A single dash can sometimes behave like a colon:

> The evidence supported one conclusion—the theory was wrong.

But don't think:

> colon = dash always.

This guide explicitly warns they are stylistic cousins, not automatically interchangeable.

For SAT grammar, most importantly:

\[
\boxed{\text{single explanatory dash still needs a complete lead-in}}
\]

---

# Part 10 — ESSENTIAL VS NONESSENTIAL INFORMATION

This controls commas around phrases.

Use what this guide calls the **Deletion Test**.

---

## 10.1 Essential information

You need it to identify the noun.

Example:

> The notebook **that is covered in doodles** is mine.

Suppose there are many notebooks.

Remove:

> that is covered in doodles

Now:

> The notebook is mine.

Which notebook?

Unclear.

Therefore phrase is essential.

Rule:

\[
\boxed{\text{essential = NO commas}}
\]

---

## 10.2 Nonessential information

Example:

> My laptop, which is covered in stickers, is on the desk.

Remove:

> which is covered in stickers

You still have:

> My laptop is on the desk.

The noun is already identified.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{nonessential = commas}}
\]

---

## 10.3 “That” vs “which”

Useful SAT tendency:

\[
\boxed{\text{that then usually restrictive/essential}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{which then usually nonrestrictive}}
\]

So:

> the book **that** won the prize

usually no commas.

> the book, **which** won the prize,

commas.

But the real rule is essential vs nonessential, not blindly memorizing the words.

---

# Part 11 — APPOSITIVES

An appositive renames a noun.

Example:

> J.R.R. Tolkien, **a member of the Inklings**, wrote *The Hobbit*.

The inserted noun phrase is additional info.

Remove it:

> J.R.R. Tolkien wrote *The Hobbit*.

Still identifies Tolkien.

Therefore commas.

---

## 11.1 Essential name

> My friend Rebecca is coming.

If you have multiple friends, “Rebecca” identifies which friend.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{no commas}}
\]

not:

> My friend, Rebecca, is coming.

unless the context already uniquely identifies the friend.

---

# Part 12 — MATCHING PAIRS

If a nonessential insertion opens with a comma, close it with comma.

\[
\boxed{,\ ...\ ,}
\]

If dash:

\[
\boxed{-\ ...\ -}
\]

Parentheses:

\[
\boxed{(\ ...\ )}
\]

Do not mix.

Wrong:

> The result, surprisingly—confirmed the hypothesis.

Opening comma + closing dash.

Wrong.

Correct:

> The result, surprisingly, confirmed...

or:

> The result—surprisingly—confirmed...

---

# Part 13 — NO PUNCTUATION QUESTIONS

These are very important because students overpunctuate.

The manual explicitly says the SAT tests situations where **nothing** belongs in the blank.

---

## 13.1 Never separate subject and verb

Wrong:

> The study conducted by several researchers, **found** an unexpected result.

Subject:

> study

Verb:

> found

Everything between them is just description.

Correct:

> The study conducted by several researchers found an unexpected result.

\[
\boxed{\text{Subject + verb cannot be split with a random comma}}
\]

---

## 13.2 Long subjects are traps

Example:

> An analysis of hundreds of archaeological sites across several countries ___ suggests that...

You may feel a pause.

Ignore your ear.

Subject:

> analysis

Verb:

> suggests

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{NO punctuation}}
\]

---

## 13.3 Compound predicate

One subject doing two actions.

> The researcher collected the samples and analyzed them.

Subject:

> researcher

Verbs:

> collected

> analyzed

Only one subject.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{no comma before and}}
\]

Wrong:

> The researcher collected the samples, and analyzed them.

---

## 13.4 Contrast with two ICs

> The researcher collected the samples, and **the laboratory analyzed them**.

Now second half has its own subject:

> laboratory

Therefore:

\[
IC,\ and\ IC.
\]

Comma is legal.

---

# Part 14 — PARTICIPIAL PHRASES

Very common.

> Urban heat islands occur, **causing higher temperatures**.

“causing...” has no subject.

It is not a clause.

Therefore you cannot use semicolon:

> occur; causing...

Wrong.

Because a semicolon requires a complete clause after it.

Correct:

\[
\boxed{\text{IC, participial phrase}}
\]

This guide explicitly teaches \(-ing/-ed\) phrases as comma-attached nonclauses.

---

# Part 15 — LISTS

## 15.1 Simple list

> apples, bananas, and oranges

Commas.

---

## 15.2 Complex list

If list items themselves contain commas, use semicolons between the main items.

Example:

> researchers from Boston, Massachusetts; Austin, Texas; and Portland, Oregon

Why?

If you only used commas:

> Boston, Massachusetts, Austin, Texas, Portland, Oregon

the reader can't see the main boundaries.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{internal commas then semicolons between main items}}
\]

This guide calls these “super-commas.”

---

# Part 16 — QUOTATION PUNCTUATION

The important SAT rules:

### Direct quote after a lead-in

> The researcher said, “The results are significant.”

Comma before quote.

---

### “that” introduction

> The report states that “the treatment produced no effect.”

No comma after “that.”

---

### Periods and commas

In American usage, commas and periods go inside closing quotation marks.

---

### Question mark

If the quoted material itself asks:

> She asked, “Did the trial succeed?”

Question mark inside.

If whole sentence asks about a statement:

> Did she really say, “The trial succeeded”?

Question mark outside.

This guide covers these specific patterns.

---

# Part 17 — BOUNDARIES MASTER TABLE

This is the punctuation sheet I want you to memorize.

| Structure | Correct |
|---|---|
| IC + IC | `.` |
| IC + IC | `;` |
| IC + IC | `, FANBOYS` |
| DC + IC | `,` after DC |
| IC + essential DC | usually nothing |
| IC + participial phrase | comma |
| complete IC + explanation | `:` or suitable dash |
| nonessential insertion | matching `, ,` / `— —` / `( )` |
| subject + verb | nothing |
| one subject + two verbs | usually nothing |

And these are automatically suspicious:

\[
\boxed{IC,\ IC}
\]

comma splice.

\[
\boxed{IC;\ DC}
\]

semicolon followed by dependent clause.

\[
\boxed{\text{incomplete phrase: list}}
\]

colon after incomplete left side.

# Part 18 — SUBJECT–VERB AGREEMENT

Now we enter Form, Structure, and Sense.

The SAT tries to hide the subject.

Rule:

\[
\boxed{\text{Verb agrees with TRUE subject}}
\]

not nearest noun.

---

## 18.1 Basic example

> The collection of artifacts **is** on display.

Cross out:

> of artifacts

You get:

> The collection **is**.

Subject:

\[
\boxed{\text{collection = singular}}
\]

So **is**, not are.

---

## 18.2 Insertions are distractions

> A volume of English translations of French poems **has** enhanced...

True subject:

> volume

Singular.

“translations” and “poems” don't control the verb.

---

## 18.3 Indefinite pronouns

Usually singular:

- each
- either
- neither
- everyone
- everybody
- everything
- someone
- somebody
- no one
- nobody

So:

> Each of the students **has** a book.

Not:

> have.

---

## 18.4 Clearly plural indefinite pronouns

- both
- few
- many
- several

> Several **are** missing.

---

## 18.5 Some/all/most/none

These depend on the noun after “of.”

> Some of the **cake is** gone.

Cake = uncountable/singular concept.

> Some of the **cookies are** gone.

Cookies = plural.

---

## 18.6 “or / nor”

With:

> neither A nor B

verb usually agrees with closer subject.

> Neither the coach nor the players **are** ready.

Closer:

> players

Plural.

But:

> Neither the players nor the coach **is** ready.

Closer:

> coach

Singular.

---

## 18.7 “along with” trap

> The director, along with the producers, **was** present.

True subject:

> director

“along with” does NOT equal “and.”

Same for:

- as well as
- together with
- in addition to

They do not make subject plural.

---

## 18.8 “the number of” vs “a number of”

Memorize:

\[
\boxed{\text{The number of... = singular}}
\]

> The number of participants **was** small.

But:

\[
\boxed{\text{A number of... = plural}}
\]

> A number of participants **were** absent.

---

## 18.9 “There is/are”

“There” is not the real subject.

> There **are** three reasons.

Real subject:

> three reasons.

Plural.

This guide's method is exactly to strip inserted material and ask “who/what is doing the verb?”

---

# Part 19 — VERB TENSE

SAT tense questions are about **time logic**, not “past sounds better.”

---

## 19.1 Consistent timeframe

Wrong:

> The scientist examined the samples and **publishes** the results.

First verb:

\[
\text{examined = past}
\]

Second should match:

\[
\boxed{\text{published}}
\]

unless context gives a reason to change time.

---

## 19.2 Legitimate time shifts

> The excavation concluded last year, but analysis **will continue** next year.

Past event + future event.

Different tenses make sense.

---

# Part 20 — SIMPLE PAST VS PAST PERFECT

Past perfect:

\[
\boxed{\text{had + past participle}}
\]

Use it to mark the **earlier of two past events**.

Example:

> By the time the team **arrived**, the experiment **had ended**.

Timeline:

1. experiment ended
2. team arrived

Earlier past action:

\[
\boxed{\text{had ended}}
\]

Later past action:

\[
\boxed{\text{arrived}}
\]

---

# Part 21 — PRESENT PERFECT

\[
\boxed{\text{has/have + past participle}}
\]

Often indicates something beginning in the past and connected to present.

> She **has studied** this species for ten years.

Usually implies she's still doing so / the time extends to now.

Compare:

> She **studied** the species for ten years before retiring.

Finished past period.

---

# Part 22 — PROGRESSIVE AND PERFECT PROGRESSIVE

### Progressive

\[
\boxed{\text{was/were + -ing}}
\]

Action was in progress.

> The researchers **were analyzing** the samples when the power failed.

---

### Past perfect progressive

\[
\boxed{\text{had been + -ing}}
\]

Action had been ongoing leading up to a past point.

> The researchers **had been monitoring** the population for a decade when new data arrived.

Think:

\[
\boxed{\text{ongoing before a past reference point}}
\]

---

# Part 23 — SUBJUNCTIVE

Hypothetical/unreal situations may use:

\[
\boxed{\text{were}}
\]

> If the treatment **were** effective, more patients would recover.

Even with singular subject:

> if it **were**

This is formal Standard English.

---

# Part 24 — CONDITIONAL TENSE

Normal possible condition:

> If the treatment **proves** effective, doctors **will adopt** it.

Not:

> If the treatment **will prove** effective...

Usually the “if” clause takes present tense for future conditions.

Hypothetical:

> If the treatment **proved** effective, doctors **would adopt** it.

---

# Part 25 — VERB FINITENESS AGAIN

This deserves repetition because it's high yield.

Ask:

> Does this subject already have a finite verb?

Example:

> The theory ___ three predictions.

No existing verb.

Need:

\[
\boxed{\text{makes}}
\]

Not:

> making

> to make

---

But:

> The theory, ___ in 1985, remains influential.

Finite verb already:

> remains

Therefore blank should be a participle:

\[
\boxed{\text{proposed}}
\]

not:

> was proposed

---

# Part 26 — PRONOUN–ANTECEDENT AGREEMENT

A pronoun replaces a noun.

That noun is its **antecedent**.

You check:

1. number
2. person
3. clarity

---

## 26.1 Number

> The students submitted **their** papers.

Plural then plural.

---

## 26.2 Singular “they”

This guide notes modern digital SAT accepts singular **they** for unspecified gender.

> Each participant submitted **their** form.

Do not automatically mark this wrong.

---

## 26.3 Ambiguous pronoun

> When the curator spoke to the conservator, **she** raised concerns.

Who is she?

Could be curator.

Could be conservator.

Ambiguous.

SAT prefers:

> ...the curator raised concerns.

---

## 26.4 Not every nearby noun creates ambiguity

> When Dr. Moreau spoke to the committee, **she** raised concerns.

“She” reasonably refers to Dr. Moreau.

Committee is not “she.”

So this is not ambiguous merely because two nouns exist.

---

## 26.5 Person shift

Wrong:

> If a student wants to improve, **you** should practice daily.

Started:

> a student = third person

Switched:

> you = second person

Correct:

> If a student wants to improve, **they** should practice daily.

Or:

> If **you** want to improve, **you** should practice daily.

Stay consistent.

---

# Part 27 — POSSESSIVES AND CONTRACTIONS

These should become free points.

---

## 27.1 its vs it's

\[
\boxed{\text{it's = it is}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{its = belonging to it}}
\]

Test:

> The company changed ___ policy.

Try:

> changed **it is policy**

Nonsense.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{its}}
\]

---

> ___ important to review.

Try:

> **It is** important.

Works.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{It's}}
\]

---

## 27.2 whose vs who's

\[
\boxed{\text{who's = who is}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{whose = belonging to whom}}
\]

> Whose book is this?

Ownership.

---

## 27.3 your vs you're

\[
\boxed{\text{you're = you are}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{your = belonging to you}}
\]

---

## 27.4 their / there / they're

\[
\boxed{\text{their = possession}}
\]

> their findings

\[
\boxed{\text{there = place/existence}}
\]

> There are three explanations.

\[
\boxed{\text{they're = they are}}
\]

> They're conducting another trial.

---

## 27.5 Plural vs possessive

Students = multiple students.

Student's = belonging to one student.

Students' = belonging to multiple students.

Examples:

> The **students** arrived.

> The **student's** notebook.

> The **students'** notebooks.

---

## 27.6 Irregular plural possession

Children is already plural.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{children's}}
\]

not:

> childrens'

Women:

\[
\boxed{\text{women's}}
\]

---

## 27.7 Decades

Plain plural:

\[
\boxed{\text{1990s}}
\]

not:

\[
1990's
\]

unless showing actual possession, which is rare.

---

# Part 28 — MODIFIERS

This is extremely testable.

The central rule:

\[
\boxed{\text{modifier should sit next to what it describes}}
\]

---

## 28.1 Dangling modifier

Wrong:

> Walking to the excavation site, **the rain** began to fall.

Who was walking?

According to grammar:

> the rain

Absurd.

The noun after the introductory modifier should be the doer.

Correct:

> Walking to the excavation site, **the researchers** noticed the rain.

Researchers can walk.

---

## 28.2 Quick test

If sentence begins:

> Having analyzed the data,...

Immediately look at the next noun.

If:

> Having analyzed the data, **the researchers**...

Good.

If:

> Having analyzed the data, **the results**...

Bad.

Results cannot analyze data.

This is one of the fastest SAT grammar checks.

---

## 28.3 Misplaced modifier

Wrong:

> The archaeologist displayed the artifact in the museum **that was thousands of years old**.

Grammatically, the phrase sits next to “museum.”

It sounds like museum is thousands of years old.

If artifact is old:

> The archaeologist displayed **the artifact that was thousands of years old** in the museum.

---

## 28.4 Limiter words

Words like:

- only
- almost
- nearly
- just
- even

should sit next to what they limit.

Compare:

> She **almost drove** the entire route.

Potential meaning:

she nearly drove but didn't.

Versus:

> She drove **almost the entire route**.

Meaning:

she drove most of it.

Placement matters.

---

# Part 29 — PARALLEL STRUCTURE

Parallel structure means items doing the same grammatical job should use the same grammatical form.

---

## 29.1 Gerund list

Wrong:

> The course teaches reading, writing, and **to calculate**.

Forms:

\[
-ing,\ -ing,\ infinitive
\]

Mismatch.

Correct:

> reading, writing, and **calculating**

---

## 29.2 Verb list

> The museum plans to **expand** its collection, **renovate** the gallery, and **construct** a new wing.

All:

\[
\boxed{\text{bare verbs after shared “to”}}
\]

Wrong:

> expand, renovate, and **constructing**

---

## 29.3 Clauses

Wrong:

> The report stated that emissions increased, that temperatures rose, and **ice caps melting**.

Correct:

> that emissions increased, that temperatures rose, and **that ice caps melted**.

Same grammatical form.

---

## 29.4 Not only...but also

Wrong:

> The policy was not only **costly** but also **it was ineffective**.

First:

adjective.

Second:

full clause.

Mismatch.

Correct:

> not only **costly** but also **ineffective**.

Or:

> Not only **was the policy costly**, but **it was also ineffective**.

Clause + clause.

---

## 29.5 Either...or

Wrong:

> The committee will either **extend the deadline** or **they will cancel the project**.

Better parallel:

> The committee will either **extend the deadline** or **cancel the project**.

---

# Part 30 — LOGICAL COMPARISONS

This is a favorite trap.

Wrong:

> The novels of Toni Morrison are studied more frequently than **William Faulkner**.

You are comparing:

\[
\text{novels}
\]

with:

\[
\text{person}
\]

Wrong category.

Correct:

> The novels of Toni Morrison are studied more frequently than **those of William Faulkner**.

Now:

novels vs novels.

---

Another:

Wrong:

> The temperature on Mars is colder than Earth.

Temperature vs planet.

Correct:

> The temperature on Mars is colder than **that of Earth**.

Or:

> ...colder than the temperature on Earth.

# Part 31 — ADJECTIVE VS ADVERB

Adjective describes noun.

Adverb generally describes action/verb/adjective/adverb.

---

## 31.1 Action verb

> She writes **slowly**.

“slowly” describes the action “writes.”

Adverb.

---

## 31.2 Linking verb

Linking verbs connect the subject to a description.

Common:

- be
- seem
- appear
- look
- feel
- smell
- taste
- sound
- become
- remain
- stay
- prove

Example:

> The result seems **strange**.

“strange” describes **result**, not how it “seems.”

Therefore adjective.

Not:

> seems strangely

for that meaning.

---

## 31.3 Same verb can have two uses

> The scientist looked **carefully** at the sample.

“looked” = action.

Adverb.

But:

> The sample looked **strange**.

“looked” = seemed.

Linking.

Adjective.

---

## 31.4 good vs well

> She performed **well**.

Action then adverb.

> The result was **good**.

Linking verb then adjective.

---

# Part 32 — COMPARATIVES AND SUPERLATIVES

Count how many things.

---

## 32.1 Two things

Use comparative:

\[
\boxed{-er\text{ or more}}
\]

Examples:

> taller

> more efficient

---

## 32.2 Three or more

Use superlative:

\[
\boxed{-est\text{ or most}}
\]

> tallest

> most efficient

---

## 32.3 Double marking is wrong

Wrong:

> more stronger

Correct:

> stronger

Wrong:

> most fastest

Correct:

> fastest

---

## 32.4 Signal words

> **between the two**

then comparative.

> **of the three**

then superlative.

Example:

> Of the three methods, method B was the **most reliable**.

---

## 32.5 Comparison pairs

Memorize correct pairings:

\[
\boxed{\text{as...as}}
\]

not:

> as...than

\[
\boxed{\text{more...than}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{less...than}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{between X and Y}}
\]

not:

> between X versus Y

\[
\boxed{\text{different from}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{the same as}}
\]

# Part 33 — PRONOUN CASE

Think:

subject form vs object form.

Subject:

\[
I,\ he,\ she,\ we,\ they,\ who
\]

Object:

\[
me,\ him,\ her,\ us,\ them,\ whom
\]

---

## 33.1 Remove the other person

> Dr. Chen and ___ submitted the proposal.

Choices:

I / me.

Remove Dr. Chen:

> **I submitted**.

Correct.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{Dr. Chen and I}}
\]

---

## 33.2 Object position

> The award was given to Dr. Chen and ___.

Remove Dr. Chen:

> given to **me**

not:

> given to I.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Dr. Chen and me}}
\]

---

## 33.3 After a preposition

After:

- to
- for
- with
- between

usually object case.

> between you and **me**

not:

> between you and I.

---

## 33.4 who vs whom

Use he/him trick.

> ___ wrote the paper?

Answer:

> **He** wrote it.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Who}}
\]

---

> ___ did the committee select?

Answer:

> They selected **him**.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Whom}}
\]

Memory:

\[
\boxed{\text{he then who}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{him then whom}}
\]

---

## 33.5 whoever / whomever

Look inside its own clause.

> The award goes to ___ submits the best proposal.

Inside clause:

> ___ submits

Need subject:

\[
\boxed{\text{whoever}}
\]

Even though “to” appears earlier.

But:

> The committee will select ___ it prefers.

Inside clause:

> it prefers ___

Need object:

\[
\boxed{\text{whomever}}
\]

# Part 34 — LOGICAL CONNECTORS

Even perfect grammar can be wrong if the relationship is wrong.

---

## 34.1 Cause

- because
- since
- as
- therefore
- consequently
- so

Example:

> The sample was contaminated, **so** the researchers repeated the test.

---

## 34.2 Contrast

- but
- yet
- although
- though
- whereas
- however
- nevertheless

Example:

> **Although** the sample was small, the findings were influential.

---

## 34.3 Addition

- and
- moreover
- furthermore
- also

---

## 34.4 Choice

- or
- alternatively
- whether...or

---

## 34.5 Time

- when
- while
- after
- before
- meanwhile
- subsequently

---

## 34.6 Logic trap

Wrong:

> **Because** the study had a tiny sample, it became highly influential.

A tiny sample would normally make influence surprising, not causal.

Better:

> **Although** the study had a tiny sample, it became highly influential.

SAT wants you to understand meaning, not just punctuation.

Low frequency, but easy.

Countable:

Uncountable:

# 36. NUMBER VS AMOUNT

Countable:

\[
\boxed{\text{number}}
\]

> a large number of students

Uncountable:

\[
\boxed{\text{amount}}
\]

# Part 37 — RISE VS RAISE

Rise = go upward itself.

> Temperatures **rise**.

Raise = somebody increases something.

> Scientists **raised** the temperature.

---

# Part 38 — LIE VS LAY

Lie = recline.

> The book lies on the table.

Lay = put something somewhere.

> She laid the book on the table.

This is rare, but useful.

# Part 39 — DOUBLE NEGATIVES

Wrong:

> The researchers didn't find **no** evidence.

Two negatives.

Correct:

> didn't find **any** evidence.

Or:

> found **no** evidence.

One negative idea.

\]

Read right side alone.

Mark:

\[
IC\text{ or not}

\[

legal:

\[
.

;

# Part 44 — THE BIGGEST SAT TRAPS

These are the ones I would expect you to catch almost instantly after practice.

1. **Comma splice**

\[
IC,\ IC
\]

wrong.

2. **However trap**

\[
IC,\ however,\ IC
\]

wrong.

3. **Subject–verb separation**

> The study of hundreds of species, shows...

wrong comma.

4. **Compound predicate**

> The researcher collected samples, and analyzed them.

wrong comma if same subject.

5. **Colon after incomplete phrase**

> includes: X, Y, Z

often wrong if “includes” is still waiting for object.

6. **Nearest-noun agreement**

> The collection of paintings are...

wrong.

7. **Verb finiteness**

> The scientist writing the report.

fragment if no other finite verb.

8. **Dangling modifier**

> Having reviewed the study, the results...

results didn't review.

9. **Wrong comparison**

> climate of Mars vs Earth

climate vs planet.

10. **Its/it's**

always expand.

11. **More vs most**

2 vs 3+.

12. **I/me hypercorrection**

> between you and I

wrong.

# Part 46 — MY TEST-DAY CHECKLIST FOR YOU

Before submitting any A4 answer, run the relevant check:

1. **Punctuation choices?**
   - Identify IC/DC first.

2. **Two ICs?**
   - period / semicolon / comma + FANBOYS.

3. **Comma alone between ICs?**
   - wrong.

4. **However/therefore?**
   - needs strong boundary before it.

5. **Colon?**
   - left side must stand alone.

6. **Inserted information?**
   - essential = no commas.
   - nonessential = matching pair.

7. **Blank between subject and verb?**
   - probably nothing.

8. **Same subject doing two verbs?**
   - don't insert random comma.

9. **Verb form choices?**
   - does subject already have finite verb?

10. **is/are or has/have?**
    - find true subject; ignore inserted nouns.

11. **Tense choices?**
    - build timeline.

12. **Two past actions?**
    - earlier one may need past perfect.

13. **Pronoun?**
    - clear antecedent, correct number/person.

14. **its/it's etc.?**
    - expand contraction.

15. **Opening modifier?**
    - first noun after comma must logically perform/deserve modifier.

16. **List/comparison?**
    - parallel grammatical form.

17. **Adjective/adverb?**
    - linking verb then adjective.

18. **more/most?**
    - count entities.

19. **I/me/who/whom?**
    - isolate and test.

20. **Connector?**
    - verify actual logic: cause, contrast, addition, time, choice.

If these reactions become automatic, Standard English Conventions becomes one of the **most rule-based and controllable parts of the SAT**. You no longer need to rely on “English instinct”; almost every question reduces to a small structural test.
