# Part 0: WHAT DATA ANALYSIS IS REALLY TESTING

B3 accounts for roughly 5–7 of the 44 Math questions. It looks like many unrelated subjects, but the SAT keeps repeating the same seven jobs:

| Question type | What you need to recognize |
|---|---|
| Ratios/rates/units | “What quantities are being compared?” |
| Percentages | “What is the original/base amount?” |
| Statistics | “Center or spread?” |
| Scatterplots/models | “What does the relationship/model mean?” |
| Probability | “What is my denominator?” |
| Inference | “What can this sample tell me?” |
| Study design | “Can I generalize? Can I claim causation?” |

High-frequency skills include percent change, unit conversions, mean/median/spread, conditional probability, margin of error, line-of-best-fit interpretation, and distinguishing random sampling from random assignment.

# Part 1: RATIOS, RATES, PROPORTIONS, AND UNITS

## 1.1 Ratio

A ratio compares two quantities.

If there are:

\[
12\text{ boys},\quad 8\text{ girls},
\]

then:

\[
\text{boys:girls}=12:8=3:2.
\]

This is a **part-to-part ratio**.

But if the question asks:

> What fraction of the students are boys?

Total students:

\[
12+8=20.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac{12}{20}=\frac35.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{boys:girls}=3:2}
\]

but:

\[
\boxed{\text{boys as fraction of whole}=\frac35}.
\]

This distinction is extremely important.

---

## 1.2 Ratio “parts” method

Suppose:

\[
\text{red:blue}=3:5
\]

and there are 64 objects total.

Total ratio parts:

\[
3+5=8.
\]

Each part represents:

\[
64\div8=8.
\]

Red:

\[
3(8)=24.
\]

Blue:

\[
5(8)=40.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{24\text{ red},40\text{ blue}}.
\]

### SAT shortcut

When given a ratio:

\[
a:b
\]

and a total \(T\):

\[
\boxed{\text{one part}=\frac{T}{a+b}}
\]

then multiply by whichever part you need.

---

## 1.3 Proportions

If:

\[
\frac ab=\frac cd,
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{ad=bc}.
\]

Example:

3 pounds cost \$5.40.

7 pounds cost \(x\).

Set corresponding units together:

\[
\frac{3\text{ lb}}{5.40\text{ dollars}}
=
\frac{7\text{ lb}}{x\text{ dollars}}.
\]

Cross multiply:

\[
3x=37.8.
\]

\[
\boxed{x=12.60}.
\]

### Important

Keep the same type of quantity in the same location.

Do not write:

\[
\frac{3}{5.4}=\frac{x}{7}.
\]

That mixes pounds with dollars.

---

## 1.4 Unit rate

A unit rate means “per 1.”

240 miles on 8 gallons:

\[
\frac{240}{8}=30.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{30\text{ miles per gallon}}.
\]

Whenever you see:

- per hour
- per gallon
- per person
- per square mile
- per dollar

you are looking at a rate.

---

## 1.5 Direct variation

Direct variation:

\[
\boxed{y=kx}.
\]

That means the ratio:

\[
\frac yx
\]

is constant.

Example:

When:

\[
x=3,\qquad y=12.
\]

Then:

\[
k=\frac{12}{3}=4.
\]

So:

\[
y=4x.
\]

At:

\[
x=7,
\]

\[
y=28.
\]

Answer:

\[
\boxed{28}.
\]

### Recognition

If \(x\) doubles, \(y\) doubles.

If \(x\) triples, \(y\) triples.

---

## 1.6 Inverse variation

Inverse variation:

\[
\boxed{y=\frac{k}{x}}
\]

or equivalently:

\[
\boxed{xy=k}.
\]

If \(x\) increases, \(y\) decreases.

Example:

\[
x=4,\quad y=6.
\]

Then:

\[
k=4(6)=24.
\]

If:

\[
x=3,
\]

then:

\[
y=\frac{24}{3}=8.
\]

Answer:

\[
\boxed8.
\]

### Recognition

Direct:

\[
\boxed{\frac yx=\text{constant}}
\]

Inverse:

\[
\boxed{xy=\text{constant}}.
\]

---

## 1.7 Density

The basic relationship:

\[
\boxed{\text{density}=\frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}}.
\]

Rearrange:

\[
\boxed{\text{mass}=\text{density}\times\text{volume}}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{\text{volume}=\frac{\text{mass}}{\text{density}}}.
\]

The same idea applies to population density:

\[
\boxed{\text{population density}
=
\frac{\text{population}}{\text{area}}}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{population}
=
\text{density}\times\text{area}}.
\]

Your source explicitly groups density, average speed, weighted averages, and direct/inverse variation under the rate-and-unit toolkit.

---

## 1.8 Unit conversion

This is a major SAT skill.

The safest method is **dimensional analysis**.

Suppose:

\[
60\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{hr}}
\]

must become feet per second.

Know:

\[
1\text{ mi}=5280\text{ ft}
\]

and:

\[
1\text{ hr}=3600\text{ sec}.
\]

Write:

\[
60\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{hr}}
\times
\frac{5280\text{ ft}}{1\text{ mi}}
\times
\frac{1\text{ hr}}{3600\text{ sec}}.
\]

Units cancel:

\[
\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{hr}}
\times
\frac{\text{ft}}{\text{mi}}
\times
\frac{\text{hr}}{\text{sec}}.
\]

Leaving:

\[
\frac{\text{ft}}{\text{sec}}.
\]

Calculate:

\[
60\cdot\frac{5280}{3600}=88.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{88\text{ ft/s}}.
\]

This cancellation-factor method works for any unit conversion.

---

## 1.9 Square units

This is one of the biggest traps.

Because:

\[
1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in},
\]

then:

\[
1\text{ ft}^2
=
(12\text{ in})^2
=
144\text{ in}^2.
\]

Not:

\[
12\text{ in}^2.
\]

Example:

\[
24\text{ ft}^2.
\]

Convert:

\[
24(144)=3456.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{3456\text{ in}^2}.
\]

---

## 1.10 Cubic units

Same principle.

\[
1\text{ yd}=3\text{ ft}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
1\text{ yd}^3=(3\text{ ft})^3=27\text{ ft}^3.
\]

So:

\[
4\text{ yd}^3=4(27)=108\text{ ft}^3.
\]

Answer:

\[
\boxed{108\text{ ft}^3}.
\]

Remember:

\[
\boxed{\text{length conversion: }k}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{area conversion: }k^2}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{volume conversion: }k^3}.
\]

---

## 1.11 Average speed

This formula must be memorized:

\[
\boxed{
\text{average speed}
=
\frac{\text{total distance}}
{\text{total time}}
}
\]

You usually **cannot average the speeds**.

Example:

100 miles at 50 mph.

Time:

\[
\frac{100}{50}=2\text{ hr}.
\]

Then 100 miles at 25 mph.

Time:

\[
\frac{100}{25}=4\text{ hr}.
\]

Total distance:

\[
200.
\]

Total time:

\[
6.
\]

Average:

\[
\frac{200}{6}
=
33.33\text{ mph}.
\]

Not:

\[
\frac{50+25}{2}=37.5.
\]

The arithmetic average of speeds is generally wrong because different speeds can be maintained for different lengths of time.

---

## 1.12 Weighted average

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\bar x=
\frac{\sum w_ix_i}{\sum w_i}
}
\]

Example:

12 students score 80.

8 students score 90.

You cannot calculate:

\[
\frac{80+90}{2}=85
\]

because the group sizes differ.

Instead:

\[
\frac{12(80)+8(90)}{12+8}.
\]

\[
=
\frac{960+720}{20}
\]

\[
=
\frac{1680}{20}
\]

\[
\boxed{84}.
\]

# Part 2: PERCENTAGES

This is probably the single highest-yield B3 section.

## 2.1 Percent means “per 100”

\[
25\%=0.25=\frac14.
\]

To calculate \(p\%\) of \(x\):

\[
\boxed{\frac p{100}x}.
\]

Example:

18% of 250:

\[
0.18(250)=45.
\]

---

## 2.2 Percent change

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\text{percent change}
=
\frac{\text{new}-\text{old}}{\text{old}}\times100\%
}
\]

The denominator is always the **old/original value**.

Example:

80 to 92.

Change:

\[
92-80=12.
\]

Divide by original:

\[
\frac{12}{80}=0.15.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{15\%\text{ increase}}.
\]

### Wrong denominator

\[
\frac{12}{92}
\]

is not the percent increase.

---

## 2.3 Multiplier method

This is the method I want you using on the SAT.

Increase by \(p\%\):

\[
\boxed{1+\frac p{100}}
\]

Decrease by \(p\%\):

\[
\boxed{1-\frac p{100}}.
\]

Examples:

10% increase:

\[
\times1.10.
\]

23% increase:

\[
\times1.23.
\]

15% decrease:

\[
\times0.85.
\]

40% decrease:

\[
\times0.60.
\]

Your source explicitly recommends turning percent changes into multipliers and chaining the multipliers rather than adding percentages.

---

## 2.4 “Increase by” vs final percentage

Suppose \$100 increases by 30%.

Increase:

\[
30.
\]

New value:

\[
130.
\]

Multiplier:

\[
1.30.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{\text{increase by 30\%}=\text{final is 130\% of original}}.
\]

---

## 2.5 Percent increases above 100%

This causes many mistakes.

Suppose something increases by **150%**.

Original = 100%.

Increase = 150%.

Final:

\[
100\%+150\%=250\%.
\]

Multiplier:

\[
\boxed{2.5}.
\]

Therefore \$20,000 becomes:

\[
20000(2.5)=50000.
\]

A 150% increase does **not** mean multiply by 1.5.

Multiply by:

\[
\boxed{2.5}.
\]

---

## 2.6 Successive percentage changes

Suppose \$100 increases 20%, then decreases 20%.

First:

\[
100(1.20)=120.
\]

Then:

\[
120(0.80)=96.
\]

So final:

\[
\$96.
\]

That is a:

\[
\boxed{4\%\text{ decrease}}.
\]

Why don't they cancel?

Because the second 20% is calculated from **120**, not 100.

Mathematically:

\[
1.2(0.8)=0.96.
\]

---

## 2.7 Multiple discounts/markups

Suppose:

40% markup followed by 25% discount.

Multipliers:

\[
1.40
\]

and:

\[
0.75.
\]

Combined:

\[
1.40(0.75)=1.05.
\]

So final price is:

\[
105\%
\]

of original.

Net:

\[
\boxed{5\%\text{ increase}}.
\]

Not:

\[
40\%-25\%=15\%.
\]

---

## 2.8 Reverse percentage

Suppose a shirt costs \$45 **after a 25% discount**.

After 25% off, you retain:

\[
75\%=0.75.
\]

So:

\[
0.75(\text{original})=45.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\text{original}=\frac{45}{0.75}=60.
\]

\[
\boxed{\$60}.
\]

General rule:

\[
\boxed{
\text{original}
=
\frac{\text{final}}
{\text{multiplier}}
}
\]

Never “add 25% back.” The 25% discount was based on a different number.

---

## 2.9 Percentage points vs percent change

Suppose an interest rate goes:

\[
4\%\rightarrow6\%.
\]

Percentage-point increase:

\[
6-4=2.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{2\text{ percentage points}}.
\]

Relative percent increase:

\[
\frac{6-4}{4}=\frac24=0.5.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{50\%\text{ increase}}.
\]

These are not the same.

---

## 2.10 Percent of a percent

30% of 40%:

\[
0.30(0.40)=0.12.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{12\%}.
\]

---

## 2.11 Percent error

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\text{percent error}
=
\frac{|\text{estimate}-\text{actual}|}
{\text{actual}}\times100\%
}
\]

Always divide by the **actual value**.

Example:

Estimate \(45\), actual \(50\).

Error:

\[
|45-50|=5.
\]

Then:

\[
\frac5{50}=0.10.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{10\%}.
\]

---

## 2.12 Compound growth

Formula:

\[
\boxed{A=P(1+r)^n}.
\]

Suppose:

\[
P=10000,\qquad r=.03,\qquad n=4.
\]

Then:

\[
A=10000(1.03)^4.
\]

Approximately:

\[
\boxed{11255}.
\]

Do not use:

\[
10000(1+4(.03)).
\]

That is simple growth, not compound growth.

---

## 2.13 Finding a growth rate

Suppose:

\[
5000\rightarrow7000
\]

over 3 years with constant annual percentage growth.

Use:

\[
7000=5000(1+r)^3.
\]

Divide:

\[
1.4=(1+r)^3.
\]

Cube root:

\[
1+r=\sqrt[3]{1.4}.
\]

Approximately:

\[
1+r\approx1.119.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{r\approx11.9\%}.
\]

---

## 2.14 Simple interest

Formula:

\[
\boxed{I=Prt}.
\]

Total:

\[
\boxed{A=P(1+rt)}.
\]

Example:

\$1,000 at 5% simple interest for 2 years:

\[
I=1000(.05)(2)=100.
\]

Total:

\[
\boxed{1100}.
\]

Compound annually:

\[
1000(1.05)^2
=
1102.50.
\]

Different formulas.

---

# Part 3: ONE-VARIABLE STATISTICS

## 3.1 Mean

Formula:

\[
\boxed{\bar x=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}}.
\]

Example:

\[
72,85,90,65,88.
\]

Sum:

\[
400.
\]

Divide by 5:

\[
\boxed{80}.
\]

---

## 3.2 Reverse mean trick

If mean and number of observations are known:

\[
\boxed{\text{sum}=(\text{mean})(n)}.
\]

Example:

5 numbers have mean 12.

Total:

\[
5(12)=60.
\]

Four numbers are:

\[
8,10,14,16.
\]

Their sum:

\[
48.
\]

Missing number:

\[
60-48=12.
\]

\[
\boxed{12}.
\]

This comes up constantly.

---

## 3.3 Median

First:

\[
\boxed{\text{SORT THE DATA}}.
\]

Odd number of values:

middle value.

Example:

\[
12,7,9,14,3.
\]

Sort:

\[
3,7,9,12,14.
\]

Median:

\[
\boxed9.
\]

Even number:

average the two middle values.

\[
3,7,9,12.
\]

Middle:

\[
7,9.
\]

Median:

\[
\frac{7+9}{2}=8.
\]

---

## 3.4 Mode

Mode = most frequent value.

Example:

\[
2,4,4,6,7.
\]

Mode:

\[
\boxed4.
\]

You can have:

one mode,

multiple modes,

or no mode.

---

## 3.5 Range

\[
\boxed{\text{range}=\max-\min}.
\]

For:

\[
2,5,8,13,20
\]

range:

\[
20-2=18.
\]

---

## 3.6 Quartiles and IQR

\[
\boxed{\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1}.
\]

IQR measures the spread of the **middle 50%**.

Example:

\[
1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9.
\]

Median:

\[
5.
\]

Lower half:

\[
1,2,3,4.
\]

So:

\[
Q_1=\frac{2+3}{2}=2.5.
\]

Upper half:

\[
6,7,8,9.
\]

\[
Q_3=\frac{7+8}{2}=7.5.
\]

Thus:

\[
\text{IQR}=7.5-2.5=5.
\]

\[
\boxed5.
\]

The file's procedure is: sort, find median, split the list, find \(Q_1,Q_3\), subtract.

---

## 3.7 Outlier rule

Potential lower outlier:

\[
\boxed{Q_1-1.5(\text{IQR})}
\]

Potential upper outlier:

\[
\boxed{Q_3+1.5(\text{IQR})}.
\]

Anything outside those fences is an outlier under the standard rule.

Example:

\[
Q_1=20,\qquad Q_3=40.
\]

IQR:

\[
20.
\]

Upper fence:

\[
40+1.5(20)
=
70.
\]

A value of 100 is above 70.

Therefore it is an outlier.

---

## 3.8 Mean vs median and outliers

This is crucial.

The **mean is sensitive** to extreme values.

The **median is resistant**.

Example:

\[
2,3,4,5,6.
\]

Mean:

\[
4.
\]

Median:

\[
4.
\]

Replace 6 with 100:

\[
2,3,4,5,100.
\]

Median stays:

\[
4.
\]

Mean becomes:

\[
\frac{114}{5}=22.8.
\]

Huge difference.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{outliers pull the mean}}
\]

but:

\[
\boxed{\text{median changes little or not at all}}.
\]

Your source emphasizes this exact distinction.

---

## 3.9 Distribution shape

## Symmetric

Approximately mirrored.

Usually:

\[
\boxed{\text{mean}\approx\text{median}}.
\]

---

## Right-skewed

Long tail to the right.

Large values pull the mean right.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{mean}>\text{median}}.
\]

Think income.

A few extremely rich people pull the average upward.

---

## Left-skewed

Long tail to the left.

Small values pull mean left.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{mean}<\text{median}}.
\]

---

## Bimodal

Two peaks.

The mean can lie in a region where very few observations exist.

So the mean may not represent a “typical” observation well.

---

## 3.10 Standard deviation

You generally do **not** calculate SD manually on SAT Math.

Understand the concept:

\[
\boxed{\text{SD measures spread around the mean}}.
\]

Small SD:

values clustered tightly.

Large SD:

values farther away.

Example:

A:

\[
95,100,105.
\]

B:

\[
50,100,150.
\]

Both means:

\[
100.
\]

But B has much larger SD because its values are much farther from 100.

---

## 3.11 Same range does NOT imply same SD

Dataset A may have all values packed near center except extremes.

Dataset B may have many observations spread throughout the range.

Same min/max:

same range.

But:

different SD.

Range uses only **two observations**.

SD uses every observation.

---

## 3.12 Transforming a data set

This is very testable.

Suppose you add \(c\) to **every value**.

Mean:

\[
+c.
\]

Median:

\[
+c.
\]

But differences between values do not change.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{SD unchanged}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{range unchanged}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{IQR unchanged}}.
\]

Example:

Data:

\[
10,15,20.
\]

Add 100:

\[
110,115,120.
\]

The center moves by 100.

Spread remains identical.

---

## 3.13 Multiply every value by \(c\)

Then all distances also multiply.

So:

mean multiplied by \(c\),

median multiplied by \(c\),

range multiplied by \(|c|\),

IQR multiplied by \(|c|\),

SD multiplied by \(|c|\).

Your notes summarize these transformation rules directly.

---

## 3.14 Weighted mean from frequency table

Suppose:

| Score | Frequency |
|---|---:|
| 70 | 3 |
| 80 | 5 |
| 90 | 2 |

Total people:

\[
3+5+2=10.
\]

Weighted total:

\[
3(70)+5(80)+2(90)
\]

\[
=210+400+180
\]

\[
=790.
\]

Mean:

\[
\frac{790}{10}
=
\boxed{79}.
\]

Do not calculate:

\[
\frac{70+80+90}{3}.
\]

---

## 3.15 Histograms

A histogram represents **numerical data grouped into intervals**.

Important:

bars usually touch.

Bar chart:

categorical groups.

Bars can be separated.

### Finding median interval

Suppose frequencies:

| Interval | Frequency |
|---|---:|
| 0–10 | 4 |
| 10–20 | 8 |
| 20–30 | 12 |
| 30–40 | 6 |

Total:

\[
30.
\]

Middle observations are 15th and 16th.

Cumulative:

after first bin = 4.

after second = 12.

after third = 24.

Therefore observations 15 and 16 fall in:

\[
\boxed{20-30}.
\]

---

## 3.16 Box plots

A box plot gives you:

\[
\boxed{\min,Q_1,\text{median},Q_3,\max}
\]

or whiskers to the non-outlier extrema, depending on convention.

The box represents:

\[
Q_1\rightarrow Q_3.
\]

That is the middle:

\[
\boxed{50\%}
\]

of the data.

Use:

median to compare center.

IQR to compare spread.

---

# Part 4: TWO-VARIABLE DATA

## 4.1 Scatterplots

Each point represents:

\[
(x,y).
\]

When inspecting a scatterplot, think:

**direction, form, strength.**

Direction:

positive,

negative,

none.

Form:

linear,

curved/nonlinear.

Strength:

tight or scattered.

---

## 4.2 Positive correlation

As \(x\) increases:

\[
y\text{ tends to increase}.
\]

Example:

study time and test score.

---

## 4.3 Negative correlation

As \(x\) increases:

\[
y\text{ tends to decrease}.
\]

Example:

speed for a fixed-distance trip and travel time.

---

## 4.4 Correlation coefficient \(r\)

\[
\boxed{-1\le r\le1}.
\]

Closer to:

\[
1
\]

means stronger positive **linear** association.

Closer to:

\[
-1
\]

means stronger negative linear association.

Closer to:

\[
0
\]

means weak/no linear association.

Important:

\[
r=0
\]

doesn't necessarily mean no relationship whatsoever.

There could be a strong nonlinear pattern.

---

## 4.5 Line of best fit

General form:

\[
\boxed{y=mx+b}.
\]

Slope:

\[
\boxed{m=\text{predicted change in }y\text{ for each +1 in }x}.
\]

Intercept:

\[
\boxed{b=\text{predicted }y\text{ when }x=0}.
\]

Your notes define slope and intercept this way and emphasize reading predicted values from the fitted line, not individual points.

---

## 4.6 Interpreting slope in context

Suppose:

\[
y=2.5x+10
\]

where:

\(x\) = hours studied

\(y\) = predicted exam score.

Then \(2.5\) means:

\[
\boxed{\text{for every additional hour studied, predicted score rises by 2.5 points}}.
\]

Not:

> 2.5 extra hours for every point.

Always track which variable is which.

---

## 4.7 Intercept in context

Suppose:

\[
H=3t+50,
\]

where \(H\) is plant height and \(t\) is days since planting.

At:

\[
t=0,
\]

\[
H=50.
\]

Interpretation:

\[
\boxed{\text{predicted plant height at planting was 50 cm}}.
\]

SAT questions want contextual interpretation, not merely:

> \(y=50\) when \(x=0\).

---

## 4.8 Prediction using line of best fit

Suppose:

\[
y=.8x+3.
\]

At:

\[
x=10,
\]

\[
y=.8(10)+3=11.
\]

If an actual data point at \(x=10\) happens to be \(12\), the **model prediction** is still:

\[
\boxed{11}.
\]

If question says:

> based on the line of best fit

use the line.

---

## 4.9 Residual

Memorize:

\[
\boxed{\text{residual}=\text{actual}-\text{predicted}}.
\]

Think:

\[
\boxed{A-P}.
\]

Example:

actual:

\[
15.
\]

Predicted:

\[
13.
\]

Residual:

\[
15-13=2.
\]

Positive residual.

The actual point is above the line.

Therefore the model **underestimated**.

---

## 4.10 Residual sign interpretation

\[
\boxed{\text{positive residual}
\Rightarrow
\text{actual}>\text{predicted}}
\]

so model underestimates.

\[
\boxed{\text{negative residual}
\Rightarrow
\text{actual}<\text{predicted}}
\]

so model overestimates.

---

## 4.11 Residual plots

A good linear model produces residuals randomly scattered around:

\[
y=0.
\]

No obvious pattern.

If the residual plot is U-shaped:

the linear model missed systematic curvature.

Likely use quadratic/nonlinear model.

Your file specifically flags curvature in the residual plot as evidence that a linear model is inappropriate.

---

## 4.12 Linear vs exponential

This is a classic.

### Linear

Same amount added each time.

Constant **difference**.

Example:

\[
100,115,130,145.
\]

Differences:

\[
15,15,15.
\]

Linear.

---

### Exponential

Same factor multiplied each time.

Constant **ratio**.

Example:

\[
3,6,12,24.
\]

Ratios:

\[
2,2,2.
\]

Exponential.

So remember:

\[
\boxed{\text{constant differences}\Rightarrow\text{linear}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{constant ratios}\Rightarrow\text{exponential}}.
\]

The source explicitly uses this as the comparison test.

---

## 4.13 Choosing a model

| Pattern | Likely model |
|---|---|
| Constant rate/change | Linear |
| One max/min | Quadratic |
| Constant percent/factor | Exponential |
| Rapid rise then leveling | Logarithmic-type |

SAT clue words:

“increases by 5 each year” to linear.

“increases by 5% each year” to exponential.

“doubles” to exponential.

“reaches a maximum” to quadratic.

---

## 4.14 Interpolation

Interpolation means prediction **inside** observed data range.

If data ranges:

\[
10\le x\le50,
\]

a prediction at:

\[
x=35
\]

is interpolation.

Usually more reliable.

---

## 4.15 Extrapolation

Prediction **outside** observed range.

If data ends at \(x=50\) and you predict \(x=100\):

extrapolation.

The equation may produce a number, but the real-world relationship may stop behaving that way.

The source explicitly distinguishes interpolation from extrapolation and notes that extrapolation is less reliable.

# Part 5: PROBABILITY

## 5.1 Basic probability

\[
\boxed{
P(E)=
\frac{\text{favorable outcomes}}
{\text{total outcomes}}
}
\]

Example:

3 red, 5 blue, 2 green.

Total:

\[
10.
\]

Probability blue:

\[
\frac5{10}
=
\boxed{\frac12}.
\]

---

## 5.2 Complement

\[
\boxed{P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A)}.
\]

This becomes extremely useful for “at least one.”

---

## 5.3 “At least one”

Usually:

\[
\boxed{
P(\text{at least one})
=
1-P(\text{none})
}
\]

Example:

Roll a die twice.

Probability at least one 6.

Rather than enumerate:

find probability of **no sixes**.

Each roll:

\[
\frac56.
\]

Both:

\[
\left(\frac56\right)^2
=
\frac{25}{36}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
1-\frac{25}{36}
=
\boxed{\frac{11}{36}}.
\]

This is much safer than adding probabilities.

---

## 5.4 Addition rule

For events \(A,B\):

\[
\boxed{
P(A\cup B)
=
P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)
}
\]

Why subtract?

Because overlap was counted twice.

---

## 5.5 Mutually exclusive

Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen.

Therefore:

\[
P(A\cap B)=0.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
P(A\cup B)
=
P(A)+P(B)
}
\]

Example:

one die roll.

A = roll a 1.

B = roll a 6.

Cannot both happen on the same roll.

---

## 5.6 Independent events

Independent means event B does not change the probability of A.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P(A\cap B)
=
P(A)P(B)
}
\]

Equivalent condition:

\[
\boxed{P(A|B)=P(A)}.
\]

---

## 5.7 With replacement

Bag has:

3 red,

2 blue.

Draw red, replace it, draw again.

First red:

\[
\frac35.
\]

Since replaced, second red:

\[
\frac35.
\]

Both:

\[
\frac35\cdot\frac35
=
\boxed{\frac9{25}}.
\]

---

## 5.8 Without replacement

Same bag.

Draw two without replacement.

First red:

\[
\frac35.
\]

Now only 2 red remain out of 4 balls.

Second red:

\[
\frac24.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac35\cdot\frac24
=
\frac6{20}
=
\boxed{\frac3{10}}.
\]

Without replacement creates dependence because probabilities change.

---

## 5.9 Conditional probability

This is one of the most important SAT probability topics.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P(A|B)
=
\frac{P(A\cap B)}
{P(B)}
}
\]

But for SAT tables, there is an easier rule:

> **The denominator is the group after “given that.”**

Your source explicitly teaches this denominator rule.

---

## 5.10 Two-way table example

| | Passed | Failed | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Studied | 100 | 20 | 120 |
| Didn't study | 30 | 50 | 80 |
| Total | 130 | 70 | 200 |

Question:

> Given that a student studied, what is the probability they passed?

The condition is:

\[
\boxed{\text{studied}}.
\]

Therefore denominator:

\[
120.
\]

Studied AND passed:

\[
100.
\]

So:

\[
P(\text{passed}|\text{studied})
=
\frac{100}{120}
=
\boxed{\frac56}.
\]

Do NOT use:

\[
\frac{100}{200}.
\]

That ignores the condition.

---

## 5.11 Reverse condition trap

From the same table:

\[
P(\text{studied}|\text{passed})
=
\frac{100}{130}.
\]

This is different from:

\[
P(\text{passed}|\text{studied})
=
\frac{100}{120}.
\]

Conditional probability is not generally reversible.

\[
\boxed{P(A|B)\neq P(B|A)}
\]

in general.

---

## 5.12 Independence from a table

Using that table:

\[
P(\text{passed})
=
\frac{130}{200}=0.65.
\]

But:

\[
P(\text{passed}|\text{studied})
=
\frac{100}{120}
\approx0.833.
\]

Since:

\[
0.833\neq0.65,
\]

studying and passing are **not independent**.

---

## 5.13 Reverse conditional probability

Suppose:

80% of the 120 students who studied passed.

Number who passed and studied:

\[
0.80(120)=96.
\]

\[
\boxed{96}.
\]

This is also called the reverse-probability form.

---

## 5.14 Geometric probability

When a point is uniformly random over an area:

\[
\boxed{
P(E)
=
\frac{\text{favorable area}}
{\text{total area}}
}
\]

Example:

square side:

\[
20.
\]

Circle radius:

\[
5.
\]

Square area:

\[
400.
\]

Circle:

\[
25\pi.
\]

Probability:

\[
\frac{25\pi}{400}
=
\boxed{\frac{\pi}{16}}.
\]

Do not compare lengths.

Use **areas**.

---

# Part 6: STATISTICAL INFERENCE

This section is less computational and more about exact wording.

## 6.1 Population vs sample

Population:

\[
\boxed{\text{entire group we care about}}.
\]

Sample:

\[
\boxed{\text{subset actually studied}}.
\]

Example:

A school has 1,500 students.

Researchers survey 200.

Population:

\[
1500.
\]

Sample:

\[
200.
\]

---

## 6.2 Parameter vs statistic

A **parameter** describes a population.

A **statistic** describes a sample.

Example:

42% of surveyed voters support something.

That:

\[
42\%
\]

is a sample statistic.

We use it to estimate the true population parameter.

The source frames this exactly as “sample statistic to estimate of population parameter.”

---

## 6.3 Estimating a population total

Suppose:

60 of 200 sampled students own cars.

Sample proportion:

\[
\frac{60}{200}=0.30.
\]

School has 1,500 students.

Estimate:

\[
0.30(1500)=450.
\]

So approximately:

\[
\boxed{450}.
\]

Notice the word:

**approximately**.

Sampling produces estimates.

---

## 6.4 Margin of error

Suppose a poll says:

\[
42\%
\]

with margin of error:

\[
\pm4\%.
\]

Then plausible population range:

\[
42-4=38
\]

to:

\[
42+4=46.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{38\%-46\%}.
\]

General:

\[
\boxed{
\text{estimate}\pm\text{MoE}
}
\]

---

## 6.5 What margin of error DOES NOT mean

If the sample found exactly 42%, you should not say:

> Between 38% and 46% of the surveyed people supported it.

The surveyed proportion is already known.

The uncertainty concerns the **population parameter**.

Correct idea:

> The population proportion is plausibly between 38% and 46%.

The notes explicitly warn that the interval concerns the parameter, not individual observations or the already-observed sample.

---

## 6.6 Sample size and margin of error

Holding other things comparable:

larger sample:

\[
\boxed{\text{smaller margin of error}}
\]

which means:

\[
\boxed{\text{more precision}}.
\]

Smaller sample:

larger margin of error.

But:

## HUGE distinction

A very large **biased sample is still biased**.

Sampling 1 million people from the wrong group does not magically make the result representative.

---

## 6.7 Confidence intervals

Suppose:

\[
95\%\text{ CI}=(122,138)
\]

for a population mean.

SAT interpretation:

\[
\boxed{\text{the true population mean is plausibly between 122 and 138}}.
\]

Do NOT say:

> 95% of people have values between 122 and 138.

The interval concerns the **mean/parameter**, not individual observations.

---

## 6.8 What “95% confidence” means

Conceptually:

If the same sampling procedure were repeated many times, about 95% of intervals constructed this way would capture the true population parameter.

For SAT purposes, the most important thing is:

\[
\boxed{\text{confidence interval is about population parameter}}
\]

not:

individual values.

---

## 6.9 Random sample

Random selection allows you to **generalize**.

Suppose you randomly sample registered voters in Ohio.

You can generalize to:

\[
\boxed{\text{registered voters in Ohio}}.
\]

Not automatically:

all Americans.

Not automatically:

all Ohio residents.

The population is the group from which the sample was selected.

Your file explicitly distinguishes random selection as enabling generalization.

---

## 6.10 Convenience sample

Example:

Want to learn how often Americans exercise.

Survey people entering a gym.

That is biased.

Why?

People at a gym are likely more exercise-oriented than average Americans.

Even if you survey 10,000 gym members, it is still not representative.

---

## 6.11 Voluntary-response bias

You post:

> Vote online if you want to tell us what you think!

People with stronger opinions may be more likely to respond.

Therefore voluntary response is often biased.

---

## 6.12 Nonresponse bias

You randomly choose people, but most do not answer.

If responders differ systematically from nonresponders, your results can be biased.

---

## 6.13 Undercoverage

Part of the target population has little/no chance of appearing in the sample.

Example:

A survey conducted only by landline may underrepresent people without landlines.

---

## 6.14 Leading/question wording bias

Question:

> Do you support this sensible and effective new policy?

Words like “sensible and effective” push the respondent.

That can bias responses.

---

## 6.15 Social desirability bias

People may give answers they think are socially acceptable rather than fully truthful.

Example:

“How often do you recycle?”

Respondents may overreport.

---

# Part 7: STUDY DESIGN AND CAUSATION

This is probably the easiest conceptual area once you learn one distinction.

## 7.1 The two words you must never confuse

## Random selection

Answers:

> Who gets into the study?

Enables:

\[
\boxed{\text{generalization}}
\]

---

## Random assignment

Answers:

> Once people are in the study, who gets which treatment?

Enables:

\[
\boxed{\text{causal inference}}
\]

This is the most important study-design distinction in the domain. Your source's formula sheet summarizes it as random selection to generalize, random assignment to causation.

---

## 7.2 Four possible situations

| Random selection? | Random assignment? | What can you do? |
|---|---|---|
| Yes | Yes | Generalize + causal claim |
| Yes | No | Generalize, association only |
| No | Yes | Causal claim for studied subjects, limited generalization |
| No | No | Association only, limited generalization |

Memorize this table.

---

## 7.3 Observational study

Researchers merely observe what people already do.

Example:

Researchers compare people who drink coffee versus those who don't.

Nobody is assigned coffee.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{association only}}
\]

You cannot automatically conclude:

coffee causes the observed outcome.

---

## 7.4 Experiment

Researchers impose a treatment.

For example:

randomly assign patients to:

drug,

placebo.

If outcomes differ significantly, random assignment supports a causal interpretation.

---

## 7.5 Correlation does not establish causation

Suppose people who exercise more have lower blood pressure.

Maybe exercise lowers blood pressure.

But observationally there could be other explanations:

diet,

age,

income,

health awareness,

medication,

etc.

So observational result:

\[
\boxed{\text{exercise is associated with blood pressure}}
\]

not necessarily:

\[
\boxed{\text{exercise caused the difference}}.
\]

---

## 7.6 Confounding variable

A confounder is a third variable related to both:

the explanatory/treatment variable,

and the outcome.

Classic example:

Ice cream sales increase when drowning rates increase.

Did ice cream cause drowning?

No.

Temperature is a confounder:

hot weather to more ice cream.

hot weather to more swimming.

more swimming to potentially more drownings.

So temperature helps explain both.

---

## 7.7 Another confounding example

Students taking SAT-prep courses have higher scores.

Possible confounder:

\[
\boxed{\text{motivation}}.
\]

More motivated students may be:

more likely to enroll in prep,

and more likely to study independently and score higher.

So an observational association does not prove the course caused the entire difference.

---

## 7.8 Control group

A control group gives a baseline.

Example:

Treatment group gets new medicine.

Control group gets existing treatment or placebo.

Then compare outcomes.

---

## 7.9 Placebo

A fake/inactive treatment designed to resemble the real one.

Why?

People may improve simply because they believe they are being treated.

That is the placebo effect.

---

## 7.10 Blinding

Single-blind:

participants do not know their assigned group.

Double-blind:

participants and relevant researchers/evaluators do not know.

Purpose:

reduce expectation and measurement bias.

---

## 7.11 Blocking

Researchers first group subjects by an important characteristic:

age,

sex,

baseline severity,

etc.

Then randomize within groups.

Purpose:

balance known sources of variation.

---

# Part 12: FINAL SAT B3 DECISION TREE

This is the final checklist I would study right before the test:

1. **Ratio?** Decide part-to-part or part-to-whole.
2. **Proportion?** Keep matching units in corresponding positions.

5. **Unit conversion?** Make units cancel.
6. **Area/volume conversion?** Square/cube the factor.
7. **Average speed?** Total distance / total time.
8. **Weighted groups?** Use weighted average.
9. **Percent of?** Multiply by decimal.
10. **Percent change?** \((new-old)/old\).
11. **Increase/decrease?** Use multiplier.
12. **Several percent changes?** Multiply multipliers.
13. **Original before discount?** Divide by multiplier.
14. **Percentage points?** Subtract percentage values.
15. **Mean?** Sum/count.
16. **Missing value with mean?** Mean × count = total.
17. **Median?** Sort first.
18. **IQR?** \(Q_3-Q_1\).
19. **Outlier?** 1.5×IQR rule.
20. **Outlier present?** Mean and SD are sensitive; median/IQR resistant.

22. **Left skew?** Mean usually below median.

24. **Add constant to all data?** Center moves; spread stays.
25. **Multiply all data?** Center and spread scale.
26. **Line of best fit?** Use the line, not a nearby point.
27. **Slope?** Predicted \(y\)-change per +1 \(x\).
28. **Residual?** Actual − predicted.
29. **Residual positive?** Model underestimated.
30. **Curved residual plot?** Linear model inappropriate.
31. **Constant differences?** Linear.
32. **Constant ratios?** Exponential.

34. **Probability?** Identify favorable and total outcomes.
35. **“Given that”/“among”?** Conditional probability; change denominator.
36. **Without replacement?** Update probabilities.
37. **At least one?** Complement method.
38. **Mutually exclusive?** Intersection = 0.
39. **Independent?** \(P(A|B)=P(A)\).
40. **Area probability?** Favorable area / total area.
41. **Sample result?** It estimates a population parameter.
42. **Margin of error?** Statistic ± MoE.
43. **Larger random sample?** Usually smaller MoE.
44. **Huge but biased sample?** Still biased.

47. **Observational study?** Association only.

50. **Answer says “exactly” about population from sample?** Usually reject it.
