I sat down for my computer science exam and went blank. The logic was in my head: if this, then that, but it never turned into syntax. One question showed a broken Fibonacci function and asked us to find the mistake, but I barely understood what it was doing. How was I supposed to know what was wrong? I left the exam thinking coding wasn't for me.

Then I had an idea for a notes app and mentioned it to my brother. He opened an AI coding tool, typed a few words, and a homepage appeared with pastel cards for my subjects. I still couldn't code, but code could make something I wanted. What did you type? How is this happening? What else can it make? My brother said I could use it as long as I understood what I was building. I heard the first half.

Soon I asked for things I couldn't build myself. When a login page came back in syntax I had never seen, I pasted it in and watched it work. Not asking why was a relief, and getting something to work mattered more than knowing why it worked.

During AS Level, I carried the same habit into a research paper. The question, at least, was mine. Strong quarterly results sent stock prices jumping, but not everywhere. Why did similar news move one stock and barely touch another?

I asked AI what researchers said about investor attention, then built my paper around an argument I couldn't explain. The sentences felt like mine; the thinking wasn't. I knew that and called it finished anyway.

When I told my teacher I was done, he asked why I chose the question. That was easy; the question came long before the paper. Then he asked me to explain the paper as if he knew nothing. I couldn't. With proper market data, I started over and called him again, journals already picked out. "Let's see," he said. "It will take time. Choose your journal well."

This time, my model produced an attention threshold so exact it looked too clean. I couldn't tell whether I had found something in the market or built it into my code. I still used AI for code and analysis, but before calling anything finished, I made myself answer without it: What am I claiming, and why should anyone believe it?

For months, I cleaned data across 23 markets and reran models, half-hoping the threshold would break, because I would rather find the weakness than have a reviewer find it. I tried different windows, added variables, and changed the assumptions behind that clean answer. The number moved. Weirdly, that made me trust the result more, because a number that never moved would have worried me.

The first journal rejected the paper before review, and only then did I read what it publishes. I had researched my paper far more than where I was sending it.

At the next journal, it reached review, and the decision came as I walked down the school stairs, almost two weeks early. For a moment, I took that as a good sign. I had planned to open it at home, alone. I opened it on the stairs. Rejected, with ten comments. One was that my equations weren't even numbered. I understood the paper, but I had written it as if the reader already did. Rewriting it, I found that deciding what not to show was harder than showing everything.

A line from the Gita I grew up hearing stopped sounding like background: I have a right to my actions, but not to their fruits. I still wanted the word published next to my name, but the line gave me somewhere to put the wanting.

I keep reaching beyond what I understand. The blank page still comes, and sometimes I have nothing to write, but now I open it anyway.
