# 04 — Writing

Writing tasks feed **Production, Literacy and Writing**. Graders reward: answering the prompt, structure, vocabulary range, grammatical range and accuracy, and enough length.

---

## 1. Write About the Photo (×3, 1 minute each)

**Goal:** 2–3 complex sentences, ~40–50 words. Last 10 s = proofread. Not adaptive; the photo stays on screen.

**Official tips:** describe in detail, not just a list of objects; **no long introductions** ("This picture shows a situation that might…"); use full sentences and punctuation.

**Template**
> In the foreground, [subject] is [action], while [second detail]. Behind [them/it], [background details] can be seen. It appears to be [time/place], and the atmosphere seems [mood].

**Model answer** (photo: people at a market)
> In the foreground, a middle-aged woman is carefully selecting fresh tomatoes from a wooden stall, while a vendor in a striped apron weighs vegetables on a scale. Behind them, several shoppers carrying canvas bags are browsing other stalls. It appears to be a busy weekend morning, and the atmosphere seems lively and friendly.

**Useful language**
- Position: *in the foreground/background, on the left/right, in the center, next to, beside, surrounded by*
- Inference: *appears to be, seems to, is likely, probably, judging by*
- Precise verbs: *browsing, gazing, strolling, examining, gathering*

## 2. Interactive Writing (30 s prep + 5 min + 3 min)

**Prep (30 s):** plan your position and two reasons.
**Part 1 (5 min):** answer a prompt. Aim for **~120–150 words**.
**Part 2 (3 min):** a second question on the same topic asks about an idea you **didn't** write about before. Aim for **~80–100 words of new ideas**. Both parts are scored together.

**Part 1 template**
> I believe that [position] for two main reasons. First, [reason 1], because [explanation]. For example, [specific example]. Second, [reason 2]. This matters since [consequence]. Although some people argue that [counterpoint], I think [rebuttal]. Overall, [restate position].

**Part 2 template**
> [Direct answer to the follow-up]. One key factor is [new idea]. For instance, [example]. In addition, [second new point]. Therefore, [conclusion linking back].

**Model — Prompt: "Should schools require students to learn a musical instrument?"**
> I believe schools should encourage, but not require, students to learn an instrument. First, music offers genuine cognitive benefits, because practicing an instrument strengthens memory and concentration. For example, many students who play piano report that they focus better in mathematics. Second, however, forcing every student into lessons could create stress, particularly for those who are already balancing demanding academic schedules. Although some people argue that compulsory lessons expose children to talents they would never discover, I think optional programs with strong support achieve the same goal without resentment. Overall, a flexible approach respects students' interests while still promoting the arts.

**Follow-up: "How could schools make music more accessible to students from low-income families?"**
> Schools could make music accessible mainly by removing financial barriers. One practical solution is an instrument-lending library, funded by local businesses or alumni donations. For instance, a school could lend violins or keyboards for a full year at no cost. In addition, partnering with community orchestras would provide free mentoring from experienced musicians. Therefore, cost would no longer decide which students benefit from music education.

**Biggest mistake:** repeating Part 1 in Part 2. Bring new ideas.

## 3. Writing Sample (30 s prep + 5 min) — scored, and universities read it

Prompts ask about something that happened to you, or your opinion. Leave time at the end to check spelling, grammar and punctuation.

**Goal:** 140–180 words, short essay with 4 parts.

**Structure**
1. **Intro (1–2 sentences):** restate the question + your position.
2. **Body 1:** reason + specific example.
3. **Body 2:** reason + specific example (or counterargument + rebuttal).
4. **Conclusion (1 sentence):** summary + forward-looking thought.

**Model — "Describe a skill you want to learn and why."**
> One skill I would like to develop is public speaking, because it affects almost every area of academic and professional life.
>
> First, confident speaking is essential at university. Seminars, group projects and presentations all require students to explain complex ideas clearly. When I presented a robotics project at a school exhibition, I realized that a strong idea can be weakened by nervous delivery.
>
> Second, public speaking builds leadership. People who communicate persuasively can motivate teams and resolve disagreements. Many of the leaders I admire, from scientists to entrepreneurs, are memorable not only for their work but for how they explain it.
>
> To improve, I plan to join a debate club and record myself practicing. Ultimately, I believe that mastering this skill will help me share my ideas with far more impact.

## Writing checklist (every answer)
- [ ] Answers the exact question
- [ ] Clear structure with linkers
- [ ] At least 2 complex sentences (relative clause, conditional, concession)
- [ ] 3+ less common words used correctly
- [ ] No repeated words in a row; use synonyms
- [ ] Proofread: articles, plurals, verb tense, spelling, capital letters
- [ ] No contractions in formal answers (optional but cleaner)

## 04.01 The writing rubric, decoded

Every written task is scored on four criteria. The same four apply to the photo task, both parts of Interactive Writing, and the Writing Sample.

| Criterion | What the scorer looks for | How to show it in 60 seconds | How to show it in 5 minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Content** | Answers the exact question; relevant, developed ideas | Describe the photo's main action + background + inference | State a position, give two reasons, one example each |
| **Discourse coherence** | Logical order; clear paragraphs; effective linkers | Foreground → background → inference | Intro → reason 1 → reason 2 → conclusion |
| **Grammar** | Accuracy + range | One relative clause or participle phrase | Mix simple, compound, and complex sentences |
| **Lexis** | Precise vocabulary; correct collocations | Precise verbs (*examining, browsing*) | 3–5 less common words used naturally |

**What does *not* earn credit:** long introductions, memorized paragraphs that only loosely fit, lists of objects, repeating the prompt, or filling space with "very, very".

## 04.02 Write About the Photo — advanced templates

### Template A — people doing something

> In the foreground, [person] is [precise action], while [second person] [contrasting action]. Behind them, [background detail] suggests [place/time]. Judging by [clue], they seem to be [inference], and the mood feels [adjective].

### Template B — place or object

> The image is dominated by [main object/place], which [what it does or looks like]. To the left/right, [detail], and in the distance [detail]. The light suggests [time of day/season], giving the scene a [mood] atmosphere.

### Template C — action or event

> At the centre of the photo, [subject] is [action], apparently in the middle of [event]. Around them, [details] show [context]. It looks like [inference], and the people appear [emotion].

### Precise verb bank

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| people doing something | *crowding, gathering, queuing, browsing, strolling* |
| looking at | *examining, observing, gazing at, scanning* |
| holding | *clutching, gripping, carrying, balancing* |
| talking | *chatting, discussing, explaining, gesturing* |
| walking | *strolling, marching, hurrying, wandering* |
| working | *assembling, repairing, arranging, measuring* |

### Inference phrases

- *It appears to be…*
- *Judging by [the clothing / the light / the setting], …*
- *The scene seems to take place…*
- *They look as though they are…*
- *The atmosphere comes across as…*

### Model answer — outdoor market

> In the foreground, a woman in a light blue shirt is examining a crate of tomatoes, while the stallholder beside her wraps a bunch of herbs in brown paper. Behind them, several shoppers with canvas bags move between tables piled with fruit. The soft, low light suggests it is early morning, and the scene feels calm but busy.

**Why it scores:** precise verbs, clear spatial order, one inference, 55 words, no wasted introduction.

### Model answer — science lab

> At the centre of the image, two students in safety goggles are adjusting a glass beaker over a Bunsen burner, while a third checks a reading on a laptop. Around them, shelves of labelled bottles and a whiteboard covered in equations suggest a teaching laboratory. The scene appears to be a practical lesson, and the students look focused rather than rushed.

## 04.03 Interactive Writing — full model, Part 1 and Part 2

**Prompt:** *Should universities require every student to study a foreign language?*

### Part 1 (5 minutes, ~140 words)

> I believe universities should encourage students to study a foreign language, but should not make it compulsory for everyone. First, language study develops valuable professional skills. In a global job market, the ability to communicate with clients or colleagues in another language is a genuine advantage, particularly in business, diplomacy, and research. For example, a engineering graduate who speaks Mandarin can work directly with suppliers rather than relying on translators. Second, however, a compulsory requirement can be unfair to students whose degrees are already demanding. A medical student carrying a heavy laboratory timetable may struggle to fit in language classes, and a forced course can create resentment rather than enthusiasm. Although some people argue that mandatory language study promotes cultural understanding, I think well-funded optional courses and exchange programmes achieve the same goal without overloading students. Overall, universities should offer strong incentives, not rigid requirements.

### Part 2 follow-up (3 minutes, ~90 words)

**Question:** *How could universities make language learning more accessible to students who cannot afford a semester abroad?*

> Universities could remove the biggest barrier by offering virtual exchange programmes. Instead of travelling, students could join online conversation sessions with partner universities in other countries, which costs far less than a flight and accommodation. Another solution is to embed language practice in existing courses: a history seminar could include readings in French or Spanish, with support from a language tutor. Universities could also provide free evening classes and small grants for students who need them. In this way, cost would no longer determine who benefits from international experience.

**Why Part 2 scores:** it introduces **new** ideas (virtual exchange, embedded reading, free classes) and does not repeat Part 1's argument.

## 04.04 Writing Sample — two full models

### Model A — opinion prompt

**Prompt:** *Is it better to live in a city or in the countryside?*

> Choosing between city and countryside life depends on what a person values, but for a young adult starting a career, the city is usually the better choice.
>
> The strongest argument for the city is access. Jobs, universities, hospitals, and cultural events are concentrated in urban areas, and public transport makes them reachable without a car. For a graduate, this density creates opportunities that would be difficult to find in a small village: internships, networking events, and evening courses all happen within a few kilometres.
>
> However, the countryside offers advantages that cities cannot match. Housing is cheaper, the air is cleaner, and daily life is quieter. For families with young children or people who work remotely, these benefits can outweigh the convenience of the city. The danger is romanticising rural life without considering limited healthcare, weak public transport, and fewer job prospects.
>
> Ultimately, the best choice is not a permanent identity but a stage of life. Cities suit ambition and early careers; the countryside suits stability, space, and a slower pace. A sensible person might choose each at a different point.

**Why it scores:** balanced position, two developed body paragraphs, concession, clear conclusion, ~200 words.

### Model B — experience prompt

**Prompt:** *Describe a challenge you overcame and what you learned from it.*

> The hardest challenge I have faced was learning to manage a team project during my final year of school. I had always worked alone, and I assumed that leadership meant giving instructions. I was wrong.
>
> Our group was assigned to build a small weather station. In the first week, I divided the tasks without asking anyone what they wanted to do. Two members disengaged, and one stopped attending meetings. When I confronted them, I discovered that they felt their skills had been ignored: one was excellent at coding, and the other had experience with sensors. I had assigned both to write the report.
>
> I apologised and reorganised the work around their strengths. I also introduced a short weekly check-in so that problems surfaced before they became crises. The project was completed on time, and the station worked in the final demonstration.
>
> That experience taught me that leadership is not about having the right answer; it is about creating conditions in which other people can contribute theirs. I now ask questions before making decisions, and I listen for what people are good at rather than what is convenient for me.

**Why it scores:** specific details, clear problem–action–result structure, reflective conclusion, natural vocabulary, ~210 words.

## 04.05 Sentence bank for higher scores

### Opinions

- *I would argue that…*
- *It seems to me that…*
- *The strongest case for [X] is…*
- *The main drawback of [X] is…*
- *This argument is persuasive up to a point, but…*
- *A more convincing position is…*

### Causes and effects

- *This is largely because…*
- *One consequence of [X] is…*
- *As a result, …*
- *This can lead to…*
- *The effect is compounded by…*

### Examples

- *A clear example is…*
- *This is illustrated by…*
- *Consider the case of…*
- *In practice, this means…*

### Concessions

- *Admittedly, …*
- *Critics might argue that…*
- *While this may be true in some cases, …*
- *This objection has some force, but…*

### Conclusions

- *Overall, …*
- *On balance, …*
- *The evidence therefore suggests that…*
- *A sensible approach would be to…*

### Complex structures to use at least twice

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| Relative clause | *Students **who study abroad** gain independence.* |
| Second conditional | *If universities **made** language classes optional, students **would** choose them more willingly.* |
| Third conditional | *If I **had planned** earlier, I **would have avoided** the delay.* |
| Passive | *The policy **was introduced** in 2020.* |
| Participle clause | ***Having finished** the experiment, she wrote up the results.* |
| Concession | ***Although** the scheme is expensive, it is effective.* |
| Cleft | ***What matters most is** consistency.* |
| Inversion | ***Not only does** it save time, **but it also** reduces cost.* |

|---|---|

| 45–55 s | Add one inference |

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–30 s | Decide position + two reasons (prep time) |
| 0:30–1:00 | Write thesis sentence |
| 1:00–3:30 | Reason 1 + example; reason 2 + consequence |
| 3:30–4:15 | Concession + rebuttal |
| 4:15–4:45 | Conclusion |
| 4:45–5:00 | Proofread |

### Interactive Writing Part 2 (3 minutes)

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–20 s | Read follow-up; identify **new** angle |
| 0:20–2:20 | Two new ideas + example |
| 2:20–2:45 | Link back to the overall topic |
| 2:45–3:00 | Proofread |

### Writing Sample (5 minutes + 30 s prep)

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| Prep 30 s | Choose position/experience; list 2 points |
| 0–1:00 | Intro + thesis |

| 3:30–4:15 | Conclusion |
| 4:15–5:00 | Proofread spelling, tense, punctuation |

**Do not** spend more than 20% of the time planning. The scorer needs text, not a perfect outline.

## 04.07 The 10-point proofreading pass

Run this checklist in the last 30–60 seconds:

1. **Articles:** a/an/the before singular countable nouns.
2. **Plurals:** -s on countable plurals; no -s on uncountables (*information, advice, research*).
3. **Subject–verb agreement:** *he goes, they go*.
4. **Tense consistency:** past tense for a past experience; present for general opinions.
5. **Word form:** *dangerous* (adj) vs *danger* (noun); *economic* vs *economical*.
6. **Prepositions:** *depend on, interested in, responsible for, according to*.
7. **Sentence boundaries:** no comma splices or run-ons.
8. **Linkers:** vary them; do not start every sentence with *Firstly*.
9. **Spelling:** *government, environment, necessary, receive, separate, definitely*.
10. **Punctuation:** capital letters, full stops, apostrophes.

## 04.09 Avoiding the memorisation flag

The DET flags answers that look memorised or off-topic. You can use templates, but the **content** must fit the exact prompt.

**Safe:**

- memorised sentence frames (*"The strongest case for X is…"*),
- transition phrases,
- a fixed paragraph structure,
- vocabulary you have practised.

**Risky:**

- a full essay you wrote for a different prompt,
- statistics or examples that do not fit,
- an introduction that could apply to any topic,
- answering a related question instead of the one asked.

**Fix:** before writing, underline the **exact question words** (should / why / how / compare). Then make your first sentence answer that specific question, not the general topic.

---

## 04.10 Score killers in writing

- **No clear position.** The scorer cannot infer your argument from a description.
- **Part 2 repeats Part 1.** It must add new ideas.
- **Long, empty introduction.** "In today's modern world…" wastes 40 words.
- **Lists instead of sentences.** Photo descriptions need grammar, not nouns.
- **No examples.** Reasons without examples feel undeveloped.
- **Overusing linkers.** *Firstly, secondly, moreover, furthermore* in every sentence is mechanical.
- **Ignoring the word limit.** 60 words cannot show range; 300 words in 5 minutes usually contains errors.
- **No proofread.** Article and tense errors are the easiest marks to save.
