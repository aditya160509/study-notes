# 05 — Speaking

Speaking tasks feed **Production, Conversation and Speaking**. Graded on: content, fluency, pronunciation clarity, vocabulary, grammar, and coherence.

**Golden rules**
- Steady pace (~130–150 words/min). Fast ≠ fluent.
- Replace "um/uh/like" with a short silent pause.
- Small mistakes are fine; constant self-correction isn't.
- Look at the camera, speak clearly, keep good lighting.
- Never read prompt text aloud as your answer.

---

## 1. Speak About the Photo (20 s prep + 90 s)

**Aim:** use the full 90 seconds. Duolingo's official tip is to say as much as you can. You get one recording only; click **Record Now** to start.

**Official tips:** imagine describing it to someone who can't see it. If you don't know a word, describe what it looks like or does instead of skipping it. The photos are real, not AI-made.

**Template**
> In this picture, I can see [main subject] who is [action]. On the left/right, there is [detail]. In the background, [details]. Judging by [clue], it seems to be [time/place]. The people look [emotion], probably because [reason]. Overall, this image gives me a feeling of [mood], and it reminds me of [personal connection].

**Tip:** the personal connection at the end adds 15–20 seconds of natural speech.

---

## 2. Read, Then Speak (20 s prep + 90 s)

A written prompt (a past experience or your opinion) with bullet points. The prompt stays on screen while you speak. Aim to fill most of the 90 seconds.

**Template (story prompts)**
> I'd like to talk about [topic]. It happened [when/where]. [What happened, with 2 specific details]. The reason it stands out to me is [why/feeling]. Looking back, it taught me [lesson], and I still [connection to now].

**Template (opinion prompts)**
> In my opinion, [position]. The main reason is [reason], because [explanation]. For example, [specific example]. Another point is [reason 2]. Of course, some people think [opposite], but [response]. So overall, [restate].

**Rule:** cover **every bullet**, in order.

**Model — "Talk about a time you helped someone. Who? What did you do? How did you feel?"**
> I'd like to talk about the time I helped my younger cousin prepare for her math exam last year. She was really worried because she had failed a test on fractions. So, every evening for about two weeks, we sat together and worked through practice problems. I tried to explain things using real examples, like dividing a pizza. When she got her result, she had scored over eighty percent, and honestly, I felt even happier than when I do well myself. Looking back, it taught me that explaining something is one of the best ways to understand it, and it's actually one reason I enjoy tutoring now.

## 3. Interactive Speaking (6–8 questions, 35 s each) — new July 2025

A conversation with a Duolingo character: **3–4 questions on each of two topics**. **Each question plays once, with no replay and no prep time.** The 35 s timer starts when the character finishes; click **Record Now** to start. Questions adapt: they get harder if you're doing well.

**35-second formula (ARE)**
- **A**nswer directly (1 sentence)
- **R**eason (1 sentence)
- **E**xample (1–2 sentences)
- (optional) wrap-up

> I'd say I prefer studying in the morning. The main reason is that my mind feels much clearer before the day gets busy. For example, last week I finished a whole chemistry worksheet before breakfast, which would have taken twice as long at night. So mornings definitely work best for me.

**Tips**
- Listen to the whole question before clicking record.
- Connect to earlier answers: *"As I mentioned before…"*.
- If you don't know, give an opinion anyway: *"I haven't thought about that much, but I'd guess…"*.

## 4. Speaking Sample (30 s prep + 3 min) — scored, and universities watch it

**Aim:** 2–3 minutes of structured, relevant speech. Duolingo's official tip: speak as long as you have something to say about the question. Don't pad with filler. The question stays on screen.

**Structure**
1. Opening: answer the question in one sentence.
2. Point 1 + example.
3. Point 2 + example.
4. Closing: one-sentence summary.

**Presentation**
- Plain background, front-facing light, sit up straight.
- Dress as you would for an interview.
- Smile at the start, look at the camera, not the screen.

---

## Fillers to replace

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| um, uh | (short pause) |
| like, like | for example / such as |
| I don't know | That's an interesting question. I'd say… |
| very good | excellent, remarkable, valuable |
| very bad | harmful, frustrating, serious |
| and then, and then | after that / later / eventually |

## Practice method
1. Record answers on your phone daily (3 prompts).
2. Play back: count fillers, check pace, check you answered every part.
3. Re-record once with improvements.
4. Use [10-practice-prompts.md](10-practice-prompts.md).

## 05.01 The speaking rubric, decoded

| Criterion | What earns credit | What loses credit |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | Directly answers the question; developed with reasons/examples; covers all bullets | Off-topic, memorised, one-word answers, missing a bullet |
| **Discourse coherence** | Clear order; signposting; a beginning, middle, end | Jumping between ideas; no conclusion; random "and then" |
| **Fluency** | Steady pace; natural pauses; few restarts | Long silences; repeated "um/uh/like"; abandoning sentences |
| **Grammar** | Accurate basic sentences + some complex structures | Repeated tense/article errors; fragments |
| **Lexis** | Precise, varied vocabulary; natural collocations | Basic words repeated; wrong word forms |
| **Pronunciation** | Clear, intelligible; correct word stress | Mumbling; unclear endings; flat intonation |

**Key insight:** you do **not** need a native accent. You need to be **understandable at first listening** and **fluent enough to keep going**.

---

## 05.02 The 90-second architecture

Use this for Speak About the Photo, Read Then Speak, and the Speaking Sample. It prevents rambling and guarantees structure.

| Segment | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–10 s | Direct answer or overall impression |
| Point 1 | 10–30 s | Main idea + detail |
| Point 2 | 30–55 s | Second idea + example |
| Point 3 / reflection | 55–75 s | Consequence, contrast, or personal connection |
| Close | 75–90 s | One-sentence summary |

**If you finish early:** add a personal example or a contrast. **If you are running out of time:** skip Point 3 and close with a summary.

## 05.03 Speak About the Photo — full model

**Photo:** a busy outdoor café.

> This picture shows a lively outdoor café on what looks like a sunny afternoon. In the foreground, a waiter in a black apron is carrying a tray of drinks between tables, while two customers at the nearest table are laughing at something on a phone. Behind them, several other tables are full, and a row of potted plants separates the café from the street. Judging by the light and the casual clothing, it seems to be a weekend in summer. The atmosphere feels relaxed and social, and it reminds me of a café near my school where friends often meet after class. Overall, the image captures a small, ordinary moment of people enjoying each other's company.

**Why it scores:**

- spatial order: foreground → behind → street
- precise verbs: *carrying, laughing, separates*
- inference: *judging by the light…*
- personal connection: adds 15 seconds naturally
- close: interprets the scene rather than listing objects

## 05.04 Read, Then Speak — full models

### Story prompt

**Prompt:** *Talk about a time you learned something difficult. What was it? How did you learn it? How did you feel?*

> I'd like to talk about learning to swim, which was genuinely difficult for me as a child. I was afraid of deep water, and even putting my face under the surface made me panic. When I was about ten, my parents signed me up for a small class at the local pool. The teacher started with breathing exercises in the shallow end and did not let us go deeper until we were comfortable. I practised twice a week for almost a year. The turning point came when I swam one full length without stopping; I was exhausted, but I felt incredibly proud. Looking back, it taught me that fear is often reduced by small, repeated exposure rather than one brave moment.

**Why it scores:** covers what, how, feeling; specific details; reflection; past tense consistent.

### Opinion prompt

**Prompt:** *Is it better to study alone or with a group?*

> In my opinion, studying alone is usually more effective for deep work, although groups are useful for specific tasks. The main reason is concentration: when I study alone, I can follow my own pace and spend extra time on the topics I find difficult. For example, when I revise mathematics, I often need twenty minutes on a single problem, which would be impossible in a group that moves on quickly. That said, group study is valuable for comparing notes and explaining ideas, because teaching someone else exposes gaps in your understanding. So overall, I prefer a mix: alone for learning, together for reviewing.

**Why it scores:** clear position, reason + example, concession, balanced conclusion.

## 05.05 Interactive Speaking — 35-second answer bank

### Structure reminder: ARE

- **A**nswer directly.
- **R**eason.
- **E**xample.
- Optional wrap-up.

### Model answers

**Q: Where do you usually study?**

> I usually study at my desk at home, mainly because it is quiet and I can control the lighting. At my desk I have everything I need — my laptop, notes, and a bottle of water — so I do not waste time looking for things. For example, last week I finished a whole practice paper there in one sitting, which rarely happens in a noisy café. So my desk is definitely my best study spot.

**Q: Has technology made people more or less social?**

> I'd say it has made people more social in some ways and less in others. The main benefit is that I can stay in touch with friends who live abroad through video calls, which would have been impossible twenty years ago. But face-to-face conversation sometimes suffers when everyone is looking at a phone. For instance, at dinner, my family now puts our phones in another room, and we talk much more. So technology helps connection, but only if we use it deliberately.

**Q: Should children have smartphones?**

> I think children should have a basic phone for safety, but not a smartphone until they are older. The main reason is that smartphones give access to social media and endless notifications, which can distract from sleep and schoolwork. For example, my younger cousin became much more anxious after getting a smartphone at ten, and his parents had to set strict limits. So I would say a simple phone in primary school, and a smartphone in the early teens with clear rules.

**Q: What technology will be important in ten years?**

> I think artificial intelligence will be the most important technology, because it is already changing how people work and learn. The main reason is that AI tools can handle routine tasks — summarising documents, writing code, analysing data — which frees people to focus on judgment and creativity. For example, my teacher now uses AI to generate practice questions, but still decides which ones are useful. So in ten years, I expect AI to be as ordinary as electricity, and the key skill will be using it well.

**Q: Would you ever take a week off from technology?**

> Yes, I would, and I think I would enjoy it after the first day. The main reason is that constant notifications make it hard to concentrate, and a break would let me read and sleep better. For example, last summer I went hiking for three days with no signal, and I felt calmer by the second day. Of course, I would tell my family where I was and keep a basic phone for emergencies. So a short break is healthy, as long as it is planned.

## 05.06 Speaking Sample — a model answer

**Prompt:** *Describe a person who has influenced you and explain how.*

> The person who has influenced me most is my grandmother. She grew up in a small village and had very little formal education, but she taught herself to read, manage a business, and support a family. What makes her influence so strong is not a single dramatic event; it is the way she approaches problems.
>
> First, she is extraordinarily calm under pressure. When our family faced a financial difficulty a few years ago, she did not panic or blame anyone. She made a list of what could be controlled, worked through it step by step, and kept everyone's spirits up. Watching her, I learned that composure is not the absence of worry; it is the decision to act anyway.
>
> Second, she values practical kindness. She never gives long speeches about generosity, but she notices when someone needs help and does something concrete — cooking a meal, making a phone call, remembering a name. That has shaped how I try to treat friends and classmates.
>
> Finally, she is curious. Despite her age, she asks about new technology and listens carefully to the answer. She does not pretend to understand, and she is not embarrassed to ask.
>
> Overall, my grandmother influenced me less through advice than through example. She showed me that calmness, kindness, and curiosity are habits rather than talents, and that has changed how I try to live.

**Why it scores:** clear structure, three developed points, specific examples, natural vocabulary, reflection. At ~250 words it runs about 2–2.5 minutes; add one more specific memory if you need to fill the full 3 minutes.

## 05.07 Fluency drills that actually work

### Drill 1 — 4/3/2

1. Speak on a topic for **4 minutes**.
2. Repeat the same content in **3 minutes**.
3. Repeat in **2 minutes**.

This forces you to compress and prioritise. Do it once a day with a familiar topic.

### Drill 2 — Shadowing

Play a 60-second clip from a TED talk or podcast. Speak **simultaneously**, copying rhythm and stress. Do not worry about meaning. This trains pace and intonation.

### Drill 3 — Pause replacement

Record yourself. Every time you say "um" or "like", replace it with a **one-second silent pause** in the next recording. Silence sounds more fluent than filler.

### Drill 4 — Sentence ladder

Take one idea and say it in four ways:

1. Simple: *I like studying at home.*
2. Reason: *I like studying at home because it is quiet.*
3. Example: *For example, I can finish a whole practice paper without interruptions.*
4. Contrast: *Although cafés are lively, they are too noisy for deep work.*

### Drill 5 — 30-second sprint

Set a 30-second timer. Answer a question with **one direct answer + one reason + one example**. Stop at 30 seconds. This builds the Interactive Speaking habit.

## 05.08 Pronunciation priorities

You do not need to fix every sound. Fix the ones that cause **misunderstanding**.

| Feature | Why it matters | Quick fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Word endings** | *walk / walked / walks* change meaning | Exaggerate final -ed/-s in practice |
| **Consonant clusters** | *asked, texts, strengths* | Slow down; do not add vowels |
| **Word stress** | *PREsent* (noun) vs *preSENT* (verb) | Learn stress with the word |
| **Sentence stress** | Stress content words, not every word | Highlight 3–4 key words per sentence |
| **Intonation** | Rising for questions, falling for statements | Record and compare |
| **Vowel length** | *ship / sheep*, *full / fool* | Use minimal-pair drills |
| **Th / v / w** | *think / sink*, *vest / west* | Practise in mirrored mouth positions |

**Minimal pairs to drill:** ship/sheep, full/fool, live/leave, bad/bed, think/sink, three/tree, west/vest, cat/cut.

## 05.09 What to do when you go blank

1. **Repeat the question in your own words.** This buys 3–5 seconds and confirms understanding.
2. **Give a direct answer even if uncertain.** "I haven't thought about that much, but I'd guess…"
3. **Use a personal example.** Everyone can talk about their own experience.
4. **Use a contrast.** "Some people think X, but in my experience…"
5. **Close with a summary.** "So overall, I'd say…"

**Never:** apologise repeatedly, say "I don't know" and stop, or switch to another language.

## 05.10 Recording review sheet

Listen to every recording and score yourself 1–5:

| Question | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Did I answer the exact question? | No | Partly | Fully |
| Did I cover every bullet? | None | Some | All |
| Was my structure clear? | Rambling | Some order | Clear beginning/middle/end |
| Did I use specific examples? | None | One vague | One or two specific |
| Was my pace steady? | Very slow/fast | Uneven | Steady |
| Did I avoid filler? | Constant | Some | Rare |
| Were my endings clear? | Mumbled | Mostly | Clear |
| Did I self-correct constantly? | Yes | Sometimes | Rarely |

**Fix one thing per recording.** Do not try to fix everything at once.

## 05.12 Score killers in speaking

- **Speaking too fast** and losing clarity.
- **Long silences** while searching for the perfect word.
- **Memorised answers** that do not fit the question.
- **Ignoring a bullet** in Read, Then Speak.
- **One-sentence answers** in Interactive Speaking.
- **Constant self-correction** (*"I went — no, I go — no, I have gone"*).
- **Reading the prompt aloud** instead of answering it.
- **Looking away from the camera** or covering your mouth.
- **Fading volume** at the end of sentences.
- **No conclusion** — the answer just stops.
