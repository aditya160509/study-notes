I sat down for my computer science exam and went blank. The logic was in my head: if this, then that. I couldn't get it onto the page. Around me, pens moved fast and pages flipped. I'd studied harder for this exam than for any other and still had nothing to write. I could explain concepts. I couldn't make the code appear. I wanted to cry. I walked out sure of one thing: I was never doing this again.

Months later, I told my brother, home from university, that I didn't know how to code. He opened a website on his laptop and typed one sentence. The blank page filled with code. Then a home page came together. He didn't make a big deal of it. I stared at the screen. Wait. I can tell it what I'm trying to make?

At first, I used it to build things I understood but couldn't code myself. They were small, real, mine. Every finished version made the next thing seem possible. Then the bridge started working too well. Soon I was using it to produce things I didn't understand at all. I stopped checking. Understanding, I told myself, could come later.

The shortcut caught up with me in eleventh grade. I used AI to start a research paper, then wrote from it without really thinking it through. The words were mine; the thinking wasn't. It came back flagged, high percentage. The flag did the asking for me, and I told my teacher the truth before he could: it was mostly AI, and I hadn't tried.

He didn't yell. He just looked at me a second too long and said he'd expected better. That was worse than yelling. If he'd asked me to explain my own paper, I would have had nothing to say. I hadn't earned the right to call it mine.

I didn't stop using the tools. I started pausing to ask: What are we building? Why does this work? What would I change? If I couldn't explain the system simply, I wasn't finished.

I kept reaching. At what point does attention become too much and harm the market? I thought that would need a complicated answer. Instead, one useful measure was almost embarrassingly simple: a thirty-day moving average of volume. I built the paper around that question and sent it to a journal whose name sounded right.

The rejection came fast, before formal review. I read it first as a verdict on the work. Then I read the sentence that mattered: "not a good match with the current scope." It was polite and generic, but it showed me what I had missed. I thought good work should speak for itself. It didn't. I had sent a paper into the wrong room and expected the room to change.

I rebuilt it for a journal that fit. This time it made it past the first desk, then came back on structure: how I had built the argument, not whether the finding held. A reviewer asked me to explain a choice. It was my teacher again: explain this. I ask what I failed to make clear enough. Then I go back to the work.

A line I heard growing up says you have a right to your actions, never to their fruits. It didn't mean much to me then. Rejection didn't make it feel better. I still wanted the result I had imagined. But the line gave me somewhere to put it.

The one thing I swore off in that exam hall is most of what I do now. I still reach past what I understand. That part of me is not going away. What changed is smaller than a victory and harder to keep: I no longer let the bridge count as the crossing. The blank page still comes. Sometimes I still have nothing to write on it. I open it anyway.
