I sat down for my computer science exam and went blank. The logic was in my head, in plain English: if this, then that. I couldn't get it onto the page. My hands shook. I kept looking at the clock while pens moved and pages flipped around me. I'd studied harder for this exam than for any other and still had nothing to write. I could explain concepts. I couldn't make the code appear. I wanted to cry. I walked out sure of one thing: I was never doing this again.

Months later, I told my brother, home from university, that I didn't know how to code. He opened Lovable on his laptop. “Write whatever you want to make,” he said. He typed. The blank page filled with code. Then a home page came together. I stared. I could tell it what I was trying to make?

At first, I used it to build things I understood but couldn't code myself. They were small, real, mine. Every finished version made the next thing seem possible. Soon I was using it to produce what I didn't understand at all. I stopped checking. Understanding, I told myself, could come later.

The shortcut caught up with me in eleventh grade. I used AI to find a starting point for a research paper, then wrote from it without really thinking it through. The words were mine; the thinking wasn't. I told my teacher before he asked. He looked at me a second too long. “I expected better,” he said. It landed harder than yelling would have. If he'd asked me to explain my own paper, I would have had nothing to say. I hadn't earned the right to call it mine.

I didn't stop using the tools. I started pausing to ask: What are we actually building? Why does this work? What would I change? If I couldn't explain the system simply, I wasn't finished.

I kept reaching. What happens to a market when everyone is looking at the same thing at once? When does attention become too much? I thought a question like that would need a complicated answer. Instead, one useful measure was almost embarrassingly simple: a thirty-day moving average of volume.

The first journal sent it back before formal review. I was frustrated. I read the decision as a verdict on the work. Then I read it again: the paper had merit but might not fit its scope. Maybe the paper wasn't bad. Maybe I'd just sent it to the wrong place.

I rebuilt the paper for a room that fit. At the second journal, it made it past the first desk, then came back with questions about how I'd structured and presented the findings. A reviewer asked me to explain a choice. It was my teacher again: explain this. Except now, most of the time, I can. Each comment finds the exact spot where my explanation stops. I ask what I left unclear, then go back to the work.

While reading the Bhagavad Gita, I found Krishna's words: you have a right to your actions, never to their fruits. It didn't mean much to me then. Rejection didn't make it feel better. I still wanted the result I had imagined. But the line gave me somewhere to put that wanting: I could own the work without believing I was owed a particular response.

The one thing I swore off in that exam hall is most of what I do now. I still reach past what I understand. That part of me is not going away. But I am learning not to fill every blank just because I can. I can leave a page empty long enough to understand what belongs on it.
