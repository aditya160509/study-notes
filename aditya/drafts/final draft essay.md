I sat down for my computer science exam and went blank. The logic was in my head: if this, then that. It never turned into syntax. Around me, pens moved and pages flipped. I'd read pseudocode until its patterns looked familiar. One question gave us a broken Fibonacci function and asked us to find the mistake. I barely understood what it was doing. How was I supposed to know what was wrong? I walked out sure of one thing: I would never write code again.

Months later, I still thought coding wasn't for me. I needed somewhere to sort my notes. When I told my brother, he opened an AI coding tool and typed a few words. A homepage appeared with pastel cards for my subjects. It worked. I still couldn't code, but code could make something I wanted. What did you type? How is this happening? What else can it make? He told me to make a project and understand it too. I heard the first half.

At first, I built things I understood but couldn't code. Then I asked for a login page and got syntax I had never seen. I pasted it in. It worked. Not asking why was a relief. Soon it stopped feeling like a shortcut and became how I finished things.

By AS Level, I took that shortcut while drafting a research paper. I asked AI what researchers had said about investor attention, then built my paper around an argument I couldn't explain. The sentences felt like mine; the thinking wasn't. I knew that and called it finished anyway. If my teacher had asked me to explain it, I would have had nothing to say.

Once I had real market data, I told my teacher I couldn't defend the first version. "Sir, this time I'll do it properly. I'll send it somewhere serious." "Okay, okay," he said. "Let's see. It will take time." I wanted him to be wrong. He wasn't.

What was still mine was the question. More investor attention usually meant more volatility. What happened when it didn't?

I kept using the tools for code and analysis. I still wanted them to answer for me. But before calling anything finished, I had to answer without them: What am I claiming? Why should anyone believe it? If I couldn't answer, I wasn't finished.

For months, I cleaned data across 23 markets and reran models, half-hoping something would break. One choice bothered me: thirty days felt too simple. I tried twenty-day and sixty-day averages and added variables. Each change blurred the signal. I returned to dividing daily trading volume by its thirty-day average to estimate investor attention. Once I could defend the paper, I sent it to a journal that seemed to fit.

The rejection arrived before review. I read it as a verdict, then read it again. One possibility caught me off guard: fit. I had spent so long asking whether it was right that I had not asked where it belonged. At the next journal, it passed the editorial screen, then came back with comments: the first figure was hard to read, the equations were unclear, and the results needed a better summary. I knew how I'd reached the results. I had assumed the reader did too. I went back to where my explanation broke. That I could fix.

A line from the Gita that I grew up hearing made sense: I had a right to my actions, but not to their fruits. Rejection hurt; I still wanted it published. But the line gave me somewhere to put the wanting.

The one thing I swore off in that exam hall is most of what I do now. I still reach beyond what I understand, and that isn't going away. The blank page still comes, and sometimes I have nothing to write. I open it anyway.
