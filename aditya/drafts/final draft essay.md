I sat down for my computer science exam and went blank. The logic was in my head: if this, then that. It never turned into syntax. Around me, pens moved and pages flipped. My hand wanted to write; nothing came down. I'd studied harder for this exam than for any other. I could explain concepts. I couldn't make the code appear. I walked out sure of one thing: I was never doing this again.

Months later, I told my brother, home from university, that I was done with coding. He opened an AI coding tool, typed a few words, and turned the laptop toward me. The blank page filled with code. A homepage took shape. Wait. I can tell it what to make?

At first, I used it to build things I understood but couldn't yet code. They were small, real, mine. Each project made the next one feel possible. Soon I was asking it to produce things I couldn't explain. I stopped checking. Understanding, I told myself, could come later.

By eleventh grade, the habit had followed me into a research paper. I asked an AI tool to trace investor attention into market volatility, then built my paper around an answer I couldn't explain. The sentences felt like mine; the thinking wasn't. I wanted the finished paper more than the work. When my teacher returned it with questions, I told him before he had to ask: I had shaped the sentences, but I couldn't defend the thinking behind them.

He didn't yell. He just looked at me a second too long and said he'd expected better. That was worse than yelling. If he'd asked me to explain my own paper, I would have had nothing to say. The paper had my name on it, but I couldn't stand behind it.

I didn't stop using the tools. I went back to the paper. I stopped letting them do what I couldn't explain. What am I claiming? Why does this work? If I can't answer, I'm not finished.

I carried that rule into rebuilding it. I wanted to know when investor attention helped a market and when it distorted one. I expected a complicated answer. Instead, the measure I used was embarrassingly simple: volume divided by its thirty-day moving average, my proxy for investor attention. Once I could explain it, I built the paper around it and sent it to a journal whose scope seemed to fit.

The rejection arrived before formal review. I read it as a verdict on the work. Then I read what the editor said: it was not a final assessment of merit; the paper was not a good match with the journal's current scope of interest. Frustratingly vague, but it showed me what I had missed. I had sent a paper into the wrong room and expected the room to change.

I rebuilt it for a journal whose scope fit the paper. It passed the initial editorial screen, then came back with feedback on presentation: the introduction was hard to follow, the equations were unclear, and the results needed a clearer summary. The comments focused on whether a reader could follow how I had found it. Now, before I rewrite, I ask where my explanation breaks.

Growing up, I heard a line from the Gita about having a right to my actions, but not to their fruits. It didn't mean much to me then. Rejection didn't make it feel better. I still wanted the result I had imagined. But the line gave me somewhere to put the wanting.

The one thing I swore off in that exam hall is most of what I do now. I still reach beyond what I understand. That part of me is not going away. But I no longer let the bridge count as the crossing. The blank page still comes. Sometimes I have nothing to write. I open it anyway.
