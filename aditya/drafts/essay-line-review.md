## Verdict
1. The essay already works: it is a real failure, shown on the page, with a family ritual (the hymn in the car) that frames it and an ending that is an action, not a moral.

3. The fixes are small: remove the two signposts, clarify two terms for a US reader, and cut one tidy aphorism. That gives 624 words with no new facts.

## Signposting recommendation
**Cut "The first warning came from my older brother" and "The second warning came from that teacher." Keep #9 ("I had been warned twice…").**

Reasoning:
- None of the strongest pieces in the lab label their sections. Phoebe Hughes moves on "One Christmas" and "This summer". Ruoyu moves on "the next summer". Tima's essay uses file names. When they parallel two moments, they do it by repeating a phrase ("made brave by love"), and here "I heard 'use it.'" and "I heard 'take my time'" already do that.
- Two topic sentences with the same structure, one after the other, are exactly the over-parallel scaffolding the brief warns about. They make the essay read as outlined rather than lived.
- #9 is different. It is a thought Aditya has that night, it tells the reader to watch for a pattern, and it is said only once. Cutting it too would make the two flashbacks feel unmotivated after the hymn. The two "I heard" lines then reward the reader for noticing the pattern, without being told "this is the second one".
- Clarity doesn't suffer: the brother is named in #12 and the teacher in #18 ("My research teacher").

## Top 5 changes (priority order)
1. Delete #10 and #19, the "first warning" and "second warning" signposts.
2. #12: "told my older brother I would never study computer science like him" (fixes "his subject" and brings the brother in).
3. #16 "In AS Level" becomes "In eleventh grade", and #18 "My teacher" becomes "My research teacher". Both are clarity fixes for a US reader.
4. #35: cut "Cutting weeks of work was harder than producing it, because"; keep "Each one had felt like proof that I was serious."
5. Split #22 and #8 so the definition (threshold) and the gloss (mūḍhamate) each get their own sentence, and change #6's "which is hypocritical because" to "even though".

## Revised essay (all five changes applied; nothing else changed)

The journal's email arrived almost two weeks early, during a computer science class. Veer grabbed my phone and read the screen out loud in his announcer voice: "Decision on your manuscript." Nobody sees my results before I do, so I took it back, meaning to open it at home, alone. I opened it on the stairs anyway, friends leaning in. Rejected, with ten reviewer comments.

My friends told me it was fine, and I hated hearing it, even though I say exactly that to everyone else. It sounded like pity for a mistake that was mine. At home, I put on Bhaja Govindam, a Sanskrit hymn that keeps calling the listener mūḍhamate, foolish mind. Listening to it is the calmest part of my day. That night I didn't argue with it, because I had been warned twice, and both times I had listened only to the half I liked.

In my tenth-grade board exam, I knew the logic but couldn't write a line of code. I cried, barely spoke for three days, and told my older brother I would never study computer science like him. That summer, he pasted our syllabus into an AI tool, and a notes site built itself on the screen. He said I could use it as long as I understood what it built. I heard "use it." In eleventh grade, I wanted to know why two companies could report strong earnings while only one stock jumped. I asked AI to explain what researchers had found and drafted a paper around those explanations. My research teacher asked me to explain it as if he knew nothing, and I couldn't.

I started over with data from 21 markets and told him I was ready to send it to a journal. He told me to take my time, and to learn first what a journal wants: a clear question, what it must show, and what it can leave out. I heard "take my time," and spent it all on the model. It found a threshold, a level of trading at which a market starts to swing. I was proud of it.

The first journal rejected the paper without review, and only then did I read what it publishes. The second sent it to reviewers, and I was happier than I let anyone see. Its decision was the email on the stairs. One reviewer found the idea "interesting," my introduction "really difficult to follow," and my equations "really confusing for the audience." That stung, because out loud I could explain it. I had walked my friends through it with Nvidia: if everyone watches the same stock, when does attention make its price swing? They got it. On the page, I had written for someone who already understood it.

I reread the paper the way a stranger would, one claim at a time, and cut whatever I couldn't defend. I had crammed in too many questions, and I kept one. For days I looked for reasons to keep the results that answered the others. Each one had felt like proof that I was serious. Just before midnight, I saved those results in a separate file, scrolled through them once more, and deleted them from the paper.

Whenever we get in the car, my father plays the same hymn and explains it word by word, including what mūḍhamate means, in case I've forgotten I'm being called a fool. I mumble along, smiling, because he loves explaining, and he never assumes I remember.

I rewrote the paper for a reader who knows nothing and sent it to the same journal again. I still reload the portal more often than I would admit. And when my father explains mūḍhamate again in the car, I let him finish.
