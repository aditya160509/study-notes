## MIT

### 1. Field of study

**Prompt:** What field of study appeals to you most right now? Reflect on what led you to this interest. Approx. 100–200 words.

Computer Science appeals to me because it lets me test explanations rather than leave them as stories.

I first saw that difference while studying Fantasy Premier League. Millions of managers could see almost the same information, yet ownership still clustered around choices the data did not support. Economics gave me ways to think about incentives, imitation, and risk. Code let me measure those choices across gameweeks and build a simulated market where different kinds of traders could interact.

That movement between an observed pattern and a system I can disturb is what keeps me in Computer Science. I can change an agent's behavior, rerun the market, and watch an explanation survive or collapse. I want stronger foundations in algorithms, probability, machine learning, and systems so I can study how models and people change one another—and build decision tools whose answers remain open to challenge.

### 2. Educational journey

**Prompt:** In what ways have you done something different than expected in your educational journey? Approx. 100–200 words.

“Neural Tangent Kernel” sounded like a warning that I had arrived several years too early. I was an A-Level student; the papers I found assumed mathematics I had never formally studied.

Then I listed what the derivation actually used. Matrices. Eigenvalues. Taylor series. Differential equations. I had met each piece in Further Mathematics, just never in the same room. I rebuilt the argument around what I knew, filling gaps as they appeared, and finished with a two-neuron network calculated line by line. If I could not explain what a symbol was doing, it did not stay on the page.

The resulting exposition became a curriculum I had made for myself. It also changed how I see a syllabus: less as a border around what I am ready to learn and more as a set of tools I can carry into questions that lie beyond it.

### 3. Problems and community impact

**Prompt:** How have your experiences influenced the problems you want to tackle with an MIT education and the impact you aim to make on your community? Approx. 100–200 words.

An incident-response model can sound certain while inventing the number that matters most. While building Glassbox, I made impact figures come directly from monitoring data and reconstructed timelines from stored events. The model could interpret evidence; it could not manufacture it.

I recognized the same standard in a very different place. While teaching basic mathematics and English, I could give a correct explanation and still watch it fail. It worked only after I turned lessons into team games that gave the children a reason to use what they had learned.

Both experiences made me interested in systems whose reasoning remains visible to the people depending on them. At MIT, I want to study reliable AI and decision-making systems, then test them with people outside the team that built them. A system should not earn trust because its answer sounds polished. It should make the evidence, assumptions, and points of failure possible to inspect.

### 4. Unexpected challenge

**Prompt:** How did you manage a situation or challenge you did not expect? What did you learn? Approx. 100–200 words.

Future Lab began as a group idea and quickly became my project. I knew how I wanted the simulated market to work, so instead of dividing the build properly, I started constructing nearly every part myself. The teammate who had helped shape the idea was left without enough ownership, while the repository grew scattered because I was moving across too many pieces at once.

I had mistaken knowing how to do the work for knowing how to lead it. We stopped and separated the system into parts with clear owners and interfaces. I had to explain what I wanted before building it, then accept implementations that were not exactly how I would have written them.

The difficult part was not sharing the workload; it was giving up control early enough for someone else to shape the result. I still move too quickly toward doing things myself. Now, when I lead, I try to define the decision, hand over a real piece of it, and stay available without quietly taking it back.

### 5. What do you do just for fun? (40–50 words)

I play football or pickleball with three or four close friends. No datasets, no paper hiding inside it for once. We argue about rules, keep playing after someone says “last game,” and remember the score differently on the way home.

### 6. Someone you admire (40–50 words)

I admire my father. After my grandfather’s heart attack and death, he handled hospitals, relatives, work, rituals, and the daily care my grandmother still needs. I watched him stay steady without pretending the situation was easy. I remember thinking: I could not do all of this. He did.

### 7. A topic you could discuss for hours (40–50 words)

Why do millions of Fantasy Premier League managers, looking at nearly identical information, converge on the same wrong players? I can talk for hours about copied confidence, price changes, risk, interfaces, and the strange moment when a game begins behaving like a market.

### 8. Generalist or specialist? (40–50 words)

I am a specialist in one recurring irritation: answers that work before I understand why. I am a generalist about the tools I use against it—mathematics, code, economics, simulation, and sometimes football data. The question stays; the discipline changes.

## Stanford

### 1. Society’s most significant challenge (≤50 words)

We are making answers cheaper faster than we are making them accountable. The problem is not only whether an AI output is correct, but whether someone can trace its evidence, challenge its assumptions, and accept responsibility when it guides a real decision.

### 2. How did you spend your last two summers? (≤50 words)

In 2025, my brother came home from university; we played downstairs almost daily and ate out every weekend before AS Level began. Summer 2026 was tighter: school preparation, research revisions, new builds, football when I managed it, and the discovery that ambition can consume a holiday.

### 3. Historical moment or event (≤50 words)

I would witness the week Lehman Brothers collapsed in 2008. Charts show the fall afterward; I want to hear the decisions before they became history—what traders, regulators, and families believed was still containable, and the exact moment that belief disappeared.

### 4. Extracurricular activity (≤50 words)

Co-founding our Sports Analysis Hub mattered less than keeping it alive after the novelty faded. I initiated meetings, brought questions when discussion stalled, and learned that leadership can look like being the person who arrives prepared often enough that everyone else keeps arriving too.

### 5. Five things that are important to you (≤50 words)

My parents; the MacBook holding years of unfinished and finished work; quiet time at a temple; one idli-sambar at Dakshinayan after an hour in traffic and another hour waiting; football or pickleball with the friends who always turn “last game” into three more.

### 6. Idea or experience that makes you excited about learning (100–250 words)

On Friday nights, before the Fantasy Premier League deadline, every choice starts sounding contagious. One manager captains a player; a group chat follows; ownership rises before the match begins. I wanted to know whether we were exchanging information or merely exchanging confidence.

I began measuring the gap between the players the data favored and the players managers actually owned. The game became a record of imitation, risk, and interface design. A player’s recent score could outweigh weeks of evidence; an ownership percentage could make a decision feel safe because millions of strangers had made it too.

What excited me was that the question refused to stay inside one subject. Economics helped explain incentives. Statistics let me test whether the gap persisted. Code made hundreds of gameweeks manageable. Football kept interrupting the model because a red card, injury, or manager’s decision could undo the cleanest prediction.

I eventually built a larger study around that gap, but I still return to the game before each deadline. Knowing how herd behavior works has not made me immune to it. Sometimes I can watch myself wanting the popular choice precisely because it is popular. That is the part I enjoy most: the dataset includes me.

### 7. Note to future roommate (100–250 words)

Hi roommate,

I sleep around 10:30 and usually wake up between six and seven without much help from an alarm. This sounds disciplined until you learn that I may forget where I left the room key five minutes earlier. I like the room clean and my browser limited to whatever I am working on; twenty unrelated tabs make me nervous.

I work at my desk, often with quiet music, and my family calls frequently. You will probably know their voices before you meet them. If an important football match is on, the early bedtime becomes negotiable.

Food is easy: take mine. I am happiest with South Indian food, though I will travel an unreasonable distance and wait an unreasonable amount of time for one idli-sambar at Dakshinayan. I will join almost any football or pickleball game, especially the ones that are supposed to end after “one last round.”

One warning: I can turn a casual question technical without noticing. If you ask why something happened, you may receive variables. You are allowed to stop me. In return, I will keep the room quiet when you need it, share whatever I have, and probably be awake before you when we lose the key.

### 8. Distinctive contribution (100–250 words)

I usually begin alone: a question, a first version, and the conviction that I can save time by doing one more part myself. The work becomes useful only when another person enters it.

My STEM Racing team turned Daedalus from a large optimization system into a tool for actual car decisions. Quant Finance Club members turned Future Lab from my model of a market into a place where students could alter information and watch prices respond. They also exposed a weakness in how I work. I sometimes arrive with too much already built, which leaves other people using the system rather than shaping it.

I have started changing the order: bring the question earlier, divide ownership before the repository grows, and let another person’s method survive even when it differs from mine. At Stanford, I would contribute tools for groups to test claims together—markets, simulations, or evidence-tracing systems—but also a willingness I am still developing: showing unfinished work while there is time for other people to change it.

## Princeton

### 1. B.S.E. Computer Science (≤250 words)

I became interested in engineering when my programs stopped being private experiments. A model I understand alone is not enough if a teammate cannot inspect its trade-offs or an incident responder cannot verify its numbers.

For my STEM Racing team, I built Daedalus to compare car designs under uncertainty. The first temptation was to make the optimizer more powerful. The harder task was making every recommendation traceable: which equation produced it, which assumption moved it, and where the design stopped being reliable. In Glassbox, an incident-response system, I faced a related problem. A language model could interpret evidence, but impact figures had to come directly from monitoring data. Sounding certain was not evidence.

These projects made me want to study Computer Science as the engineering of accountable systems. Princeton’s B.S.E. foundations would deepen the algorithms and systems behind my work, while the department’s required independent work would let me carry one question through research with sustained faculty criticism rather than build alone. I am also drawn to the Center for Information Technology Policy, where technical design meets the rules and institutions surrounding its use.

I want to build systems that can make difficult decisions without hiding how they reached them. Princeton suits that goal because it would push me beyond implementation: toward stronger theory, independent inquiry, and the consequences that begin once someone relies on the answer.

### 2. Your Voice (400–500 words)

I have a habit my friends notice before I do: a casual question becomes technical around me.

Someone asks why a football player’s price changed, and I begin talking about ownership and information. A conversation about an app becomes a discussion of what the interface is making people choose. I enjoy finding the machinery underneath things. The problem is that I can keep explaining after the conversation has stopped needing an explanation.

At home and with friends, I move between Hindi and English without deciding where one ends. A thought can begin in one language, borrow the sharper phrase from the other, and finish as a mixture everyone around me understands. That ease disappears when the subject becomes technical; then I can mistake formal language for clear language.

That habit became obvious in my Quant Finance Club. During our first sessions, the other founders and I taught the Black–Scholes equation by walking through the stock price, strike price, time to expiry, volatility, and risk-free rate. We thought we were giving members the serious version. The club met at the end of a full school day; instead of curiosity, we got tired faces and a room that made finance feel like another class.

We had answered a question nobody in the room was asking.

Afterward, we changed the sessions. Rather than begin with equations, we used games. In one, information about possible choices arrived gradually, and members had to revise probabilities as each piece appeared. We used playing cards, charts, and the market simulator I had built. People who had gone quiet started making predictions and arguing about them. The mathematics did not disappear. It arrived after there was something they wanted it to explain.

I still believe in going deep, sometimes deeper than the occasion requires. What has changed is that I pay more attention to whether other people have entered the question with me. I ask what they already see, where I lost them, and whether another explanation would leave more room for their own reasoning. I am not always good at this. When I get excited, I still move too fast and say more than I need to.

My classmates at Princeton would meet someone who enjoys asking what evidence could change an opinion and who will happily carry a dining-hall argument into data, code, or a badly drawn diagram. They would also be allowed to say, “Aditya, it was just a football question.” I hope they would learn that technical ideas do not have to remain closed rooms. I would learn, repeatedly, that opening the door is different from filling the room with my voice.

### 3. Service and civic engagement (≤250 words)

I knew the mathematics I had been asked to teach. I did not know how to make a room of children want to listen to me teach it.

My first attempts were explanations: stand in front, describe the rule, ask whether everyone understood. Communication was difficult, attention disappeared, and “yes” did not mean the idea had landed. I had treated knowing the topic as proof that I could teach it.

I began dividing the children into teams and turning practice into games. To win, they had to solve the problem, explain a word, or help a teammate reach the answer. The noise level rose, but so did participation. Children who would not answer me directly argued with one another about the next move. Competition gave the lesson a purpose that my explanation had lacked.

Over repeated visits, I learned to judge the session less by what I had covered and more by what the children could do without me. The same lesson shaped my crowdfunding work, where raising money mattered only if study kits, clothing, and footwear actually reached the villages through unglamorous planning and follow-through.

Service, for me, means giving up the assumption that what I prepared is automatically what someone else needs. It requires returning, watching where the effort fails, and changing it without making the recipient responsible for my good intentions.

### 4. New skill (≤50 words)

I want to learn Tamil properly, especially enough to read and speak without retreating into English or Hindi. It is my mother tongue, but not yet fully mine in practice. I would like conversations with family to stop passing through translation.

### 5. What brings you joy? (≤50 words)

Football or pickleball with three or four close friends, after somebody has already announced the last game. The score becomes disputed, the rules become negotiable, and nobody leaves when they said they would.

### 6. Soundtrack of your life (≤50 words)

Michael Jackson’s “Man in the Mirror.” I have spent months inspecting models, papers, and explanations for what needs fixing. The song turns the inspection around. It is less comfortable when the system under review is me.

## Purdue

### 1. Opportunities at Purdue (≤250 words; verify live limit)

I can build a model alone. I cannot reproduce, alone, the pressure of designing with a community partner who must live with the result.

That is why Purdue’s EPICS program appeals to me. Its teams work on long-term problems defined with community and education organizations, which would force my interest in traceable decision systems into a setting where technical elegance is not the final judge. A partner may care more about cost, access, or whether the explanation makes sense. I need more of that friction.

I would pair that work with undergraduate research through programs such as DURI or faculty-led projects in Computer Science. My current projects have made me interested in simulation, reliable AI, and systems where human decisions affect the output. Purdue would give me collaborators who can challenge the mathematics, architecture, and evaluation separately rather than letting me serve as my own reviewer.

Outside class, Boiler Quant is a natural place to contribute my experience building market simulators and a 13-week student curriculum. I would not want to recreate my school club under a new name. I would rather enter an existing group, learn how its project teams work, and help build experiments members can question together.

Purdue’s fit is the combination: stronger foundations, research with sustained feedback, and projects answerable to people beyond the person writing the code.

### 2. Why Computer Science? (≤250 words; verify live limit)

A market result pulled me further into Computer Science: volatility sometimes fell when investor attention became most intense. Statistics could establish the pattern, but I wanted to test what kind of behavior might produce it.

I built an agent-based exchange with nine kinds of traders, then changed their rules and watched the effect appear or disappear. That experiment clarified what I want from Computer Science. It lets me move between explanation and construction: propose a mechanism, implement it, disturb it, and find the point where the explanation fails.

The same interest now appears in systems outside markets. In Glassbox, I designed an incident-response agent whose numerical claims come from monitoring data rather than the language model. In Daedalus, I made engineering recommendations traceable to equations and assumptions so teammates could inspect them. Different applications keep returning me to the same problem: how can a system make a useful decision without hiding the path behind it?

I want formal study in algorithms, probability, machine learning, and computer systems because my current work has also shown me the limits of building ahead of my foundations. Computer Science gives me the theory to understand a mechanism, the machinery to test it, and the responsibility of deciding what should happen when someone trusts the result.

## Virginia Tech

### 1. Ut Prosim: community (≤120 words)

I taught foundational mathematics and English to children in rural villages. My first explanations were correct and mostly useless. Communication was difficult, attention disappeared, and asking “understood?” produced answers I could not trust.

I began dividing the class into teams and turning practice into games. To win, children had to solve a problem, explain a word, or help a teammate. The room became louder, but students who had avoided answering me began arguing for their teams.

I learned that service is not measured by what I deliver. The other person decides whether it works. At Virginia Tech, I would bring that habit to tutoring and project teams: notice who has not entered the question, change the explanation, and try again.

### 2. Inclusion (≤120 words)

In Quant Finance Club, our early discussions assumed everyone already understood equities, derivatives, and market language. Newer members went quiet while the founders kept talking. Nobody had excluded them deliberately; we had built a conversation with an entrance exam.

We replaced equation-first sessions with probability games, playing cards, charts, and a market simulator members could alter themselves. One student who had stopped following began returning and participating once the ideas had something visible to attach to.

That experience changed what I watch for. Silence does not always mean agreement, and asking “Does everyone understand?” places the burden on the person already outside. I now try to design the entry point before asking people to join the discussion.

### 3. Leadership (≤120 words)

I am proud that our Sports Analysis Hub continued after the excitement of starting it wore off. I initiated meetings, arrived with questions when discussion slowed, and made the room reliable enough that members kept returning.

That required preparation, but also restraint. A sports discussion dies if one person treats every opinion as a presentation. I learned to bring a question, let disagreement run, and step in only when the group needed another direction.

Others relied on me less for answers than for continuity. The experience changed my idea of leadership: sometimes the useful person is simply the one who keeps showing up prepared without making the space depend on his voice.

### 4. Goal (≤120 words)

My current goal is to complete a journal resubmission while preparing for board examinations and university applications. Reviewers identified weaknesses I can repair, but the publication cycle does not care that other deadlines compete with it.

I am separating the revision into analyses, presentation changes, and a final explanation check, assigning each a fixed block rather than reopening the entire paper whenever I sit down. My research teacher challenges whether each addition is necessary; my family keeps the rest of my schedule from disappearing into it.

The harder goal is not publication by a date I cannot control. It is submitting a revision I can defend without sacrificing every other responsibility to perfect it.

## United Kingdom: shared UCAS statement

I want to study Computer Science because it allows me to turn claims about complex behaviour into systems I can test. While researching investor attention, I found that volatility sometimes fell when attention became most intense. Establishing the pattern raised a harder question: what interactions could generate it? I built an agent-based exchange with different trader archetypes and tested when the effect appeared or disappeared. That movement—from an observed result to an implemented mechanism—made me interested in algorithms, simulation, machine learning and computer systems. I extended matrices, eigenvalues, Taylor series and differential equations into a first-principles exposition of neural-network generalisation, including a two-neuron example derived line by line. Computer Science gave me the tools to implement and evaluate systems, while Economics helped me model incentives and market behaviour. Physics strengthened my attention to measurement, assumptions and the difference between a model and the system it represents. Through the Cambridge IPQ, I completed a 4,982-word investigation into whether high-frequency trading destabilises markets during stress, maintaining a research log and evaluating competing evidence. ### Question 3 — What else have you done, and why is it useful?

During a three-month internship at MalkansView, I studied company fundamentals and saw how research informs decisions under real time and information constraints. Building Daedalus for my STEM Racing team then made usability part of technical correctness: teammates needed transparent trade-offs, not simply an optimised output. I also founded a Quant Finance Club and designed a 13-week curriculum, using Future Lab, a simulated market, to let members test how information and fundamentals affect prices. Teaching mathematics and English to rural children reinforced the same lesson from another direction: an explanation has not succeeded until another person can use it. These experiences have made me more willing to expose unfinished work to users, divide ownership earlier, and treat communication as part of the system rather than something added at the end.
