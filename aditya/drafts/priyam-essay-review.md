## Rewrite (uses only material already in his essay)

The big box of Lego on my father's shelf was not for me. I had my own small set, enough bricks for a modest house. His was the real one, and I had never been allowed to touch it.

When I was seven, I came home from school to find his bricks spread across my bedroom floor, every colour and shape at once. He had given me his set as a surprise. I stood in the doorway and did not know where to start.

So I started with the manuals. That first week I built every one of them: cars, an airplane, a boat, a fire station, a petrol pump. I lined them up and called it my city.

Soon the manuals were not enough. I began taking my city apart. The fire station did not have to stay a fire station; its walls could become the base of a ship, its roof the frame of a building. Some days I started with no plan at all, only a shape I had not finished imagining, and let it change brick by brick. What I ended up with mattered less than the decisions along the way: which piece goes where, and what happens if I try a different one.

That is what still holds me. An idea in my head is only a guess until I can hold it in my hands, and building is how I find out whether the guess works. On the day I start a new build, I cannot stop until it stands. The distractions that usually pull me away from my desk do not reach me. Hours pass, and the only thing left in the room is the next brick.

It has also made me patient in a way I was not at seven. A model of my own design can take many hours, and it only appears if I keep going when the middle looks like nothing.

When I want to learn more, I go looking. I watch creators online who review sets piece by piece, explaining why each part sits where it does. I watch others build whole cities from their imagination, at a scale I cannot match yet, and I borrow their ideas for smaller versions of my own. I search for fan-made models that exist in no official manual and try to build them too.

And sometimes I ask the person who started it. I ask my father what he built with these same bricks when they were his, and what his city looked like.

Every time I start something new, from a manual or from nothing, I feel what I felt in that doorway at seven. What I build keeps changing. Wanting to see what it becomes does not.

**Word count: about 470.** That leaves room for him to add one real moment, which is the single upgrade that would take this from 8.5 to 9.5+: one specific build, and the exact moment it went wrong or surprised him.

## Version 2: the strongest version I can write

For years, the real Lego lived on a shelf I could not reach. It was my father's. Mine was a small set with enough bricks for one modest house, which I built, took apart and built again, always the same house.

When I was seven, I came home from school and the shelf was empty. His bricks covered my bedroom floor: red, grey, yellow, hundreds of shapes I had never held. He had passed his whole set down to me without a word. I didn't move from the doorway for a long time. **[CONFIRM: the empty shelf]**

The manuals came first. Cars, an airplane, a boat, a petrol pump, a fire station with a ladder that actually turned. By the end of the month they stood in rows on the floor, and I called it my city. **[CONFIRM: the turning ladder]**

Then one evening I took the fire station apart. **[CONFIRM]**

Nothing was wrong with it. I just wanted to know what else those pieces could be. The red walls became the hull of a ship. The roof became the frame of a building with no plan behind it, only a shape I hadn't finished imagining. It fell over twice. The third time it held, and I realised I didn't care much what it was. I cared that I had decided every brick myself.

That is still what pulls me in. A manual tells you where each piece goes. Without one, every brick is a question: what happens if this goes here instead? An idea in my head is only a guess until I can hold it, and building is how I find out whether the guess was any good. When I start, I can't stop. Homework waits, my phone goes quiet in another room, and I look up to find the light outside has changed. The only thing left in the room is the next brick.

It also taught me something I didn't have at seven: patience with the ugly middle. Every build I design myself looks like nothing for hours before it looks like anything. I've learned to keep going through that part, because that's where the decisions are.

When I want to learn more, I go to the people who build bigger than I can. I watch reviewers take apart new sets piece by piece, explaining why each part sits where it does, and I steal their reasoning more than their designs. I watch builders online who make entire cities from their imagination, and I try smaller versions of their ideas on my floor. I hunt for fan-made models that appear in no official manual, because those are proof that someone else asked "what else could this be?" too.

And sometimes I go back to the first builder I knew. I sit with my father and ask what he made with these same bricks when they were his: what his city looked like, and whether he ever took his fire station apart.

He usually laughs and says he followed the manuals.

The bricks on my floor are older than I am. **[CONFIRM: his father's own childhood set]** They have been a house, a city, a ship, and a hundred things without names. What I build keeps changing. What hasn't changed is the reason I sit down: to find out what something becomes when I stop following the instructions.

**Why this is stronger:**
- **One object runs the whole essay:** the shelf, then the floor, the fire station, and back to the father.
- **The "lose track of time" answer is shown,** not stated: the light outside changes and the phone goes quiet.
- **The "why" shows how he thinks:** every brick without a manual is a question.
- **There's a moment of risk:** he breaks a perfect build on purpose, and it falls over twice.
- **The "whom" turns into a scene with his father,** with a twist that works as gentle humour ("he followed the manuals").
- **The ending reframes the whole topic:** it was never about Lego, it was about not following instructions.
