## The standard

Aditya does not want a merely competent or polished application essay. He wants the strongest truthful version this material can support: an essay that makes an admissions officer understand how his mind works and remember him as a person.

That standard does **not** mean:

- accepting every suggestion that sounds more dramatic;
- adding “literary” sentences to manufacture importance;
- inventing dialogue, physical details, reactions, or motives;
- turning every sentence into a quotable line;
- making the essay sound like an admissions consultant;
- promising that an essay alone will compel admission;
- optimizing for an automated score instead of a human reader.

The target is truth with pressure: specific memories, uncomfortable motives, real contradictions, clean causality, and enough restraint that the reader discovers the meaning rather than being repeatedly told it.

## Editorial authority

Aditya has given the editor full freedom, including freedom to reject his preferences and outside reviews. Use that authority.

Do not agree automatically. For every proposed change, ask:

1. Is it factually true?
2. Does it reveal Aditya, or merely improve the sentence's surface?
3. Does it add information, causality, or emotional exposure?
4. Does it sound like something a thoughtful seventeen-year-old could genuinely say?
5. Does it work in the paragraph and the whole essay, not just in isolation?
6. Does it repeat a nearby insight, metaphor, rhythm, or callback?
7. Is it worth the words it consumes under the 650-word limit?
8. Would the essay be measurably weaker if the change were removed?

If the answer is unclear, keep the current version. Difference is not improvement.

If a revision breaks this causality, reject it.

## How to accept or reject future feedback

### Accept a suggestion when it does at least one of these

- corrects a factual error;
- supplies a true detail only Aditya could own;
- repairs missing causality;
- exposes a real, unflattering motive;
- replaces abstract explanation with a concrete action or memory;
- removes repeated sentence architecture;
- makes spoken dialogue sound closer to the real speaker;
- simplifies a sentence without flattening it;
- resolves reader confusion during a fast first read;
- improves paragraph rhythm when read aloud.

### Reject a suggestion when it does any of these

- invents intensity: `Something shifted in that minute that I didn't have language for yet.`
- turns a precise event into a grand identity claim: `I didn't know what I'd do with my life. I knew what I wouldn't.`
- names Krishna without giving the name a later function;
- removes the pseudocode-preparation line and makes Aditya appear simply unprepared;
- claims AI taught him coding instantly;
- makes AI the villain or savior;
- treats publication desire as shameful merely to manufacture vulnerability;
- adds `canon event`, memes, or internet-native language;
- uses “Q1,” journal prestige, or résumé benefit as a flex;
- overstates the reviewer evidence: `Nothing about the results.`
- manufactures arrogance: `If the numbers held, nothing else could touch me.`
- explains importance after the scene has already shown it;
- adds a callback only because another callback exists;
- makes every paragraph end with a polished aphorism;
- introduces a detail that raises a question the essay never answers;
- replaces plain English with consultant language;
- chases a score without improving the reading experience.

### When reviews conflict

Use this priority order:

1. Factual accuracy.
2. Aditya's lived emotional truth.
3. Whole-essay causality and coherence.
4. Voice and naturalness.
5. Specificity and memorability.
6. Rhythm and sentence craft.
7. Word economy.
8. Outside reviewer preference.
9. Numerical ratings.

Never use majority vote among models. Several models can repeat the same attractive but wrong suggestion.

```text
I sat down for my computer science exam and went blank. The logic was in my head: if this, then that. It never turned into syntax. Around me, pens moved and pages flipped. I'd read pseudocode until its patterns looked familiar. One question gave us a broken Fibonacci function and asked us to find the mistake. I barely understood what it was doing. How was I supposed to know what was wrong? I walked out sure of one thing: I would never write code again.

Months later, I still thought coding wasn't for me and had no plans to try again. I needed somewhere to sort my notes. When I told my brother, he opened an AI coding tool and typed a few words. A homepage appeared with pastel cards for my subjects. It worked. I still couldn't code, but code could make something I wanted. What did you type? How is this happening? What else can it make? He told me to make a project and understand it too. I heard the first half.

At first, I built things I understood but couldn't code. Then I asked for a login page and got syntax I had never seen. I pasted it in. It worked. Not asking why was a relief. Soon it stopped feeling like a shortcut and became how I finished things.

By AS Level, I took that shortcut while drafting a research paper. I asked AI what researchers had said about investor attention, then built my paper around an argument I couldn't explain. The sentences felt like mine; the thinking wasn't. I knew that and called it finished anyway. If my teacher had asked me to explain it, I would have had nothing to say.

Once I had real market data, I told my teacher I couldn't defend the first version. "Sir, this time I'll do it properly. I'll send it somewhere serious." "Okay, okay," he said. "Let's see. It will take time." I wanted him to be wrong. He wasn't.

What was still mine was the question. More investor attention usually meant more volatility. What happened when it didn't?

I kept using the tools for code and analysis. I still wanted them to answer for me. But before calling anything finished, I had to answer without them: What am I claiming? Why should anyone believe it? If I couldn't answer, I wasn't finished.

For months, I cleaned data across 23 markets and reran models, half-hoping something would break. One choice bothered me: thirty days felt too simple. I tried twenty-day and sixty-day averages and added variables. Each change blurred the signal. I returned to dividing daily trading volume by its thirty-day average to estimate investor attention. Once I could defend the paper, I sent it to a journal that seemed to fit.

The rejection arrived before review. I read it as a verdict, then read it again. One possibility caught me off guard: fit. I had spent so long asking whether it was right that I had not asked where it belonged. At the next journal, it passed the editorial screen, then came back with comments: the first figure was hard to read, the equations were unclear, and the results needed a better summary. I knew how I'd reached the results. I had assumed the reader did too. I went back to where my explanation broke. That I could fix.

A line from the Gita that I grew up hearing made sense: I had a right to my actions, but not to their fruits. Rejection hurt; I still wanted it published. But the line gave me somewhere to put the wanting.

The one thing I swore off in that exam hall is most of what I do now. I still reach beyond what I understand, and that isn't going away. The blank page still comes, and sometimes I have nothing to write. I open it anyway.
```
