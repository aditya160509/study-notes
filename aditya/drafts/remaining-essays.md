## UIUC: Second-choice major, Mathematics (150 max)


Most of the mathematics in my research arrived when a result needed defending. In my paper on investor attention across 21 markets, the first test treated each country as separate, so I switched to Driscoll-Kraay standard errors because markets tend to fall together, and later corrected for the number of things I had tested. Both fixes worked, but I learned them from other researchers' papers and could not have derived either one myself. That gap is the reason Mathematics is my second choice. At Illinois, MATH 461, Probability Theory, would give me the theory behind the tests I already run, and MATH 415, Applied Linear Algebra, the structure underneath the factor models I built for ATLAS. I would rather understand why a method holds than keep borrowing it and hoping the assumptions fit.

## Purdue: Alternate major and campus (250 max)

When I tuned NEXUS, my simulated stock exchange, one run matched all nine patterns that real markets show, and I thought the model was finished. Then I changed nothing except the way the program drew its random numbers, and the same run matched only five. I had been treating randomness as background noise when it was the part I most needed to understand, and that is why Mathematics is my alternate major.

My research has run on mathematics I picked up one tool at a time. To trust my finding that price swings jump once trading passes about 1.4 times its usual level, I learned a correction for markets that fall together and another for testing many ideas at once. I can apply both, but I could not tell you when either one breaks.

At Purdue, MA 41600, Probability, would give me the foundation I skipped, and MA 43200, Elementary Stochastic Processes, would teach me how to model orders that arrive at random, which is exactly what my simulated traders do. MA 35301, Linear Algebra II, is built on proofs, and I want that, because the factor models I built for ATLAS rest on matrix results I have used but never proved.

West Lafayette is the right campus because Mathematics and Computer Science both sit in the College of Science there, so I could keep taking computing courses and keep building NEXUS while I finally learn why the methods I depend on actually work.

## Purdue Honors 1: Interdisciplinary Academics (200 max)

PhenoSync began as a biology question about whether blueberry flowers are opening before the bumblebees that pollinate them, as springs get warmer. Biology decided what I measured, which was the date flowers open, the date bees appear and how many days the two overlap.

The data turned it into a different problem. NASA satellite readings, NOAA weather records and GBIF's citizen-science sightings were collected by different people for different reasons, and they did not always agree, so before trusting any difference I had to learn how each dataset was made. A rise in recorded bee sightings, for example, could come from more bees or simply from more people out looking for them.

Economics added a third question. Using FAOSTAT harvest records, I checked whether the mismatch, which grew by 4.57 days a year between 2016 and 2025, showed up in what farmers actually picked, which meant a statistical result of mine now had a cost attached to it.

Since then I start research differently. Before choosing a model, I ask who collected the data, why they collected it and what they were likely to miss, because that is usually where the mistakes are hiding.

## Purdue Honors 2: Undergraduate Research (200 max)

Anyone who has played in goal, as I have for years, knows that a good save and a lucky one look identical from the stands. Fantasy Premier League made me wonder whether managers can tell the difference when they pick players, and since millions of them make public choices every week, the question could actually be tested.

The paper covers 100,801 player-gameweeks. Managers rushed to buy players whose strong week came mostly from luck. Ownership of those players rose by 1.3 percentage points, and their later results did not justify it. The hardest part was deciding what counted as luck in the first place, because a convenient definition can quietly build the answer into the question, so I tried other definitions and checked that the result held under each.

What I took from it is that curiosity gets you to a question, but it takes a lot of dull checking before the answer is worth anything. The paper, written with a co-author at the University of Illinois, is now submitted to the Journal of Sports Economics. At Purdue I want to do research where other people challenge my definitions early, before I spend months defending the wrong one.

## Purdue Honors 3: Community & Global Engagement (200 max)

I want to visit Osaka for two reasons that rarely meet. The first is markets, because in 1730 the Tokugawa shogunate officially authorized futures trading at the Dojima Rice Exchange, where merchants traded contracts on harvests not yet grown, centuries before I wrote the order book for my own simulated exchange. The second is One Piece, which I have watched for more than 1,000 episodes, and I am still not sure whether that says more about my discipline or my free time.

I would begin at the old Dojima site and then spend a morning at Kuromon Market, where sellers still decide each day what to stock, what to charge and what to do with fish that will not sell by evening. Later I would walk through Den Den Town, Osaka's district of anime and electronics shops, and ask the people who run them what fans buy and why, since a fandom is also a market with its own strange prices.

Before going I would learn enough Japanese to follow a stallholder's answer without a translator. The traders in my simulator follow rules I wrote myself, so they never surprise me, and the people of Osaka would.

## Purdue Honors 4: Leadership Development (200 max)

When I founded our school's Quant Finance Club, I wrote a 13-week course and taught every session, because I assumed that leading a club meant knowing the material best and explaining it clearly.

The session that changed my mind was on the Black-Scholes equation. I worked through it one variable at a time, and because the three or four members who already knew some finance kept nodding, I kept going for an hour before noticing that the other twenty were playing games at the back. My co-founder pointed out that we had built the session for people who understood the subject.

We changed the format. The next week opened with the Monty Hall problem, which half the room argued about, and later we built Future Lab, a simulated market of 150 companies across 15 industries where members ran their own portfolios and had to explain their losses to each other. Members started asking questions I had not planned to teach.

I still write material for the club, but I now judge a session by how many people speak and whether the discussion carries on without me, and I check that with the quiet members instead of the nodding ones.

## UMass: Commonwealth Honors College (100 max)

My market simulator, NEXUS, once matched all nine patterns I checked in real markets, and I nearly called it finished until four of ten fresh runs failed. Since then every result I report lists the runs it failed. I want Commonwealth Honors College because its small, discussion-based seminars would put me among students who judge evidence in ways I do not, and the Honors Thesis would give me time to test whether NEXUS reproduces market patterns for the right reasons or only by luck.
