# Essay Standards: the 10/10 Bar

This guide is how every essay, supplement and activity line gets judged before it goes in `final draft essay.md`. An essay that passes all 12 standards is a 10. Each standard has a test you can actually run.

## The 12 standards

1. **Direct answer.** Every clause of the prompt is answered, in the prompt's order where possible.
   *Test:* break the prompt into its questions and point to the sentence that answers each one. VT's prompts have three or four questions each, and missing one costs more than a weak sentence.

2. **Concrete evidence.** Claims are shown through a moment, a decision or a number only you could write.
   *Test:* could another strong applicant paste this sentence into their essay? If yes, cut it or make it specific.

3. **Good specifics only.** Use specifics that explain what happened (who doubted you, what broke, what you changed). Avoid specifics that just show off (speeds, test counts, averages, model names).
   *Test:* does the number help a stranger understand the story? If it only proves effort, drop it.

4. **Durable truth.** Every fact is one you can defend in an interview a year from now. Code metrics change whenever the code changes, so only use them as past events ("in June, six of ten seeds matched"), never as current claims.
   *Test:* if the project changes tomorrow, is this sentence still true?

5. **Clarity for a stranger.** An admissions reader with no finance or CS background follows every line on the first read. Explain any jargon in plain words in the same sentence, or cut it.
   *Test:* read it as someone who has never heard of volatility, XGBoost or a market maker.

6. **Shows thinking.** The essay shows how you reason: a doubt, a mistake caught, a choice between options. It isn't a résumé in sentences.
   *Test:* is there one moment where you were wrong, unsure or changed your mind?

7. **One idea.** Each essay has one through-line. Courses and programs serve that idea; they are not a list.
   *Test:* sum the essay up in one sentence. If it takes "and", it's two essays.

8. **School fit with a reason.** Every named course, professor or club is tied to a need the essay has already shown ("because X broke, I need Y").
   *Test:* swap in another university's name. If the essay still works, it fails.

9. **No repeats within one school.** Each essay for the same school uses a different story, resource or angle. Reuse across different schools is fine, because each school only reads its own essays.

10. **Voice.** It sounds like a sharp 17-year-old talking: plain, mostly short sentences, active verbs. None of these AI tells: "not just X but Y", triplets for rhythm, "passion", "journey", "delve", stacked dashes, or a moral announced in the last line.
    *Test:* read it aloud. If any sentence sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

11. **Economy.** Every sentence earns its place, and the essay stays within the limit (UIUC about 150, UW 300–500 recommended, Purdue 250, UMass 100, VT 120 hard).
    *Test:* delete each sentence in turn. If nothing is lost, it stays deleted.

12. **Landing.** The last line shows a change in how you act now, as an action, not a summary.
    *Test:* does the ending describe something you now *do*?

**Humility check (applies to all 12):** credit the people who checked your work, such as your teacher, Jainam and the reviewers. Admissions readers trust applicants who let others test them.

## Activity list standard

Use **verb + what it is in plain words + one result a stranger understands**. Put the role in Position and the venue or paper in Organization, so the 150 characters go to the work itself. Leave out averages, test counts and internal metrics.

## Standards 13–16 (added after the GPT review)

13. **Precise claims.** Use "estimated" rather than "found", "suggests" rather than "proves", and describe a study's exact conditions ("when a second opinion always appeared"). Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose a technical reader.
14. **No implied guarantees.** Programs and labs are things you will *apply* to or *hope* to join, never placements ("apply to ERSP in my first year, hoping to reach his group").
15. **Honest scale.** Never imply a resource is needed when it isn't (10,200 simulations run on a laptop, so they don't need a cluster).
16. **Stands alone.** Each essay makes sense with no other essay beside it, because readers see only their own school's essays.

## School playbook (from official pages, 30 Oct 2026)

- **Illinois:** "Do not repeat things already in your application. Explain what you'll bring." The essays are about 150 words each, and the goals essay must link the major to a career.
- **Wisconsin:** both halves get answered: *why Madison* and *why this major*. Use 300–500 words, and name things only Madison has (the Sprocket Lab, WISCURDS).
- **Purdue:** essay 1 is about *opportunities*, so use concrete programs (EPICS, a professor, a club). Essay 2 must cover the major *and* the campus (West Lafayette).
- **UMass:** 100 words is a hard limit. Question 3 changes depending on whether you choose Honors: the community version is for non-Honors applicants. Confirm it in the application.
- **Virginia Tech:** 120 words is a hard limit, and answers can't be changed after you submit. Every sub-question needs a sentence. VT may barely read the Common App essay, so these answers carry the file.

## How to treat outside reviews

Check each point against the *current* draft, because reviews are often of older versions. Apply only what makes an essay truer, clearer or more complete. Reject changes that flatten your voice or add generic motivation.
