# Aditya Balaji — Supplement Master Material Book

**Cycle:** 2026–27 applications (intended 2027 entry)  
**Last researched:** 25 August 2026  
**Purpose:** the source of truth for planning and drafting every university supplement. This is not a set of finished essays. It is the material, allocation, factual guardrails, and school-by-school strategy needed to write them without repetition or invention.

> Live portals override this document. Re-check every prompt, limit, program, and deadline immediately before drafting and again before submission.

---

## 1. The application should leave this impression

Aditya is not simply “a student who has done a lot of AI and finance work.” He is a builder-researcher who becomes bothered when a system produces an answer nobody can properly explain. He moves between mathematics, computing, markets, sport, climate, and engineering because the recurring question is the same: **what is really happening underneath the output, and how can we test it?**

The complete application should make a reader feel five things:

1. **Intellectual appetite:** he follows questions beyond the syllabus and across disciplinary boundaries.
2. **Ownership:** he has learned to distinguish producing an answer from understanding and defending it.
3. **Usefulness:** he builds systems that other people can use—teammates, club members, researchers, and students—not only private demonstrations.
4. **Human range:** football, Fantasy Premier League, teaching, family, and ordinary habits coexist with research.
5. **Productive incompleteness:** he is ambitious and unusually capable, but still learning to pace, collaborate, explain, and ask for help.

### The sentence-level test

Every supplement should contain at least one sentence that could only belong to Aditya. If a sentence could be pasted into a thousand high-achieving STEM applications—“I hope to create meaningful impact,” “technology can change the world,” “I am passionate about interdisciplinary learning”—delete it or replace it with an action, question, object, result, mistake, or relationship.

## 2. What the Common App essay already owns

The personal statement already covers:

- freezing in a pen-and-paper computer-science exam;
- the broken Fibonacci question;
- the first AI-built notes homepage and login page;
- the relief of not asking why;
- using AI while drafting an early investor-attention paper without owning the reasoning;
- the conversation with the research teacher;
- rebuilding the paper with real market data;
- the 20/30/60-day attention-measure decision;
- journal fit, peer-review comments, and explanation;
- the Gita line about actions and fruits;
- opening the blank page anyway.

### Repetition rule

No supplement should retell this arc. A fragment may reappear only when the new essay reveals a different quality.

- **Allowed:** mentioning the investor-attention question in a “Why major?” response, then moving immediately to the next unanswered problem.
- **Allowed:** using the notes site in one clause to explain why building became a mode of learning.
- **Not allowed:** another shortcut → guilt → rebuild → rejection → wisdom story.
- **Not allowed:** reusing “the thinking wasn’t mine,” “before calling anything finished,” the Gita, or the blank-page ending as a portable slogan.

The supplements must add dimensions the personal statement does not already supply.

## 3. The seven material lanes

### Lane A — Intellectual signature: systems under uncertainty

**Core idea:** Outputs are easy to generate; mechanisms, assumptions, and failure modes are harder and more interesting.

**Best evidence:**

- GASI: investor attention usually increases volatility, but peak attention produced the “Silence Signature.”
- NEXUS Exchange: testing the empirical threshold through an entirely different agent-based simulation.
- Glassbox SRE: forcing impact figures to come from Prometheus rather than allowing a language model to invent them; evaluation improved top-1 accuracy from 13.3% to 86.7%.
- NTK exposition: translating a frontier idea into matrices, eigenvalues, Taylor series, and ODEs Aditya could manipulate line by line.
- PhenoSync: a biological-timing question crossing climate, statistics, agriculture, and economics.

**Best prompts:** why major, intellectual curiosity, MIT field/problems, Stanford learning, Princeton engineering/academic interest, UCAS questions 1 and 2.

**Guardrail:** one technical object per short essay. Explain the puzzle and the intellectual move; do not inventory methods.

### Lane B — Builder who wants real users

**Core idea:** The satisfying moment is not deployment. It is when another person relies on the system and exposes what the builder failed to anticipate.

**Best evidence:**

- Daedalus is used by the STEM Racing team for real design trade-offs.
- ATLAS is used by a computer-science graduate for real analysis.
- Future Lab gives 25–30 club members a market they can investigate rather than merely hear described.
- A-Level Central organizes actual notes and resources, beginning with pastel subject cards.

**Best prompts:** contribution, engineering, why school, leadership, educational journey.

**Guardrail:** do not treat “used in production” as a trophy. Show what the user needed, what changed because of feedback, or what responsibility real use created.

### Lane C — Leadership as infrastructure

**Core idea:** Aditya’s leadership is not commanding a room; it is making a room, system, or routine reliable enough that people return.

**Best evidence:**

- Quant Finance Club: 25–30 members, 13-week curriculum, Future Lab as shared learning infrastructure.
- Sports Analysis Hub: initiating and sustaining meetings; leadership through presence rather than title.
- STEM Racing: building Daedalus around teammates’ decisions.

**Future Lab leadership failure:** Aditya divided the repository into backend, frontend, calculations, and database work, with five phased checkpoints and minimum features required at each stage. One contribution failed; he took it back. He helped another teammate begin, but ownership remained uneven. The failure was not an absence of planning. The checkpoints measured whether features worked, not whether each person understood enough of the whole system to make independent choices.

**Best prompts:** Stanford contribution, Virginia Tech leadership, MIT community impact, Princeton lived experience.

**Verified meeting detail:** the club’s Black–Scholes explanation lost the room. Aditya later hosted a ten-door Monty Hall game: one first choice, eight empty doors revealed, then a stay-or-switch argument. This opened discussion about how new information should change a decision and how attention can settle on the wrong option.

### Lane D — Service through adaptation

**Core idea:** Explanation has not succeeded when the speaker finishes; it succeeds when the learner can use it.

**Best evidence:**

- Teaching foundational mathematics and English to more than 50 rural children.
- Changing lesson delivery week after week until material stuck.
- Raising ₹25,000+ and managing the unglamorous logistics of kits, clothing, and footwear.

**Best prompts:** Princeton service, Virginia Tech Ut Prosim/community, inclusion, civic engagement.

**Guardrail:** no “I gave disadvantaged children hope.” Center the students’ agency, the limits of Aditya’s first approach, the adaptation, and what sustained service demanded.

**Verified teaching detail:** after direct explanations failed, Aditya divided students into teams. One child who had barely participated worked through a basic algebra problem because getting it right meant beating his friends’ team. The exact algebra problem is not remembered and must not be invented.

### Lane E — Sport, play, and ordinary joy

**Core idea:** Football is both bodily and analytical, but it must not always become research.

**Best evidence:**

- Goalkeeping: the quiet period, sudden responsibility, recovering after a visible mistake.
- Fantasy Premier League as genuine play before it became a behavioral-market question.
- Sports Analysis Hub conversations and disagreements.

**Best prompts:** MIT fun, Princeton joy, Stanford roommate, short answers.

**Guardrail:** at least one short answer should allow football or FPL to remain fun. Do not optimize every hobby into an academic achievement.

**Verified goalkeeping scene:** across one tournament, Aditya faced twelve penalties and helped his team reach the final. A national-level side then overwhelmed them. He still received best goalkeeper but felt little satisfaction because he was replaying the final rather than the tournament.

### Lane F — Culture, family, and moral vocabulary

**Core idea:** Family and Indian cultural life should appear as lived texture, not decoration or a manufactured “identity essay.”

**Verified material:**

- Mumbai has always been home.
- Krishna, Aditya’s older brother, showed him the coding tool and told him to build a project and understand it too.
- Aditya grew up hearing the Gita’s ideas and devotional music such as Suprabhatam/Bhaja Govindam in family life; exact claims about a specific verse must remain accurate.

**Best prompts:** Stanford roommate or contribution, Princeton lived experience, admiration short answer.

**Guardrail:** the Common App already uses the Gita. Do not reuse it unless another prompt truly asks for the cultural context and the new essay adds something not present there.

**Missing detail needed:** ordinary household rituals, languages used with different people, meals, music, family jokes, responsibilities, and one way Mumbai has shaped how Aditya notices systems or people.

**Verified household detail:** Aditya's parents call frequently, sometimes in the middle of the afternoon, simply to ask, “Khana khaya ki nahi?” A roommate would learn the question quickly. Do not invent an exact time.

### Lane G — The unresolved person

**Core idea:** Aditya’s growth is real but incomplete. He can take on too much, polish too long, move faster than understanding, communicate late, and hesitate to bring others into work.

**Best evidence:**

- too many simultaneous ambitious projects;
- repeated model testing partly driven by wanting a result to move;
- learning to ask for help earlier and to communicate progress before the final product;
- the difference between useful rigor and perfectionism.

**Best prompts:** challenge, unexpected situation, roommate, personal contribution, educational journey.

**Guardrail:** do not package perfectionism as “my greatest weakness is caring too much.” Choose a real cost and a changed behavior.

## 4. The strongest one-of-a-kind story modules

These are ranked by how much they reveal beyond the activity list.

### Module 1 — A market becoming quieter when everyone looks

**Opening object:** More attention should mean more volatility. At the highest attention levels, it did not.

**Human/intellectual turn:** Aditya first treated the non-moving output as suspicious and kept testing it, partly hoping something would break. The anomaly survived. That changed the question from “How can I make the model stronger?” to “What behavior could make silence itself a signal?”

**What it reveals:** curiosity, falsification, comfort with a result that resists the expected story.

**Use for:** Stanford learning; MIT field/problems; Princeton academic/engineering; UCAS.

**Do not:** repeat the paper-rebuild chronology from the Common App.

### Module 2 — FPL as a market where belief is visible

**Opening object:** Millions of players publicly reveal whom they trust by putting a footballer into a fantasy team.

**Question:** What is the distance between the player people should choose and the player they actually choose—and what creates it?

**What it reveals:** Aditya does not leave intellectual life at the classroom door; play becomes a behavioral question because he notices incentives, imitation, and information design.

**Use for:** MIT “hours” short answer, Stanford learning, Princeton curiosity, roommate/fun in a lighter form.

**Do not:** lead with the £137.1M estimate or t-statistic. Those are evidence after the reader understands the puzzle.

### Module 3 — The platform became real when someone else depended on it

**Possible versions:**

- Daedalus: a teammate needs a design decision, not a beautiful dashboard.
- ATLAS: a graduate user encounters an assumption Aditya had not made visible.
- Future Lab: club members make decisions inside a market Aditya designed.

**What it reveals:** responsibility, usability, collaboration, communication.

**Use for:** contribution, leadership, why engineering, challenge.

**Missing fact:** obtain one exact user interaction before drafting.

### Module 4 — Teaching until the explanation belongs to the learner

**Opening object:** a specific mathematics or English explanation that failed the first time.

**Turn:** Aditya changes the example, sequence, language, or activity; the learner then does something independently.

**What it reveals:** humility, attentiveness, service, communication.

**Use for:** Princeton service; Virginia Tech community/inclusion; MIT community impact.

**Status:** draftable through the verified algebra-team moment. Keep the question generic unless the exact problem returns naturally.

### Module 5 — The goalkeeper’s lonely seconds

**Opening object:** most of a goalkeeper’s match is waiting; one mistake is immediately visible.

**Turn:** recovery is not a speech about resilience. It is resetting position before the next attack.

**What it reveals:** embodied attention, accountability, calm, life outside the laptop.

**Use for:** MIT fun/challenge, Stanford roommate, Princeton joy, Virginia Tech leadership.

**Missing fact:** one real save, error, teammate interaction, or pre-match ritual.

### Module 6 — Turning A-Level mathematics into a frontier explanation

**Opening object:** “Neural Tangent Kernel” sounded inaccessible; the underlying pieces—matrices, eigenvalues, Taylor series, ODEs—were already within reach.

**Turn:** instead of collecting terminology, Aditya built a two-neuron example line by line until every symbol had a job.

**What it reveals:** educational independence, translation, first-principles learning.

**Use for:** MIT different educational journey, Princeton BSE, Purdue why major, UCAS preparation.

### Module 7 — Glassbox: refusing invented certainty

**Opening object:** an incident-response AI can sound confident while inventing the number that matters most.

**Design choice:** impact numbers come from Prometheus and timelines from stored events; the model must cite evidence.

**What it reveals:** technical ethics expressed through architecture, not slogans.

**Use for:** MIT problems/impact, Stanford learning, why CS, why institution.

**Do not:** reuse the Common App’s general AI-ownership lesson. Keep this about system design and evaluation.

### Module 8 — Climate timing as economic risk

**Opening object:** temperature, flowering, pollination, and harvest do not shift on the same clock.

**Question:** when biological schedules separate, how can open data make the mismatch visible before it becomes agricultural loss?

**What it reveals:** range beyond finance, public-interest data work, interdisciplinarity.

**Use for:** MIT problems/community impact; Princeton academic curiosity; Stanford contribution; why school.

## 5. School-by-school writing map

### MIT — highest priority

Official 2026–27 requirements: four responses of approximately 100–200 words, four 40–50 word answers, four activities, and optional additional information. [Official prompts](https://mitadmissions.org/apply/firstyear/essays-activities-academics/)

| Prompt | Primary material | Backup | What the response must add |
|---|---|---|---|
| Field of study and what led to it | FPL/GASI pattern: computation as a way to test human systems | NTK from A-Level math | A clean intellectual origin without retelling the Common App |
| Different educational journey | NTK exposition from A-Level tools; independent curriculum built around questions | IPQ → GASI → NEXUS as escalating modes of evidence | Why the path was different, not a résumé list |
| Problems to tackle + community impact | Trustworthy decision systems: Glassbox + PhenoSync | transparent market tools | A human stakeholder and the kind of error that matters |
| Unexpected challenge | A real team/user failure in Daedalus or Future Lab | goalkeeping | Changed behavior; do not reuse exam/research rejection |
| Fun (40–50) | goalkeeper/FPL banter/football | a non-academic habit still missing | Let it be fun |
| Admired person | likely Krishna, teacher, parent, or goalkeeper—decision needed | public figure only if genuinely personal | One observed quality, not biography |
| Hours-long topic | FPL ownership behavior or why markets become quiet under attention | agent societies | Infectious curiosity, almost no credentials |
| Generalist or specialist | “specialist in a kind of question; generalist in tools” | generalist with a unifying test | Avoid the predictable “both” unless phrased concretely |

**MIT activity allocation recommendation:** GASI research; ATLAS or Glassbox; Quant Finance Club/Future Lab; rural teaching or goalkeeping. Four different functions, not four technical variations.

## 6. Reusable draft kernels (material, not final prose)

### Why Computer Science

**Claim:** Computer Science is the discipline that lets Aditya turn questions about complex behavior into systems that can be tested, inspected, and used.

**Evidence chain:**

1. FPL/markets made human decisions measurable.
2. GASI revealed an unexpected pattern but could not alone explain its mechanism.
3. NEXUS let Aditya build a synthetic market and test whether different agents could reproduce it.
4. Glassbox pushed the same concern into AI systems: outputs must connect to evidence.
5. University study should deepen algorithms, probability, systems, ML, and causal reasoning—not merely allow bigger projects.

**Avoid:** “I love coding because it brings ideas to life.”

### Intellectual curiosity

**Best opening question:** Why would a market become quieter precisely when everyone is watching?

**Movement:** expectation → stubborn anomaly → alternative tests → new mechanism/question → what remains unresolved.

**End forward:** the important result was not a threshold number; it was learning to treat the result that did not fit the story as the start of the next question.

### Contribution to campus

**Claim:** Aditya contributes by turning private curiosity into shared infrastructure.

**Evidence:** Future Lab/club curriculum; Sports Analysis Hub consistency; Daedalus for teammates; adapting explanations while teaching.

**Future behavior:** create spaces where students from finance, sport, engineering, and computing can test claims together; publish tools and assumptions; invite criticism earlier.

**Avoid:** promising to “start a club” before researching what already exists.

### Service

**Claim:** service taught Aditya that intention and delivery are not the same; the other person decides whether the explanation or resource is useful.

**Evidence needed:** real lesson scene + adaptation + sustained weekly return. Crowdfunding/logistics can show that service also includes repetitive uncelebrated work.

**Avoid:** counts as the emotional climax. “50+ children” and “₹25,000” belong in activities; the essay needs one relationship or learning moment.

### Challenge outside the Common App

Priority order:

1. a real user/team exposed a flaw in Daedalus, ATLAS, or Future Lab;
2. a club session failed and Aditya changed how he taught or led;
3. a goalkeeper error and recovery;
4. overcommitment forced prioritization and earlier communication.

The challenge must include an actual cost, another person, and a changed practice.

### “Why us?” architecture

Use three links, not a brochure list:

1. **Question:** the unresolved problem Aditya brings.
2. **Mechanism:** a course/lab/professor/program that supplies a capability he lacks.
3. **Community/use:** a group, project, or context where he will test, share, or apply it.

For every school-specific resource, answer: “What would Aditya do there in the first year that he cannot do alone now?” If there is no concrete answer, the resource is decorative and should be removed.

## 8. High-yield questions that must be answered

These are the missing material most likely to improve the whole supplement set. Answer in rough speech; polished English is not needed.

1. What happened in one rural lesson when your first explanation did not work? What exactly did you change, and what did a student do afterward?
2. Describe one Quant Finance Club meeting that was going badly. What were members doing or not doing? What did you change?
3. Who is one club member or teammate whose response changed how you built Future Lab or Daedalus?
4. What is one real mistake you made as a goalkeeper? What happened in the next five minutes?
5. What do you do for fun that you have never converted into a project, paper, club, or application item?
6. What would a roommate notice during your first week—sleep, alarms, desk, food, music, football, calls home, jokes, mess, or rituals?
7. What five physical things, people, places, or routines matter to you right now? No abstract values.
8. What song are you actually playing most in August 2026, and why now?
9. Which person do you admire because of something you have personally watched them do?
10. What historical event have you genuinely wished you could witness before seeing Stanford’s prompt?
11. What is a skill you want that is not another extension of your current résumé?
12. Describe one normal morning or long drive with your family: who is there, what is playing, what is said, and what always happens?
13. What part of Mumbai feels most yours? Give a route, sound, shop, ground, train, street, or routine.
14. When have you felt excluded, or watched someone else being excluded, and taken a specific action? Do not force an answer if no honest incident exists.
15. What project are you currently willing to stop or postpone? What made you choose?
16. What is the exact degree/program planned at every university? This controls prompt strategy and all “Why us?” research.
17. What did you actually do in each of the last two summers, month by month?
18. What disagreement do friends repeatedly have with you? When are they right?

## 9. Drafting and acceptance standard

### Accept a draft only if

- it answers the exact prompt in its first movement;
- it reveals something not already supplied by the Common App or activities list;
- it contains a real choice, tension, relationship, or question;
- its technical detail is understandable on first read and necessary to the meaning;
- it shows what Aditya did and how his thinking changed without announcing a lesson;
- school-specific claims are sourced and connected to a future action;
- its most memorable line emerges from truth rather than metaphor engineering;
- Aditya can defend every factual statement aloud;
- reading it aloud sounds like one person thinking, not an admissions consultant arranging virtues.

### Reject or rewrite a draft if

- it is primarily a list of projects, methods, or university resources;
- its conclusion says “this taught me the importance of...”;
- “impact,” “innovation,” “passion,” “interdisciplinary,” or “community” could be removed without changing the meaning;
- the school name could be replaced by another with only noun swaps;
- it makes Aditya morally flawless after one realization;
- it converts every personal interest into productivity;
- it uses impressive numbers before the reader understands why they matter;
- it invents dialogue, scenery, motivations, or emotional timing;
- it depends on publication rank or “Q1” to establish intellectual worth;
- it repeats the Common App’s AI-risk material more loudly than the Common App itself.

### Technical-detail test

Use this order: **question → plain-language mechanism → one precise detail → why it changed the inquiry.**

Bad: “I used Lewbel IV identification, Driscoll–Kraay errors, and profile likelihood across 22 markets.”  
Better material: “Because attention and volatility can move each other, I needed a way to separate cause from correlation. The method mattered because the strange high-attention pattern survived that challenge.”

### Final voice test

Read every essay aloud once without editing on screen. Mark:

- sentences too symmetrical to sound spoken;
- clusters of short sentences that feel manufactured;
- callbacks that advertise their cleverness;
- abstract nouns standing where an action should be;
- places where the sentence knows the moral too early.

Then make only edits that improve truth, clarity, rhythm, or individuality. Do not polish away the unevenness that makes the voice recognizably Aditya’s.

## 20. A universal supplement scoring rubric

Score every draft out of 100. Do not compare raw totals across different prompt types until the prompt-specific weighting is applied.

| Dimension | Points | Question |
|---|---:|---|
| Prompt completion | 15 | Does it answer every clause directly? |
| New information | 12 | Does it add something absent from Common App and activities? |
| Specificity | 12 | Can the reader name the actual question, action, object, or person? |
| Reflection | 12 | Does thinking change through the essay rather than appear only at the end? |
| Voice | 12 | Does it sound recognizably like Aditya aloud? |
| Human presence | 10 | Is another person, relationship, bodily experience, or ordinary life visible where appropriate? |
| Intellectual quality | 10 | Is the question itself interesting and accurately explained? |
| Structure/rhythm | 7 | Does each sentence cause the next without feeling engineered? |
| Restraint | 5 | Has résumé flexing, prestige, and unnecessary terminology been cut? |
| Future fit/action | 5 | Where relevant, is the future concrete and earned? |

### Prompt-specific weighting changes

- **Roommate/fun/joy:** double Human presence; halve Intellectual quality.
- **Why major/UCAS:** double Intellectual quality; keep Human presence at 5.
- **Why us:** double Future fit/action; require sourced resources.
- **Service/community:** double Human presence; numbers count very little.
- **50-word short answers:** Voice and specificity dominate; do not force a complete narrative arc.

### Score interpretation

- **90–100:** distinctive, truthful, complementary, ready for word-level refinement.
- **82–89:** strong but one dimension remains generic, repetitive, or thin.
- **72–81:** competent answer, not yet application-building.
- **Below 72:** wrong story, incomplete prompt response, or insufficient real material; rebuild instead of polishing.

No draft receives 95+ merely because every sentence is polished. A 95+ response must change how the reader understands Aditya.

## 21. Research-backed craft findings

### Finding 1 — “Authentic” does not mean unedited

Official guidance warns against prose edited into someone else’s voice, not against revision itself. The correct process is: recover Aditya’s raw thought, clarify it, remove generic language, read aloud, and retain his syntax where it carries personality. Do not import a sample essay’s cleverness.

### Finding 2 — A supplement is an application component, not a standalone masterpiece

The best topic depends on what the rest of the file already proves. Aditya’s activities already establish technical ambition. Therefore the marginal value of another technical achievement essay is lower than the value of a real teammate, failed explanation, ordinary ritual, or moment of joy.

### Finding 3 — Bounded evidence beats thematic claims

“I value collaboration” is weak. “I arrived with a finished simulator; club members could not tell why prices moved, so I exposed the five drivers and let them change the weights” would be strong—**if true**. The event proves the trait and supplies its limits.

### Finding 4 — School research must change the proposed action

Course names and labs are not proof of fit. A resource earns words only when it provides a missing capability, collaborator, constraint, or audience. The essay should make clear what Aditya would do differently because that resource exists.

### Finding 5 — The most memorable detail is often ordinary

Pastel subject cards, a goalkeeper resetting after a mistake, a club member returning, or a family song in the car can identify Aditya more effectively than another Q1 journal or model statistic. Distinctiveness comes from specificity and perception, not scale alone.

### Finding 6 — Short prompts punish throat-clearing

At 50 or 120 words, begin inside the answer. Remove “One thing that has always been important to me is...” and “Throughout my life...” The title/prompt already supplies context.

### Finding 7 — Do not resolve every tension

Aditya can still want publication, struggle to stop adding tests, or arrive too late with feedback requests. An honest unresolved edge makes growth believable. The supplement need not turn him into the person who has permanently solved himself.
