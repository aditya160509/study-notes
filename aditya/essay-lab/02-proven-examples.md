# 02 - Proven Winning Essays: What Worked and Why

A field guide built from 12 published, verifiable winning essays: official "essays that worked" pages (Johns Hopkins, MIT), viral essays covered by major outlets (Business Insider, Boston.com), and expert-analyzed accepted essays (College Essay Guy, CollegeAdvisor, AdmitYogi, College Confidential). Each entry: essay (full or substantial excerpt), school and source URL, technique breakdown, applicant fit. Quotes are verbatim from the cited sources.

---

## 1. "Costco" - Brittany Stinson (Yale, Columbia, UPenn, Dartmouth, Cornell, Stanford)

**Excerpt:**

> Managing to break free from my mother's grasp, I charged... I was the ferocious two year old rampaging through Costco on a Saturday morning... I was a conquistador, but rather than searching the land for El Dorado, I scoured aisles for free samples... I contemplated the philosophical: If there exists a thirty-three ounce jar of Nutella, do we really have free will?... With cart in hand, I do what scares me; I absorb the warehouse that is the world.

**Source:** Yale + 4 Ivies + Stanford (2016). Full text: https://www.businessinsider.com/high-school-senior-who-got-into-5-ivy-league-schools-shares-her-admissions-essay-2016-4

**Why it won:** The most famous unexpected-hook essay in modern admissions. A retail store becomes a kingdom, a conquistador quest, and a physics lab in one extended metaphor. The hook is cinematic; the metaphor carries every trait (curiosity, risk-taking). Playful voice, serious insight: "In essence, I subsist on discovery."

**Fits:** The curious generalist - many interests, one thread to tie them. Transfers to any place the applicant knows intimately (a market, workshop, family business).

## 2. "I hate the letter S" - Abigail Mack (Harvard, Class of 2025)

**Excerpt:**

> I hate the letter S. Of the 164,777 words with S, I only grapple with one. To condemn an entire letter because of its use .0006 percent of the time sounds statistically absurd, but that one case changed 100 percent of my life. I used to have two parents, but now I have one, and the S in parents isn't going anywhere.
> ... I stopped running away from a single S and began chasing a double S: Passion. Passion has given me purpose... I finally learned to move forward instead of away, and it's liberating.

**Source:** Harvard Class of 2025 admit; viral on TikTok. Text via: https://www.boston.com/news/local-news/2021/05/13/i-hate-the-letter-s-this-college-essay-on-the-loss-of-a-parent-helped-a-bridgewater-teen-into-harvard-and-went-viral/

**Why it won:** A grief essay that dodges the "pity party" trap (her own words) by hiding loss inside a linguistic puzzle. The hook weaponizes statistics; the reader discovers the trauma through the device. Structure: avoidance (busyness as escape) to growth ("chasing a double S"), ending honestly un-healed.

**Fits:** Applicants writing about loss or hardship who need a frame that turns pain into insight, not therapy.

## 3. "Where Math Collides With Art" - Anthony M. (Johns Hopkins, Class of 2029)

**Excerpt:**

> Pop quiz: A bird shoots through the crisp morning air of New York City, dodging skyscrapers at thirty kilometers per hour... How do we use velocity, angles, distance, and force to find the point at which the glass shatters?
> For me, math is more than just numbers. It's a mode of visualizing movement in action... My parametric equations are not simple problems with one-step calculations - they are cinematic universes...
> As for the bird that caught its reflection in the office window - perhaps it isn't about the force of the collision, but what happens after. The way the shards of glass multiply a single reflection into thousands of new perspectives.

**Source:** JHU official "Essays That Worked," with admissions committee comments. https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/where-math-collides-with-art/

**Why it won:** The gold standard for the academic-passion essay. Opens inside a math problem as a hook, declares the thesis in metaphor ("math as a brush to paint a canvas"), then proves it with named action (writing cinematic problems for peers, Teachers Pay Teachers, Math Olympiad captain, co-founding a nonprofit). Ends by returning to the opening image with new meaning.

**Fits:** STEM applicants (math, CS, engineering) with real proof of teaching, mentoring, or building. The direct model for a Quant Club leader or research-driven math student.

## 4. "Be the Salt of the Earth" - Maria G. (Johns Hopkins, Class of 2029)

**Excerpt:**

> "No le pongas demasiado sal!" My mom, anticipating a bitter taste from the soup, alarmed me... I felt it was my mission to discover the secrets behind the little white container...
> Ironically, for the entirety of my early adolescence, I underestimated the value of salt... fainting in the presence of heat conversely transformed this mindset... each little grain of salt in my vision embodied human connection, presenting roots and traditions with pride.
> Now, every grain of salt is an insight of diversity in our world and human interactions... salt awakened my passion for revealing paths with solutions.

**Source:** JHU official "Essays That Worked." https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/be-the-salt-of-the-earth/

**Why it won:** Extended-metaphor essay done right: one everyday object (salt) carries chemistry, history, migration, and identity. A Mexican-American student who moved states uses salt as the lens for cultural adaptation (sea salt in brown rice, lunch-table friendships), pivoting into international relations and founding her school's UNICEF Club. The metaphor is never dropped.

**Fits:** International, immigrant, or "third-culture" students whose story is adaptation and identity, especially for IR or policy.

## 5. MIT essays - Victor D. '27 (MIT, Course 11-6: Urban Science & Planning + CS)

**Excerpts (from his retrospective on the official MIT admissions blog):**

> Strawberries, blueberries, yogurt, almonds, orange juice. Pop them in the blender, and... presto! I love smoothie making... Blending smoothies is kinda like chemistry... I'm an avid Mango Lassi enthusiast... I researched the drink's ancient history for a cultural perspective to recreate Lassi... And finally! - I blended a most delectable Mango Lassi.
> (On "significant challenge," about oboe reeds): My first reed was crooked, but at least it was something... my friend and I decided to experiment with a 7-11 plastic Slurpee Cup... We laughed in our failure together... A few days before a concert, my primary reed cracked... I frankensteined a new one. The concert was a success! Lesson learned: always have spares...

**Source:** Official MIT Admissions blog, admitted Class of 2027. https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/my-mit-admissions-essays/

**Why it won:** The "for the pleasure of it" essay picks a deliberately mundane topic (smoothies) and sells it with chemistry humor and cultural depth, proving curiosity is a lifestyle. The failure essay subverts the prompt (an oboe reed, not trauma): low-stakes, funny, yet persistent. His own test: a topic that is "absurd/unusual... but meaningful and memorable."

**Fits:** STEM applicants who want to show intellectual vitality without sounding robotic - quirky hobbies, small honest failures, interdisciplinary interests. Ideal for MIT-style "pleasure" prompts.

## 6. MIT essay - student from Gaza (international, service) - MIT

**Excerpt:**

> Last May, [Place Redacted] sank into brutal violence for 20 days. My last days of holy Ramadan became defiled with dark traumatizing obsessions of losing everything I loved... I knew I had to transform my feelings into public service.
> In June... I joined the Disaster Group at the [Organization Redacted] Institute... I worked with children with PTSD, whose houses were demolished during the armed operation, on relieving their stress through storytelling and fun activities. Throughout the summer, I told "The Golden Hen" and sang "Tick Tick Tick Ya Ladybug" more times than I can count... Even though [Place Redacted] is a place where empathy and compassion are crushed by wars and deprived circumstances, I believe we can empower our children through love, support, and care.

**Source:** MIT admit, AdmitYogi's "46 Essays that Worked at MIT." https://admityogi.com/example-essays/mit

**Why it won:** An international applicant turning lived trauma into forward action. The essay does not dwell on suffering; it converts it ("transform my feelings into public service") and shows the concrete work (children's storytelling, named songs). The insight - empathy survives even where it is crushed - is the takeaway.

**Fits:** International applicants from conflict zones or displaced backgrounds whose response to adversity is service, not suffering.

## 7. UChicago extended essay, "Why are you here and not somewhere else?" - Indian international student

**Excerpt:**

> In a culture where Bollywood's 'item girls' receive fame and glory for their provocative dancing and scant clothing, I am often filled with shame and even disgust for my own Indian heritage... Because I notice these things, and define them as blatantly sexist, does that make me less Indian?
> ... The living room and kitchen stand divided between the men and women. As a female young-adult, I am confused as to where I belong - to which room do I go?... I feel sick to my stomach, as I alone perceive the waves of sexism that ripple beneath our facade.

**Source:** UChicago admit, CollegeAdvisor's UChicago compilation. https://www.collegeadvisor.com/essay-guides/uchicago-essay-examples/

**Why it won:** The UChicago "uncommon essay" at its best: it answers a philosophical prompt ("why are you here?") with a genuine cultural investigation. She examines her culture's sexism (dowries, Bollywood, divided living rooms) through two lenses - feminist and insider - and refuses a tidy answer. Holding ambiguity IS the thesis, exactly what UChicago rewards.

**Fits:** International students who can write honestly about tensions in their own culture (gender, class, religion). A template for "uncommon"/quirky-prompt schools.

## 8. Stanford Common App essay - "But those people were wrong" - Stanford

**Excerpt (refrain structure):**

> When my parents met, my mom was a 16-year-old, straight-A student from Indiana... "Don't date him," they told her... But those people were right. He was nothing but trouble... By middle school, my mother was selling drugs to pay the bills... "The daughter is going to end up just like the mother," they said... But those people were wrong.
> I may not have had parents to guide me, but I had books that showed me a better way... By the time summer arrived, I had written an entire book with 36 chapters... What matters is that it's possible for a girl like me to create my own ending.

**Source:** Stanford admit, College Confidential's essay series. https://collegeconfidential.com/articles/stanford-essays-that-worked/

**Why it won:** Structural genius: a repeating refrain ("But those people were right" x3, then the pivot "But those people were wrong") builds tension and delivers the thesis in one line. The essay shows how poverty and addiction shaped her without defining her - books as escape, writing as agency. The final line ("create my own ending") is double meaning: her book and her life.

**Fits:** Applicants from difficult family circumstances (single parents, addiction, poverty) who show the mechanism of their escape and growth; a masterclass in refrain structure for anyone.

## 9. "My Pillows & Me" - CS student (College Essay Guy examples)

**Excerpt:**

> "I can't sleep without them!" my face scrunched up, clutching piles of cushions... Today, ten-years later, I still relish flopping backwards onto my bed, cushioned by my carefully curated collection of pillows, each encasing a different side of myself...
> From the "Coding Projects in Scratch" book, I took inspiration to tinker with block-based programming, designing my own graphics-based puzzles & platformers that provided me the fundamentals to pursue higher-level CS in Python & Java...
> Maybe by next year... I'll have developed the next cutting-edge software to synthesize singing... I'll sleep on it.

**Source:** Accepted-essay compilation (student references her Columbia University Java class). https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-examples

**Why it won:** Montage structure: one physical thread (pillows) holds six separate selves - CS programmer, artist, swimmer, anime fan, overachiever, family kid. Each pillow = one identity plus one specific proof (Scratch to Python/Java, VOCALOID covers). The one-liner ending ("I'll sleep on it") is the payoff - witty, self-aware, and it delivers her insight (balance).

**Fits:** CS/STEM applicants who are also creative (art, music, design). The thread can be any collection: stickers, sneakers, playlists.

## 10. "Laptop Stickers" - business/activism student (College Essay Guy examples)

**Excerpt:**

> My laptop is like a passport. It is plastered with stickers all over the outside, inside, and bottom. Each sticker is a stamp, representing a place I've been, a passion I've pursued, or community I've belonged to. Let me take you on a quick tour:... "Catapult," middle right. This is the logo of a startup incubator where I launched my first company, Threading Twine. I learned that business can provide others access to fundamental human needs, such as economic empowerment of minorities and education... When my computer dies, it will be like my passport expiring... My next set of stickers will reveal my next set of aspirations.

**Source:** Accepted-essay compilation. https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-examples

**Why it won:** The tour structure (walk the reader through the object) makes the essay impossible to skim and shows a wide range - design, TEDx, a startup, activism, family. The passport metaphor upgrades stickers into identity; the ending points forward to the next stamp. The closest template here to a finance/business applicant: commerce connected to values.

**Fits:** Business, econ, or finance applicants with ventures or side projects who want to show the values behind the ambition.

## 11. "Punk Rock Philosopher" - David Phan (College Essay Guy examples)

**Excerpt:**

> I am on Oxford Academy's Speech and Debate Team... I have accumulated over 300 community service hours... But I am not any of these things. I am not a test score, nor a debater, nor a writer. I am an anti-nihilist punk rock philosopher. And I became so when I realized three things: 1) That the world is ruled by underwear... 2) When I realized I cannot understand the world [debating nuclear arms]... 3) When I realized I was a punk rocker philosopher [an underground show in an abandoned church]... The world I come from consists of underwear, nuclear bombs, and punk rockers. And I love this world.

**Source:** Accepted-essay compilation. https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-examples

**Why it won:** A voice essay. It opens by listing impressive credentials and then rejects them ("I am not any of these things") - an inversion that instantly creates a distinct narrator. The flat, confident tone ("the world is ruled by underwear") makes it funny; the identity ("anti-nihilist punk rock philosopher") is the thread tying the montage together.

**Fits:** The quirky, counterculture applicant - debaters, artists, musicians, anyone with a witty contrarian voice. High risk, high reward; only works if the humor is earned by real substance.

## 12. "What Rocks Tell Us" - Korean student (College Essay Guy examples)

**Excerpt:**

> Growing up, I kept my head to the ground - literally. I frequently searched the soil, collecting interesting rocks... I continue to collect rocks to this day because I believe that valuable lessons can be extracted from them...
> "Dragon egg": ...we'd feed them magical soups (composed of shampoo, daisies, and glitter), which made the rocks sparkle in the sunshine. Out of sentimentality, I kept my dragon egg, because it reminds me never to lose my "sparkle" or creativity. I practice Minhwa, traditional Korean watercolor painting, with my mom...
> Cracked pavement: ...mirrors my love for tennis and sports, as both exemplify the beauty of resilience... I've learned that success is born from dedication and a willingness to find beauty in the journey, even amidst the cracks.

**Source:** Accepted-essay compilation. https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-examples

**Why it won:** A montage where each rock type is a chapter of identity: dragon egg = creativity (Minhwa with her mom), cracked pavement = resilience (tennis), sedimentary stones = diversity of self. The rock-taxonomy frame is the intellectual hook; culture appears through detail, not cliches; every section ends with an extracted lesson.

**Fits:** International or first-gen students who want culture to appear through objects and traditions rather than explicit identity statements.

## Patterns across winners: what ~80% of these essays share

1. **An unexpected hook in the first sentence.** Costco ("I charged"), the letter S, the math pop quiz, "But those people were wrong." None open with "I have always been passionate about..." (Brennan Barnard, NYT contributor: "Don't bury the lede.")

2. **One clear thread, never two.** Every winner is about exactly one thing - Costco, salt, pillows, stickers, rocks, the letter S - usually a physical object that returns in the final image ("I'll sleep on it," "create my own ending").

3. **Specificity over abstraction.** Winners name things ("thirty-three ounce jar of Nutella," "7-11 plastic Slurpee Cup") instead of declaring "I am curious."

4. **Vulnerability with distance.** The strongest essays touch real pain - death, addiction, war - but a device (a letter, a refrain, a service project, a grain of salt) keeps it from becoming a pity party.

5. **Growth shown, not claimed.** Each essay arcs: running from S to chasing Passion; "they were right" to "they were wrong" - demonstrated with named evidence, never stated.

6. **Intellectual vitality expressed through action.** The math student writes problems for peers; the MIT student reverse-engineers a Lassi; the UChicago student critiques her culture's gender norms. Passion is proven by doing.

7. **Voice sounds like a teenager, not a thesaurus.** "Blending smoothies is kinda like chemistry," "presto!," "I'll sleep on it."

8. **The ending answers the opening.** Nearly every essay bookends: shattering glass becomes multiplied perspectives; "S" becomes "Passion." The last line delivers the insight.

9. **No achievement lists.** Impressive facts (300 service hours, a 36-chapter book) are embedded in story, not enumerated.

10. **The risk pays off because the substance is real.** Quirky topics only work when the writer extracts a genuine insight. The quirk is the hook; the reflection is the win.

**Practical takeaway for this applicant (STEM, research, international, finance-curious):** the strongest fits are the Anthony JHU template (math passion proven through teaching and building), the MIT Victor model (quirky pleasure + honest failure + interdisciplinary why-major), and a montage thread (one real object carrying multiple selves). Pick one real thread and let every paragraph add a facet.
