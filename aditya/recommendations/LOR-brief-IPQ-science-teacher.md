## 3. My research, explained simply

### Investor attention and market volatility (corresponding author)
- **The question:** when everyone is watching the same stock, at what point does that attention make its price unstable?
- **What I found:** across 21 national markets over eleven years, volatility rises sharply once trading passes about 1.39 times its normal level. Below that, extra attention makes little difference.
- **Also found:**
  - Surges that follow attention in other countries stay volatile for longer.
  - Borsa Istanbul behaves differently from most markets and tracks the Turkish lira more closely.
- **Method:** trading volume is used as a measure of attention and cross-checked against Google searches and Wikipedia page views. The turning point can be found using past data only, so it could be used in real time.
- **Status:** went through full peer review at *Borsa Istanbul Review*, a Q1 journal (the top quartile in its field, where most authors are university researchers). After the reviewers' comments I restructured the paper and resubmitted it.

### Fantasy Premier League as a behavioural market (corresponding author)
- **The question:** do the millions of people playing Fantasy Premier League behave like investors in a real market?
- **What I found:** players reward luck over skill. Of two players with equal chances, the one who happened to score is picked by about 1.3 percentage points more managers the following week.
- **Method:** I treated player selection like stock trading, tested the main result on data the model hadn't seen, and set the tests before running them.
- **Status:** submitted to the *Journal of Sports Economics*.

### PhenoSync: crops and pollinators falling out of sync (sole author)
- **The question:** is climate change pulling crops and the bees that pollinate them apart in time?
- **What I found:** in North American blueberries, the gap between bloom and the main bumblebee pollinator is widening by about 4.57 days a year, and their overlap at the start of bloom has fallen roughly 99% since 2016.
- **Method:** I combined four large public datasets (NASA satellite data on plant growth, NOAA climate records, GBIF citizen-science sightings and FAO crop data). I also designed a new way of estimating when a species first appears each year, which was about 30 times more accurate than the usual method in simulation. Regions without enough data are reported as such.
- **Status:** entered in the Oxford Saïd Business School Climate Challenge; prepared for *Methods in Ecology and Evolution*.

### NEXUS Exchange (sole author)
- **What it is:** a simulated stock exchange where nine types of automated traders interact. I built it partly to test my attention finding from a completely different direction.
- **Status:** architecture paper published on SSRN.

## 5. The IPQ

*"To what extent does high-frequency algorithmic trading destabilise financial markets during periods of stress?"* It compares five crises: the 2010 Flash Crash, Knight Capital (2012), the 2014 Treasury rally, China's 2016 circuit breakers and the COVID-19 sell-off.

**Points you may find useful:**
- I chose a question that asks for a judgement, rather than one that assumed the answer.
- I picked five crises that each worked through a different mechanism, including cases that went against my own initial view.
- My conclusion was not a simple yes or no: high-frequency trading tended to amplify some crises once they had started, rather than cause them, and its role depended on the market.
- I weighed official investigations, academic studies and my own data against each other, and noted where sources had limitations or a stake in the outcome.
- I wrote the report so that a reader without a finance background could follow it.

> *Example:* "Aditya's IPQ reached a careful, qualified conclusion rather than an easy one. He separated what started each crisis from what made it worse, and he was clear about what his evidence could and could not show."
