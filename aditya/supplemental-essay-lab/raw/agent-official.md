### Johns Hopkins #1 — The Secret Ingredient is Connection (Common App personal statement, ~650 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim if available):** JHU does not publish a prompt for these selections; the page describes them as essays where students “share stories from their everyday lives to reveal something about their character, values, and life.” Treated here as the Common App personal statement.
- **Source:** https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/the-secret-ingredient-is-connection/

> An authoritative voice bellows my name in the crowded school cafeteria: “MATZ!” My freshman year track coach advances towards me, each stride squashing surrounding chatter and closing the distance between us. I know exactly what awaits me.
>
> “Remember,” he says, standing in front of me with eyes fixed on mine, “the lemon squares.” He adds with a grin, “And could you put a smiley face on top?”
>
> Wanting his birthday treat to be memorable, I make a note to pick up raspberry jam for drawing the image.
>
> I am known as “The Baker” by countless teachers and peers. Throughout high school, I have shared hundreds of my homemade delights for special days and ordinary days, individuals and groups, and friends and strangers. Baking is incredibly meaningful to me as a way to strengthen my relationships with myself, others, and cultures around the world.
>
> In the kitchen, I transform from student into both chemical engineer and principal investigator. Each creation involves a dance of chemistry on a macro and micro scale; I pour in a bit of extra flour and knead, knowing it will strengthen the dough, and I ponder the reactions that will caramelize its sugars. Following recipes suffices, for it allows me to practice technique. It is winter, so I am making a classic: sugar cookies. I want my cookies to be homogenous, so I beat the butter and sugar vigorously. But my curiosity often takes over. An enthusiast of using creativity, innovation, and sugar in equal parts, I decide to close the recipe book. I form the dough into large, rectangular sheets and do some trigonometry in my head to determine their measurements. Once the cookies emerge from the oven, I arrange them meticulously into a 3D structure using icing as glue. After adding Hershey’s chocolate bar windows and powdered sugar snow, the sugar cookie house is complete and sure to wow at the school holiday bake-off!
>
> However, not all my experiments turn out this way. I chuckle as I recall failed explorations of yore: granulated sugar icing that tasted like sand and improperly leavened sourdough that required a saw to slice. Successful or not, each hour spent as a scientist provides fun and new knowledge.
>
> From my experience, I can ascertain that there is a strong, positive correlation between the promise of a baked surprise and club meeting attendance. Thus, after removing cornbread from the oven, I note on the agenda for tomorrow’s Tri-M Music Honor Society meeting: “Treats!” However, my baking is far from over. I find a clean whisk and start on a second batch, although this time, it is vegan. Butter and oil trade places. Eggs give way to their flaxy brethren. The batch is smaller—I know my dietary demographics—but rich in care. Though not fortune cookies, I intend for each sweet to contain a message: Welcome. Join us. I made these for you.
>
> In every mixing bowl lies a ticket to faraway lands. Seizing this opportunity, a friend and I chart a voyage around the globe. We first travel two thousand miles to Mexico where he shows me how to make polvorones, a traditional cookie served during weddings and holidays. Feeling inspired, I fly us across the Atlantic Ocean to Israel. There, I teach him how to braid babka, forming beautiful, overlapping connections between lengths of dough and between my Jewish heritage and me. When ready to return home, we simply leave the kitchen.
>
> I knock on the classroom door, my smile mirroring the one made of jam on the lemony surprise I hold in my hands. My former track coach invites me in, grabbing a couple of chairs and happily accepting his birthday gift. Even sweeter than the lemon squares is the opportunity they provide; together, we reminisce about the past and discuss future plans, catching up after all this time.
>
> The secret ingredient in each baked good I make is connection.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> David’s essay uses the lens of baking to highlight his commitment to excellence, community-building, and a solutions-oriented mindset. This essay is strong because it shows the admissions committee how David incorporates his passion into his extracurricular activities and connects with others.
>
> David discusses what it looks like for him to try something new by abandoning a recipe and turning to his own sense of innovation to make the perfect dessert to share with his team. By connecting both the art and science of baking with his personal life, activities, and core values, David’s essay paints a picture of what’s important to him. This allowed us to envision him as a key member of our campus community.

> “The most important piece of advice I have for those working on this kind of essay is to be vulnerable and authentic. If you like making jokes, let it show. If there’s something really niche that is important to you, be sure to talk about it. Be personal with anecdotes and a strong sense of voice and show the admissions committee who you really are rather than trying to conform to what you think they want. This will not only make you stand out more but also ensure that the place you end up at truly aligns with your interests.”
>
> — David M., ’30

**Technique bullets:**

- One ordinary hobby carries the whole essay; the writer never needs a second topic.
- Kitchen vocabulary (caramelize, homogenize, trigonometry) shows a scientific mind without stating 'I am a scientist.'
- Service is folded into the hobby (a vegan batch, a 'Welcome. Join us.' message), so values appear as actions.
- The final line re-states the title as a thesis, closing the loop cleanly.

### Johns Hopkins #2 — Developing Roots (Common App personal statement, ~568 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim if available):** JHU does not publish a prompt for these selections; the page describes them as essays where students “share stories from their everyday lives to reveal something about their character, values, and life.” Treated here as the Common App personal statement.
- **Source:** https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/developing-roots/

> Flipping over rocks under the imposing maple tree in my front yard, I was looking for two things: roly pollies to stash in my Roly-Poly Playground and earthworms to feed the forever hungry chickens roaming my backyard. Looking at these invertebrates that could easily fit in the palm of my hand, made me feel almost as large, strong, and invincible as the maple tree looming above me. But a maple tree isn’t the strongest, largest, or most resilient tree that exists; I wanted to be a Giant Sequoia.
>
> If maple trees seemed to touch the clouds, Sequoiadendron giganteum trees seemed to enter the mesosphere. Not only are they larger than any other tree species, but they have also evolved a thick, fire-resistant bark allowing growth to continue even after a ravaging fire in their ecosystem. In math and science, I am and always have been a Giant Sequoia, able to withstand countless derivatives, integrals, organelles, and principles blowing my way. In humanities classes, subjectivity covers all three hundred feet of my trunk and limbs in Pueraria, and my likeness to a sequoia is proven through the resilience needed to strip the vines of uncertainty off and see the beauty of the tree beneath.
>
> Being in high school, I often feel like a cottonwood with a pliable, shallow root system that hasn’t yet evolved to withstand the storm that is life. I am held upright largely due to the interconnection of my root systems with like-minded peers and friends I have found through orchestra, classes, and school clubs. However strong the power of community is, in college I long to develop a deep root system of both knowledge and self-sufficiency that can keep me upright, regardless of what happens to the connections that, right now, are crucial to my survival.
>
> The Castanea dentata, the American Chestnut, taught me to value individualism through the permeation of a fungus that ran rampant and eliminated most of the formerly populous species in the United States. Being a lone tree can limit the stresses of altruism, having each effort contribute to just myself rather than spreading through a group, making it incredibly easy to exist as a lone tree in class, absorbing nutrients accumulating ring after ring of knowledge. However, if the Giant Sequoia’s sprawling, intertwined root systems taught me anything, it was that collaboration, feedback, support, and the melding of ideas allow my community and I to have a strong foundation that can resist even the strongest of winds. I am seeking to integrate into an environment in which I can continue to live in that delicate balance between my community and individual development.
>
> While I may aspire to be like a Giant Sequoia, my confidence more closely resembles a Fraxinus tree, of whom millions have been slain by tiny Agrilus planipennis, better known as Emerald ash borers. The strong scores that made me rise to the top in high school should imprint a sense of confidence, but confidence is easily eroded by self-doubt and a focus on the tiny mistakes that remain and drive me to keep improving myself. Only by acknowledging such vulnerabilities and working within a community of friends and mentors can I improve upon them and grow from a sapling filled with hope and potential to become the well balanced contributor to my communities that the seedling who stared at the maple leaves looming above longed to be.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> In her essay, Etina uses her knowledge and passion for trees to signify community, resilience, and putting down roots. The tree metaphor effectively communicates a student who thinks across disciplines in a reflective way. We also see a thoughtful balance between community and individual growth, which suggests an understanding of the kind of learning environment where she would thrive.
>
> From her essay, we see a student who values belonging, mutual support, and shared growth, and we can picture how she would enrich our Blue Jay community. The essay is especially effective when it uses the natural world to reveal something personal about confidence, development, and the process of becoming a more grounded, independent learner.

> “The hardest part of writing my personal statement was writing about myself; everything I typed felt disingenuous and lacking in context. To combat this, I explored interests of mine that weren’t apparent in my application in hopes that it would bring an approach that felt more genuine. I landed on trees and used each species to highlight a characteristic of myself. By eliminating the expectations of what I thought a personal statement should be, I was able to overcome the hurdle of starting and represent myself genuinely.”
>
> — Etina R., ’30

**Technique bullets:**

- A single extended metaphor is systematically mapped: one tree species per personal quality.
- The writer includes weaker species (cottonwood, ash borer) to admit self-doubt rather than perform confidence.
- Technical botany names signal genuine curiosity and cross-disciplinary thinking.
- The close converts the metaphor into a stated college-fit need: community plus independence.

### Johns Hopkins #3 — The Two Sides of America (Common App personal statement, ~648 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim if available):** JHU does not publish a prompt for these selections; the page describes them as essays where students “share stories from their everyday lives to reveal something about their character, values, and life.” Treated here as the Common App personal statement.
- **Source:** https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/the-two-sides-of-america/

> Lucedale’s population is about 3,000. Its claim to fame is the Rocky Creek Catfish Cottage, the best place to get fried catfish in all of Eastern Mississippi. The residents have still not entirely mastered the four-way stop sign that was installed five years ago. There is little to no cell service, and the only place to shop is Walmart. Lucedale is the antithesis of Chicago. But still, it is my home away from home. The red clay from the dirt roads has seeped into my blood; I will forever be connected to the rural farmland of Mississippi.
>
> My first summer job on the farm was simple: pick up the pinecones that fell from the giant pine by the horse pasture. They were notorious for getting stuck in the lawn mower, so PawPaw made me a deal: five dollars if I cleared them. I spent my days picking up pinecones. No matter how many I picked up, more would fall. By the end of the summer there were still countless pinecones on the ground, but PawPaw still gave me that five dollar bill.
>
> As I got older, picking up pinecones turned to cleaning out the horse stalls turned to bush hogging. The summer that I turned 14, my brother put me on the John Deere and left. I was terrified. Driving the tractor is a major responsibility– it is easy to break and harder to fix. But, I turned it on and went back and forth in straight lines, cutting the thick, rye grass of the horse pasture.
>
> When I was 16, I was finally old enough to work with the cows. I became an employee of SPH Cattle Co., my family’s cattle company. I learned how to herd the cows into the catch pen, which rope tightens the gate and which loosens it. The calves need all of their immunizations, the older ones only need some, and they all need to be sprayed to prevent flies. It’s a process that repeats about 50 times until all of the cows have been pushed through the pen. The work started off as a burden, something that I thought no 16-year-old girl should endure, but by the end of the summer, I found joy in it.
>
> Even 900 miles away, Mississippi is still a part of me; the red dirt, and the lessons, will never leave my blood. Yes, I learned about responsibility and hard work. But the most important thing my summers on the farm gave me? Perspective.
>
> I have listened to people complain at a steakhouse serving $75 ribeyes, but I’ve also experienced the hard work that goes into raising cattle. I call myself a city kid; I have grown up living in high rises, attended private schools. Yet, I have spent my summers in a farmhouse, working in the mud, trading in a golf club for a shovel. I have seen the beauty in rural life. What I value and what I believe comes from this unique perspective that I’m privileged to hold. It’s easy for my Chicago friends to write off rural life, to confine farmers to a stereotype: simple and unaware. My Lucedale neighbors think of folks in Chicago as crazy radicals. I’m unable to make these assumptions. I see the kindness and morality of urban folks. I don’t agree with everything they believe, but that does not make me respect them any less. And I also have so much respect for people from rural communities. They are some of the kindest people I’ve ever met, willing to give the shirts off their own backs to a stranger. My worldview, what I value and what I believe, comes from this rare viewpoint. The beauty of our country, what makes it so great, is these two contradicting sides. And I know first-hand that we wouldn’t survive without both of them, no matter how different they might seem.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> Gillian draws on her summers in Lucedale to show resilience, responsibility, and a deep appreciation for her community in all its forms. She provides details of farm life like tending cattle and learning from family, creating a vivid picture of a student who understands the value of labor, stewardship, and interdependence.
>
> What stands out most is her ability to move beyond stereotypes and recognize the dignity in both rural and urban communities. Her experience living between Chicago and Mississippi has shaped her into someone who can connect with people from different backgrounds and value the strengths each community offers. This is the kind of student who would bring meaningful perspective to our campus because she understands how community is built through respect, empathy, and shared responsibility.

> “When I first started drafting my personal statement, I knew that I wanted to write about Lucedale. I felt like it showed a side of me, and who I am, that was not visible within the rest of my application. Spending my summers in Mississippi has truly shaped how I view the world and the people around me. It has given me a unique perspective that I wasn’t able to express through the rest of my application.”
>
> — Gillian H., ’30

**Technique bullets:**

- Place is established with unsentimental specifics (population, catfish shack, four-way stop).
- A skill ladder (pinecones to stalls to bush hogging to cattle) shows growth through escalating responsibility.
- The essay refuses to flatter either community, which makes the perspective claim credible.
- The conclusion turns a personal vantage point into a civic argument about interdependence.

### Johns Hopkins #4 — Clean Closet (Common App personal statement, ~642 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim if available):** JHU does not publish a prompt for these selections; the page describes them as essays where students “share stories from their everyday lives to reveal something about their character, values, and life.” Treated here as the Common App personal statement.
- **Source:** https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/clean-closet/

> My to-do list always had one unfinished task. No matter how much time passed, I could never check off “clean closet.”
>
> My closet is a time capsule going on eighteen years. It’s packed with favorite toys, plush animals, retired gymnastics leotards, and grandma-sewn dresses that once decorated my childhood. Shelves are adorned with art projects I once was so proud to showcase and yearbooks scribbled with notes from teachers and friends. The rest includes attempts to discover personal style, cheerleading uniforms, and several memory boxes filled to the brim. The closet holds who I was and who I’ve tried to be.
>
> Every attempt to clean became an excavation. Picking up Littlest Pet Shop figures brought back late nights spent creating storylines. Canvases following painting tutorials showed attempts at being worry-free like Bob Ross. Trophies and medals reminded me of how I learned to flip, terrified and exhilarated at once. Every corner echoed a moment that shaped me.
>
> As I started high school, my collection of memory boxes did too. Boxes with charming designs hold every award, letter, trinket, and photo booth strip. Another box is dedicated to flowers from friends and family that I dried. My cheer bag holds well-used pom poms and cheer shoes. All of them remind me of people I learned from and laughed with. Getting rid of them would feel bittersweet. These feelings were exactly why I could never bring myself to clean my closet before. To accommodate the future, I had to do it. One Saturday morning this spring, I sat cross-legged surrounded by piles and committed to completing the task. For years, I always put everything back in the usual place, too sentimental to let anything go. This time, I set aside what I no longer needed in a donation pile, figuring the clothes, books, and toys could benefit others rather than remain stagnant in my closet. I cleared the shelf of childhood books and old art to make space for nail supplies and more memory boxes. It felt weird. But the longer I sat, the more I valued moving toward the future. I can donate books while remembering what made me love them. I can recycle trophies without forgetting my hard work. I don’t need objects to know I’m loved. I still adore and appreciate the sentimental, but I’ve come to see it as a reminder to cherish growth rather than dwell on the past. The memory boxes are my reminders. The things I’ve let go of have grown with me: painting became nail art, gymnastics became cheer, creating storylines became exploring people.
>
> Since then, I’ve acted on that mindset beyond my closet. I stopped constantly worrying about the past and overthinking interactions and choices. Instead, I began saying yes to things that were previously met with hesitation. The same sentimentality I reserved for objects spread to people, and I developed a love for talking to those I once just observed. I started pouring into my nail hobby and volunteering at the hospital. Through these, I get to know clients, patients, and children. I use my love for art and design to reflect personalities on fingers. I celebrate in the rehab ward when patients are going home. I teach kids to be creative and kind. Though they don’t contribute physical objects to my memory boxes, I treasure their smiles. These moments remind me that my childhood self, who loved stories and color, is still within me.
>
> My closet is not spotless. It grows and cycles out, just like life. As I step into new chapters, new memory boxes will start for college, new people, and new milestones. And with the upcoming growth, I’ll have to leave some things behind; the difference is I now recognize that letting go is part of growing.
>
> The next time I’m tasked to “clean closet,” it won’t take eighteen years.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> Ruoni turns an ordinary task into a meaningful reflection on identity, memory, and learning how to let go without losing what matters. Her essay includes vivid and specific details that make the story feel authentic, and it shows change through action: donating old belongings, making space for new interests, and becoming more open to people and experiences.
>
> Her essay highlights her creativity, empathy, and growth through service. Ruoni also showcases something we value at Hopkins: a culture of discovery. Cleaning the closet shows her discovering who she is and what she values, which mirrors the kind of growth Hopkins supports through close relationships, residential life, and hands-on opportunities.
>
> Overall, the essay presents a student who is ready to explore and keep evolving in a place like Hopkins.

> “My application lists how I spent my time: playing in my orchestra, leading my cheer team, volunteering at the hospital, and working with children. In my essay on dissecting my closet, I wanted to illustrate my character and love for preserving memories through short and sweet anecdotes. These small details fill the gaps left in my application with a list of just ten things. I hope my essay gave admissions counselors a better understanding of the person behind my activities list—what I value, how I think, and my approach to life.”
>
> — Ruoni T., ’30

**Technique bullets:**

- A mundane unfinished chore supplies a ready-made structure: attempt, excavation, decision, aftermath.
- Objects are catalogued as evidence of former selves, so nostalgia never floats free of detail.
- Change is proven by action (a donation pile) rather than asserted.
- The last line undercuts sentiment with a joke, keeping the ending light.

### Johns Hopkins #5 — In the Belly of the Ship (Common App personal statement, ~650 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim if available):** JHU does not publish a prompt for these selections; the page describes them as essays where students “share stories from their everyday lives to reveal something about their character, values, and life.” Treated here as the Common App personal statement.
- **Source:** https://apply.jhu.edu/hopkins-insider/in-the-belly-of-the-ship/

> I lay on the bamboo mat in a pool of my own sweat, swaying with the ship. A net around my cot attempted to keep out the mosquitos, and the room’s thin walls leaked every crewman’s cough. Yet as I looked outside the porthole, the greatest discomfort was my own shame.
>
> Growing up in a coastal city, the sea carried a call to action. My father, once a cargo crewman and now a shipping administrator, shared tales of free-spirited voyages and world experience; yet I was most drawn to his depictions of close-knit crews. I was someone who hesitated in small social moments: lowering my eyes in the hallway, uncertain if a wave was meant for me, never taking that initial step. The ship seemed to provide what everyday life lacked: a space where closeness was facilitated.
>
> But when I interned aboard a similar vessel in summer 2024, I quickly discovered the details my dad had left out. My excitement melted under the 100°F sun. There weren’t assigned tasks to distract myself with, and both my journal and copy of Moby-Dick I had brought aboard became nothing more than paper weights.
>
> Worse, I didn’t perceive the camaraderie my father had described. The crew kept to their shifts, mostly isolated in the rooms, caring only that “the ship keeps moving.” The sailors spoke in a provincial dialect I could barely follow, and my father had advised me to conceal my background (as an international school student and administrator’s daughter) for fear it would alienate me from the crew. I tried to join conversations, but with no relatable experience and only fragments of understanding, my words fell short. All I felt I could do was lie flat in my cot and wait for morning.
>
> When I disembarked a week later, my relief was as overwhelming as the heat had been. Yet with time, what returned to me most was the sailors’ faces, carrying stories I had never uncovered.
>
> Which is why, the next summer, I stubbornly boarded again. I carried unease, knowing how physically and emotionally draining the prior voyage had been; but I also carried determination to resist that urge to retreat into my cabin. When I boarded, the engineer (the rare woman aboard) looked at me with surprise and spoke in Mandarin instead of dialect: “most never come back. They don’t appreciate the routine.”
>
> Routine. I became determined to find a routine that wasn’t “hide from the heat.” I volunteered to help: rust-chipping the deck, double-checking the cargo tallies, and washing vegetables. The more I leaned into the routine, the more it carried me. Small things I had ignored before became luminous: hammocks stretched beneath the night sky, chuckles exchanged over bowls of noodles.
>
> With little windows of opening, nods of appreciation or smiles of hello, I took small steps to unearth their experience, not allowing dialect (or a ‘blacklist’ of what truths I should withhold) inhibit me. When I led with authenticity, previous obstacles fell to the wayside. In return, I received incredible stories. Not glamorous sea adventures, but honest experiences from people of all walks of life, all of which were documented in my seafarer’s journal. Everything from a middle-aged couple who decades ago had gone deep into debt (and spent 30 years at sea repaying it), to a young captain sailing from loneliness. Soon, my observations transformed into responsibility; after months of policy research, interviews, and sample collection regarding the crew’s lack of treated water, I led water safety sessions and successfully petitioned the department of transportation for new regulations regarding small vessels’ right to clean water.
>
> My contributions were made possible by that crucible at sea, forged in sweat, silence, and routine: the value of small steps, the power of camaraderie, and the fulfillment of persevering through failure. I’m proud to have been eaten alive by a metal beast, and then entered its jaws once more.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> Ruoyu’s essay demonstrates great strength and perseverance through time spent interning at sea. Through this experience, Ruoyu notes that she wants to make her own impact instead of following in the footsteps of the people before her or blending in with the rest of the crew. She tries something new that didn’t live up to expectations on the first try but still has the courage to return. This shows the admissions committee her determination and persistence, dedication to excellence through research, and how she found community in unexpected places—all qualities that make a strong Hopkins student.

> “I chose this topic because it steps outside the usual boundaries of my student life, and that choice made the end the hardest part to write. I wrote multiple versions of that final paragraph over months because I found it difficult to determine what meaning (if any) should be used to define my experience. I was writing before I’d had the distance to know what my story meant. In the end, the gap between a sincere experience and a sincere piece of writing is simply the quiet work of time.”
>
> — Ruoyu X., ’30

**Technique bullets:**

- Physical discomfort is rendered concretely (sweat, mosquitoes, 100-degree sun) before any lesson is named.
- The return voyage is the argument: persistence is demonstrated, not claimed.
- The writer admits shame and failure of nerve, which makes the later courage believable.
- The essay widens from self to advocacy (water-safety sessions, a petition), showing impact beyond reflection.

### Connecticut College #1 — Standing up for your beliefs (Common App personal statement — Conn page label: “Standing up for your beliefs”, ~635 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim if available):** No prompt text is published with this essay; the heading above is Connecticut College's own label for the piece. Treated as a Common App personal statement.
- **Source:** https://www.conncoll.edu/admission/apply/essays-that-worked/anonymous/

> What do you get when you combine a hypodermic needle, international code, clay, an early mainframe computer, and the national budget? In a sense, you get me. All of these things are connected because they are each related to the jobs of the women in my family, all of whom have influenced who I am. My mom is a neuroscientist, my grammy worked for the NSA as a decoder, my nana is an artist, my great aunt was an early computer scientist, and my aunt is a budget analyst for the US Department of the Interior. Throughout my life I have listened to their stories and I have watched them devote countless hours to their fields. I have always wanted to be as driven and successful as these women.
>
> I have also always wanted to be my own person. Every kid gets asked “what do you want to be when you grow up?” For me, the answer has always been the same: a lawyer. I used to not know exactly why, but as I’ve gotten older, that answer has come into focus. I am interested in how people reason through things, I am determined, and I want to be in the conversation. I like to stand up for (and with) people who don’t always have a voice.
>
> Maybe unsurprisingly, history classes are important to me because we discuss past events and current politics. I ask questions and I challenge ideas. For example, last November my APUSH teacher asked “what are the problems with America?” People said things like poverty, education, the environment. No one really disagreed about anything until one student raised his hand and said, “abortion: thousands of Americans are slaughtered every day.” Knowing me well enough, the class turned in my direction and maybe some people could see that before even being called on I was preparing to respond. Internally, I was thinking over the statistics, stories, and challenges that I knew regarding women's reproductive rights. As I spoke, pretty quickly the teacher noticed that this might be more than just an in-class discussion. Instead of letting the argument unfold, he scheduled a formal debate. I began to prepare. More than just wanting to win, I felt responsible for making a compelling case. I dug in. I studied the arguments, I poured over the research and I practiced articulating my ideas.
>
> As the debate approached, people asked if I was ready and if they could be part of the audience. Other students skipped their own classes to attend. On the day of the debate, I took my seat in the front of the room and watched the rows fill with students. The room became far too crowded for its 25-seat capacity; some had to stand against the back wall. I was excited, but also had to push through a bit of nervousness. As the discussion unfolded, I gained confidence and became more assertive, bolstered by the strength of my argument and the depth of my research. In the end the audience voted for the winner and it was me. I won, overwhelmingly! I wanted this outcome, but it wasn’t like winning a game. It felt like my reasoning and research had convinced people, and had changed some minds. It made me feel like my voice could, and maybe did, make a difference.
>
> I care about important issues—one of them being women's rights and equality. I have always looked up to women who use their minds and who aim to make life better for others, whether that was when I was a little kid and my favorite Disney character was Merida, or now, still being inspired by my mother. I want to continue the generations of women in my family that do something meaningful, and are proud that what they do makes a difference.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> “Why It Worked” — Every essay we receive is read by our admission counselors. Here's what they said about this student’s:
>
> The first remark really drew us in: We were curious to find out how all of those seemingly unrelated things were connected! This set-up was also a fascinating way to share how the women in the student’s family provided the drive and values that would come into play in a debate and a possible career. In that moment, I knew my teammates had my back, even if I did not always completely comprehend their humor. Over time, the team grew into a tight-knit group of friends who fit together like a puzzle. James can break a bad round with a laugh, Matt gives the best pep talks, and Drew is reliable for sound shot advice, while my niche emerged as bringing positivity and optimism after a bad shot. This team dynamic continued in school as well, as James comes to me after a bad test, while I see Matt before a big presentation. Whether we are on or off the course, we help each other to succeed.
>
> As the daughter of two teachers, country club simulators and memberships to the area’s elite courses were not options for me. Two summers ago, I took matters into my own hands and got a job cleaning out dirty carts and taking out the trash at the local country club. Scrubbing the spilled adult beverages out of the cup holders and disposing of the deteriorating cigars was not how I pictured spending my summers, but was valuable for the free rounds I played. By the end of the summer, I realized my hard work leveled the playing field between myself and my more affluent opponents.
>
> This gentleman’s sport has become such a significant part of my life. The amount of joy I receive from sinking a lengthy putt or driving my ball straight down the center of the fairway reminds me just how grateful I am to play this sport. My sister might still dance in the parking lot after we play a round of mini-golf; I will join her, because I know that I will continue to play golf, and learn from the game, for the rest of my life.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**

> “Why It Worked” — Every essay we receive is read by our admission counselors. Here's what they said about this student’s:
>
> Riley's essay grabbed our attention through her strong use of imagery in the first paragraph. Riley shows many different sides of her personality in this essay—her goofy side has fun playing mini-golf with her sister, her ambitious side sought out female role models and access to the country club, and her optimistic side encourages her teammates. Riley maintained a single narrative throughout the essay and spoke in detail about her passion, which is why this essay works.

**Technique bullets:**

- A comic mini-golf failure opens the essay and quietly sets up the golf-team stakes.
- Class difference is handled through concrete labor (cleaning carts, free rounds), not complaint.
- Teammate sketches show belonging through small, specific exchanges.
- The sister returns in the last line, tying the frame shut.

### University of Georgia #1 — Rocky Horror, Out of My Comfort Zone (UGA short essay, ~334 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** “The college admissions process can create anxiety. In an attempt to make it less stressful, please tell us an interesting or amusing story about yourself from your high school years that you have not already shared in your application.”
- **Source:** https://admissions.uga.edu/blog/2023-application-essays/

> I am a very basic person, at least when it comes to my personal appearance. If you run across me any day of the week you’ll find I’m usually in a t-shirt, leggings, and some sneakers. That wasn’t the case one Friday night last fall. My outfit consisted of fishnet stockings and Doc Martens, a t-shirt so big I wore it as a dress, a comically large pearl necklace, and bright pink rubber gloves that went up to my elbows. I was way out of my fashion comfort zone.
>
> My friend Emma had convinced me to go see a screening of The Rocky Horror Picture Show with her, and I decided to defy my usual caution and go all in on the experience. I listened to the movie soundtrack beforehand and spent weeks planning my costume: a toned-down version of Dr. Frank N. Furter.
>
> Emma is my opposite in many ways. She loves horror movies and classic rock, wears all black, and chops off her hair every couple of weeks. Where I’m socially anxious and worried about how others might perceive me, she is bold and unforgiving and a fierce protector of her family and friends. We all need friends like Emma who can challenge our assumptions and help us overcome our fears. And who spark us to create some great memories.
>
> The screening was being held at Ciné, a local Athens art house theater, and there were butterflies in my stomach at the thought of venturing downtown in my get-up. As Emma and I walked into the theater, no one spared us a second glance. But we were clearly the coolest kids in attendance. Well, we were the only “kids” in attendance, the only high schoolers there in an audience made up mostly of people who looked well over thirty. Nevertheless, we danced in the aisle, shouted at the screen and had a blast. I’m saving that costume and will be ready to do the “Time Warp” again and again.  – Ella J.

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**
> Why is this essay strong? It is personal, it gives details and emotions, and it gives insight into the person. It also well written, shows that the student is able to stretch out beyond her comfort zone, and that she is not afraid to grow and learn. And lastly, I now know a great deal more about the student. When you write your essays, let us know more about you and less about the event (The Rocky Horror Picture Show in this case), as it lets us better understand you as an individual.

**Technique bullets:**
- A costume inventory does the descriptive work in four sentences, with no throat-clearing.
- The friend is a foil, not a prop: her contrast defines the narrator's caution.
- The stakes stay small and social, which suits a 300-word prompt about reducing anxiety.
- The closing line points forward (keeping the costume), turning a one-night story into a trait.

### Dartmouth College #1 — Lines from a “Why Dartmouth” Essay (supplemental “Why us,” EXCERPT, ~96 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** “Why do you want to attend Dartmouth College? Why us?” (author's paraphrase: the standard supplemental “Why Us?” essay, “100 words” per her account)
- **Source:** https://admissions.dartmouth.edu/follow/blog/sarah-hedgecock/reflecting-my-why-dartmouth-essay
- **Status:** EXCERPT — the author quotes only these lines from her submitted essay while annotating them; the full essay is not published.

> “I wrap up my class, GOVT 7.14: Does Democracy Work?, and head to a meeting with my SIB.”
>
> “The D-Plan gives me flexibility for world-class research and internships such as the First Year Fellows while being supported by a collaborative student community and receiving guidance through the FGO and DCCD.”
>
> “I grow my leadership skills through the D-Lab and the DOC.”
>
> “I spend my weekends exploring the Upper Valley, skiing at the Skiway Lodge, and rowing along the Connecticut River.”
>
> “Dartmouth's vast opportunities push me to explore my passions and maximize my impact on the world.”

**Published commentary (verbatim, if any):**
> It seemed like an impossible feat to capture all the reasons I wanted to attend Dartmouth in 100 words. Looking back, I'm a little shocked at how little I was able to represent what has ended up being most impactful to me during my first year at Dartmouth.

> If I were to write my "Why Us?" essay again, I would write about sledding at Pine Park and piling thirty people into a common room for a Super Bowl Watch Party.

> But you can't write about these things before you know them.

**Technique bullets:**
- The strongest lines name checkable specifics (GOVT 7.14, D-Plan, FGO, DCCD, D-Lab, DOC), which is what a “Why us” essay is graded on.
- The author later audits her own essay and admits plans changed, showing the genre rewards honest present-tense curiosity over certainty.
- Short, declarative sentences make a 100-word limit feel deliberate rather than truncated.
