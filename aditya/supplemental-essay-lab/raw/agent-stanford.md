# Stanford University — Accepted Supplemental Essays (Raw Vault)

**Scope:** Stanford supplemental only (roommate letter, intellectual vitality, "what matters to you and why", distinctive contribution, short answers, "idea that excites you"). Common App personal statement excluded.

**Exclusion note:** The 12 Stanford essays in `08-accepted-supplement-database.md` (CollegeAdvisor: face-shopping, Indefinita, Tom, Arial/Peach Snapple, Mahler/Terman, Tupac, love-as-harmony; CollegeVine: Sara, Yara, K-pop, 1984, instant ramen) are deliberately NOT repeated here. Every essay below is new to the vault.

**Provenance caveat:** Items marked ADMIT are published as essays from students admitted to Stanford. Items published without an explicit admit claim are marked EXAMPLE (published as a successful/strong sample by the source). Prompts from 2014–2020 cycles are given verbatim as published at the time; wording has since changed.

## Part A — Long-form essays (100–250 words)

### Stanford #1 — Roommate: "I have a habit of pinching ear lobes" / shelves (Roommate letter, ~361 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate-and us-know you better.*
- **Source:** https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/stanford-roommate-essay
- **Status:** ADMIT ("And for what it's worth, she did get into Stanford.")

> Everybody has peculiarities that most people don't know about. For example, I have a habit of pinching ear lobes. I also pour milk into my cereal, only to drain it out after soaking the cereal for a bit. Is that strange? Well, there's more:
>
> I have -2.75 vision but I hate wearing glasses because I feel confined and limited in my freedom to think. So you'll see me squint quite often, trying to overcome my astigmatism–it's not a death glare, I promise.
>
> I'm also extremely tactile. I like to run my fingers over laser printing because I am amazed by my fingers' ability to detect subtle impressions. This is why I hate wearing socks on carpet: my feet lose sensitivity. So I hope you don't mind bare feet.
>
> I have a fetish for things that smell nice, so I like to bury myself under fresh laundry just wheeled back from laundry room 8 (the one closest to our unit). I also alternate between three different shampoos just for the smell of it. So don't be surprised if I ask to share our toiletry items; I'm just looking for variety.
>
> Driving calms my nerves. Sometimes, my family and I go on midnight highway cruises during which we discuss weighty issues such as the reason people in our society can so adamantly advertise items like Snuggies. So I apologize if I keep you up late at night asking you to ponder the complex mysteries of our world.
>
> Also, in my home, we have an open door policy–literally. Every door, excluding those of an occupied bathroom and the fridge, is always open. I hope you and I will be comfortable enough with each other–and with those around us–that we feel no need to hide behind bedroom doors.
>
> Finally, I love shelves. They organize many different items under a unified structure and I find value in this kind of integrated diversity. And I love them as a metaphor: there is a place for everything, including even the quirkiest of our traits. That's why no one should feel left out no matter how strange or odd they might think they are.
>
> So, what are you like?

**Expert commentary (verbatim, College Essay Guy):**
> I learn so much about the writer. I learn (in order, by paragraph) that she: is confident enough to admit she's a little weird / values her freedom to think / is observant and sensitive to life's small details / is great with wordplay / is ironic and self-deprecating even while pondering life's mysteries / is willing to be emotionally open / values making order from chaos (AND she's smart enough to write an essay that *actually* creates order out of chaos–so her form matches her content, which is kinda next level)

> She began with **chaos.** She brainstormed a list of 21 random details about herself using this 21 details brainstorming exercise. Then she created **order**. She organized the details into paragraphs by theme. She found, in other words, a way to connect the random facts–to put them on different "shelves" (each "shelf" = one paragraph).

**Technique bullets:**
- Opens by normalizing weirdness ("Everybody has peculiarities"), which buys permission for every subsequent quirk.
- Each paragraph is thematically "shelved" so eight unrelated facts read as one coherent personality rather than a list.
- Closes by converting a physical object (shelves) into her thesis about inclusion — form mirrors content.
- Ends with a question, turning a monologue into an actual note to a person.

### Stanford #2 — Roommate: "Infinity+1 versions of me" (Roommate letter, ~248 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate-and us-know you better.*
- **Source:** https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/stanford-roommate-essay
- **Status:** ADMIT (published as a Stanford-attending example)

> Hi roomie!
>
> Ever wonder how many versions of yourself exist in people's minds? My take—there's an infinite number, and I hope you get to meet infinity+1 versions of me.
>
> Here's a head start:
>
> One version of me is extroverted, moving excitedly from topic to topic in social situations. Another's introverted, preferring warm, solitary nights curled up with a book at home. Together, they form my ideal studying environment: a cozy cafe, surrounded by iced coffee and friends. Maybe we can recreate this at Coupa Cafe, adding your unique study habits as we tackle freshman year. Will you be the matcha to my iced latte?
>
> There's also adventurous me, always up for a roller coaster or bungee jumping. I'm hoping we'll have quite a few Great America weekend trips.
>
> All versions are made instantly happier with a breath of fresh air, through brisk walks or long bike rides. I can't wait to represent Redmond—bike capital of the PNW—while exploring Palo Alto's trails together.
>
> There's "listener Ananya"—endlessly curious about your day and interests. I hope I'll become someone you feel comfortable talking to during midday hangouts at Meyer Green or into the wee hours.
>
> And without fail, you'll see goodnight-ritual Ananya: each night, I say goodnight to my friends and family, starting with my best friend in DC and my sister in St. Louis, ending with my parents and Alexa. As you replace Alexa as the last "person" I say goodnight to, I hope this Ananya becomes your favorite version.

**Technique bullets:**
- One governing conceit ("versions of me") replaces the usual bulleted list of quirks.
- Stanford-specific geography is load-bearing, not decorative: Coupa Cafe, Meyer Green, Great America.
- Every version is written as a shared activity, so the letter is about the relationship, not a self-portrait.
- Final line converts the conceit into a small, specific intimacy.

### Stanford #3 — Roommate: "Most people describe me as quiet" (Roommate letter, ~226 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you, or that will help your roommate—and us—get to know you better.*
- **Source:** https://www.shemmassianconsulting.com/blog/stanford-roommate-essay
- **Status:** EXAMPLE (published by Shemmassian as "a successful full-length example" from one of their students)

> Dear Future Roommate,
>
> Most people, when first meeting me, describe me as "quiet." I'm glad I have this chance to tell you that this isn't really the case. Don't get me wrong: I know how to relax by myself with a good book and a cup of tea and—don't worry—I always wear headphones when I study. My family and close friends will tell you, though, that once you get to know me, I have plenty to say.
>
> As an aspiring psychology and philosophy double major, long analytical conversations are truly my jam. People fascinate me, so I love to talk with my friends about everything from our favorite cereals to our phobias and what they say about us. (By the way, that would be Rice Krispies and spiders, respectively—any interpretations?) If you don't feel like sharing, though, it's cool. I make sure to journal every night before bed and write down my dreams when I wake up.
>
> You could say I'm a sucker for human expression. If you ever want to go to a museum or take a poetry class together, I would be delighted. I'm looking to try new things in college though, so if you'd rather bond by going rock climbing or to a comedy night (or whatever you're into), I'm game.
>
> I look forward to learning about what makes you you.
>
> Sincerely,
>
> Angelica

**Expert commentary (verbatim, Dr. Shemmassian):**
> Angelica's essay succeeds because she uses personal details to create a larger story about who she is. She gives the reader a sense of what she likes to do, but more importantly, why these things matter to her. Through this, we come to understand her unique personality.

> In reading Angelica's essay, we get a sense of her as someone who is initially reserved but ultimately chatty, interested in other people, analytical, creative, and open-minded. She was able to take the reader on a bona fide character arc in just 226 words.

**Technique bullets:**
- Corrects a first impression ("quiet") in the first two sentences — an instant premise the rest of the essay must prove.
- Defuses roommate anxiety proactively (headphones, "if you don't feel like sharing, it's cool") — proof of social awareness rather than a claim of it.
- Academics appear only as a lens on personality, never as credentials.
- Closing line hands the floor to the roommate.

### Stanford #4 — Roommate: "I don't plan on being in the room much" (Roommate letter, ~342 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim, as published at time of submission):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. What would you want your future roommate to know about you? Tell us something about you that will help your future roommate-and us-know you better.*
- **Source:** https://sites.google.com/view/admitsphere/stanford
- **Status:** ADMIT (Kay Cue, Stanford '14; "Acceptances with these essay(s): Stanford")

> Dear Roommate,
>
> I don't plan on being in the room much. I have to sleep and do homework, of course, but I'd rather not experience the rest of Stanford from the dorm. It's not you. In fact, I'd love for you to join me. That said, if you decide not to and you need to find me when I'm not in the room, look in these places first:
>
> Laboratories: I hope I can convince a future professor of mine to let me work with him or her so I can make the most of Stanford's world-class faculty and research facilities. I don't know where my pursuits will land me yet, but because I'm looking to be a chemical engineer, try the Nanoscience and Nanotechnology or Energy and Environment building.
>
> Practice rooms: I love science, but I also have a deep passion for music. I could be in a room practicing clarinet, piano, or guitar. I hope to join a band or orchestra, so you can also look wherever our rehearsals or concerts may be.
>
> Athletic events: You won't find me on a college varsity team, but I'm an avid sports fan. I'll watch any game; your best bets are football and baseball. I follow the Chargers and Red Sox too, but I'll probably be in the room for those games. Then again, I did play volleyball in high school so I might try my luck at some intramural; give that place a shot too.
>
> Libraries: Stanford's libraries have some of the world's best academic resources; I could be studying in one of them. Some homework I can do in the dorm with a bit of Jimi Hendrix on, but if I really have to read, a quiet place like the library is where I'll be. You can try the music and engineering libraries too; I could be browsing there to fuel my ambitions.
>
> If you join me, perhaps you can learn to love some of the things I do. Of course, there'll have to be balance; I'm open to your passions too.

**Technique bullets:**
- Inverts the prompt: instead of listing traits, he maps himself onto campus locations — a stealth "why Stanford" inside a roommate note.
- The list structure (Laboratories / Practice rooms / Athletic events / Libraries) gives four dimensions of one person without sounding like a résumé.
- Names real Stanford units (Nanoscience and Nanotechnology, Energy and Environment) — research without a brochure sentence.
- Last paragraph returns the invitation, keeping the letter relational.

### Stanford #5 — Roommate: "Most wanted outlaws" newspaper parody (Roommate letter, ~302 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim, as published at time of submission):** *VIRTUALLY ALL OF STANFORD'S UNDERGRADUATES LIVE ON CAMPUS. WRITE A NOTE TO YOUR FUTURE ROOMMATE THAT REVEALS SOMETHING ABOUT YOU OR THAT WILL HELP YOUR ROOMMATE – AND US – KNOW YOU BETTER.*
- **Source:** https://sites.google.com/view/admitsphere/stanford
- **Status:** ADMIT (Dory Fishie, Chemistry, Yale '15; "Acceptances with these essay(s): Stanford")

> I'm quite infamous, apparently. As divulged in the following article… Topping the list of this (school) year's most wanted outlaws again is TPHS senior Dory Fishie, juvenile at large with a record of offenses like overachieving. "I tell her every layout she needs to stop working so hard and go home," newspaper adviser Mia Smith said. "But she's still there at who-knows-when, editing pages in not just her Feature section but News and Sports as well. It's ridiculous." According to reliable sources, Fishie is also regularly spotted as late as 9 p.m. in UCSD's Pacific Hall laboratories scribbling hazardous data in a lab book. While loyal friends refused to betray her whereabouts, neighbors are encouraged to watch for a short, bubbly figure lugging an oversized backpack and a Canon EOS Rebel. Never seen without a voluminous ponytail adorned by at least 3 fluorescent bands, Fishie will likely be clad in varying hues of blue. Known for a laugh resembling a D Major scale, Fishie does not, authorities warned, possess the stereotypical criminal appearance, as she always waxes an enduring smile and is fond of assisting peers in writing or calculus. "Seriously, people need to be careful; she looks so sweet, but she'll hit the highest note on the clarinet and blow your eardrums to shards," band director Amy Willcox said. Indeed, Fishie tends to carry concealed weapons such as ink pens (to engage in lethal literary battles) and an army of post-its. As she is prone to prowl local streets on rollerblades, residents are advised to spread all driveways with sand or water to deter her escape. "There's only one way to catch her," former lab partner Jamie Li said. "Make a trap with a Steinway grand and an unlimited cache of Chopin impromptus. Or burnt cookies. She absolutely adores burnt cookies."

**Technique bullets:**
- Whole essay is a single sustained genre parody — the quirks arrive as "evidence" in a police blotter, so self-promotion never has to be stated.
- Third-person voice lets her brag (editing three sections, lab work, clarinet) while appearing to mock herself.
- Physical specificity (ponytail bands, Canon EOS Rebel, hues of blue, post-its) makes her picturable in a shared room.
- Ends on a domestic, roommate-relevant detail — burnt cookies — which doubles as an invitation.

### Stanford #6 — Roommate: "Like Kevin, Like Pam, Like Angela, Like Dwight" (Roommate letter, ~250 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim, as published at time of submission):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate - and us - know you better.*
- **Source:** https://sites.google.com/view/admitsphere/stanford
- **Status:** ADMIT (Eric Smalls, Computer Science, Stanford '16; "Acceptances with these essay(s): Stanford")

> Netflix made me who I am today: a faithful binge-watcher of The Office. The show is universally acclaimed for a reason; it's relatable. It's so relatable, in fact, that its character ensemble makes an excellent tool to catalog the facets of my personality:
>
> Like Kevin, I'm a midnight snack lover. Normally I prioritize a healthy lifestyle, but devouring low-fat ice cream straight from the container has some irresistible appeal after the clock strikes 12. Actually, that's when everything gets a bit more enjoyable. After the sun has been relaxing below the horizon for a few hours, even boring grocery store runs are stripped of mundanity and life as a whole emulates my carefree disposition.
>
> Like Pam, I'm all ears, all the time. I'd love to hear what you have to say, especially if you say it with conviction. As a chronic analyzer, I'll join you in trying to break down anything from theodicy to that cryptic text your ex sent you.
>
> Like Angela, I'm a busy beaver. Even though half of the metaphorical dams I've built are irreparably broken (programming isn't easy), you can assuredly still catch me gazing into my computer with my head cocked to one side or typing with an almost vindictive ferocity.
>
> Like Dwight, I'm an enthusiast. My curiosity regularly throws me into personally uncharted territories, with my current fascination being sci-fi-worthy "space buckets" used to cultivate plants indoors.
>
> But unlike any of these characters, I'm a real person: I'm _____, and I'll be your roommate.

**Technique bullets:**
- Borrowed structure (four TV characters) does the organizational work, freeing word count for voice.
- Each character beat is a behavior with a time stamp (midnight, late night, at the computer) — roommate-legible, not abstract.
- Self-deprecation ("programming isn't easy", broken dams) keeps the competence claims from reading as bragging.
- The final turn from fiction to name is the only "reveal," and it lands because everything before it was specific.

### Stanford #7 — Roommate: "Don't mind the morning clutter" / earrings + beatbox (Roommate letter, ~241 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate — and us — know you better.*
- **Source:** https://admityogi.com/blog/stanford-essays-guide-2023
- **Status:** ADMIT (Atman, Stanford; "a Stanford student who is now studying biology and design")

> Don't mind the morning clutter! I'll be swapping out jewelry. My daily earring choices are contingent on anything from the outfit to the weather—today, I've got on a dangly butterfly and a silver key, but I may shift to some big resin sunflowers to protest this Minnesota cold.
>
> Unfortunately, my beautiful smile won't greet you some mornings as I'll be starting bright and early in the lab. If I feel like leaving the excitement, we'll go rate bubble teas from local shops (my spreadsheet would benefit from more Californian influence).
>
> If you're the type of person who "doesn't really listen to music," that will definitely change. Our room will be playing a variety of sounds 24/7—I'm talking tunes from Tyler the Creator to Thundercat, Michael Buble to Baby Keem. You'll find me making my viral TikToks dissecting Frank Ocean songs—share your music taste with me and maybe I'll remember you when I'm famous!
>
> I'll be passively beatboxing as we study, arbitrarily prompting any stranger to freestyle over my bizarre, yet curiously potent beats. Prepare yourself: You've arrived at Stanford's "Bars 101" class.
>
> You play Ping-Pong? Check again. Against more ill-advised challengers, I'll replace my paddle with objects around me—a stray shoe, my hospital ID, my wallet, or even your wallet (you'll grumble now, but true mastery requires complete material detachment). This habit had a shamefully large impact on my decision to buy a larger phone, so meet me at the tables!

**Technique bullets:**
- Opens mid-scene ("Don't mind the morning clutter!") as if the letter were already in progress — immediately a shared room, not an essay.
- Quirks escalate in absurdity (earrings → beatboxing at strangers → Ping-Pong with a shoe → the roommate's wallet), so the humor has a rising shape.
- One spreadsheet gag and one lab reference carry all the "serious" signal; no achievements are listed.
- Every paragraph ends in an invitation to do something together.

### Stanford #8 — Roommate: "3:13AM / Hiya roomie!" (Roommate letter, ~250 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate – and us – get to know you better.*
- **Source:** https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/stanford-supplemental-essay
- **Status:** EXAMPLE (published by College Essay Guy as "a nice example essay"; Stanford-specific NSO/SLAC details)

> 3:13AM
>
> Hiya roomie! Please forgive the email at this late hour-my energy levels are directly proportional to how late it gets.
>
> I figured I'd introduce myself before we meet at NSO. Here are some cool (I hope) things about me:
>
> First off, true to my mountainous heritage, I'm quite outdoorsy, having spent many weekends trekking around state parks. I can't wait to explore these uncharted waters-wanna join me on a trip later this week to SLAC?
>
> I should warn you beforehand: I explore at an unusually zippy pace and tend to perk up at minor disturbances. That's because a bluebird day in my state can change into a roaring thunderstorm within just a few minutes, turning Fourteener hikers into lightning rods, so I've learned to always be on the lookout.
>
> Oh, and no matter what I'm doing, there's always music in the background. You'll notice that I have profound kinetic responses to melodies, which come in the form of flailing my arms during the climactic moments of symphonies. I guess music really does move me!
>
> What kind of songs do you like? I love to recreate radio music with my violin-feel free to request a song anytime, and I promise I'll give it my best effort!
>
> Lastly, I must share that there are things I will miss as I leave home. Most of all, I will miss biking with my sister around the neighborhood. So, hopefully you won't mind my daily family FaceTimes after each day's festivities!
>
> See you soon! 🙂

**Technique bullets:**
- Timestamp headline plus an email framing makes the "note" conceit literal — the reader is inside a real moment.
- Turns a weather fact into a personality claim (vigilance) rather than stating "I'm attentive."
- One vulnerability (missing home, daily FaceTimes) balances the performed energy.
- Stanford insider markers (NSO, SLAC) prove homework without a single sentence of praise for the school.

### Stanford #9 — Roommate: "My most involved project was a bowl of ramen" (Roommate letter, ~250 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate and us know you better.*
- **Source:** https://www.ivywise.com/blog/stanford-supplemental-essays/
- **Status:** EXAMPLE (published by IvyWise as "a great example" of a response)

> Dear Future Roommate,
>
> I look forward to meeting you! Something that you should know about me is that I'm really into baseball, math, music, and ceramics. But if you're ever wondering what I'm spending so much time watching on YouTube, it's most likely one of my favorite chefs, J. Kenji López-Alt, Ethan Chlebowski, or Claire Saffitz because I'm fascinated with refining my cooking technique. My most involved project to date was a bowl of ramen. I spent weeks researching its five components—soup, tare (seasoning), noodles, toppings, and aromatic oil—and how to make them from scratch. Fun fact: Ramen noodles differ from pasta in two ways. They are alkaline and lower hydration. To make them basic, use sodium carbonate, which can be derived from baking soda. Then, to make the dough cohesive despite its low hydration, put the mixture into a Ziploc bag and stomp on it. Sounds crazy, but it mimics the forces of industrial rollers. By the way, there is a really good ramen restaurant in Palo Alto called Ramen Nagi that friends and I tried last summer during SUMaC. We should go!
>
> I'm also excited to discover the food culture on campus, including the Stanford farm, teaching kitchen, and classes like Global Gastronomies and Multicultural Cooking at the international dorm. Maybe we could check them out together. Do you like to cook? What are your favorite foods? I'd love to hear all about your hobbies over some good eats.
>
> See you soon!
>
> Your Future Roommate

**Technique bullets:**
- Four interests are listed in one clause and then only one is developed — proof of restraint and of a real obsession.
- Technical specificity (alkaline, low hydration, sodium carbonate, industrial rollers) is the intellectual-vitality signal smuggled into a roommate note.
- Stanford resources are named as things to do *with* the roommate (farm, teaching kitchen, dorms), not as flexes.
- Ends by asking about the roommate's food — the letter's attention points outward.

- **Status:** EXAMPLE (published by CollegeVine as an example that "shows a ton of personality")

> Ahem…May I make a toast?
>
> First off, I am so pumped to live with you. I don't have a sibling, so this is as close as it gets!
>
> Also, I just wanted you to know…
>

ENFJs are "focused on values and vision, passionate about the possibilities for people, tuned into the needs of others, and tend to be optimistic and forward-thinking," — sums up my personality, I'd say!
>
> * I free my mind by exercising and writing. Our campus's fresh forestry is perfect for morning/night runs/picnics. It would be so fun if you joined!
>
> * I'm one to stay up all night to chat over caramel lattes and Cheez-its. I'm also one to venture out and walk/bike ten miles for Polaroid pics and yummy eats. Yelp4lyfe.

> Well, I couldn't be happier to be home with you.
>
> Cheers,

> Name (or nickname)

- Closes on "home," reframing the roommate as family — matches the opening sibling line.

### Stanford #11 — Intellectual vitality: "Shark attacks vs. falling airplane parts" (Intellectual vitality, ~251 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim):** *Stanford students possess an intellectual vitality. Reflect on an idea or experience that has been important to your intellectual development. (250 word limit)*
- **Source:** https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/stanford-supplemental-essay (full text; also introduced at https://www.admitsee.com/blog/5-stanford-intellectual-vitality-supplemental-essay-examples)
- **Status:** ADMIT (StanfordAndrew, Stanford '21)

> What's more probable: dying from a shark attack, or dying from falling airplane parts? Surprisingly, the answer is falling airplane parts. But why does our intuition point us towards shark attacks?
>
> The answer lies in the availability heuristic, or the WYSIATI ("what you see is all there is") rule, which describes how our minds evaluate decisions based on how easily we can think of examples to support both sides. From Jaws to YouTube surfer videos, we have all likely heard of a horrific shark attack, and by WYSIATI, the ease with which we conjure up that memory leads us to assign greater probability.
>
> Learning about WYSIATI evolved the way I communicate my ideas. When I first started debate, I over-focused on comparing statistics at the expense of clearly communicating larger arguments. WYSIATI taught me that a more effective approach involves weaving in memorable images like that of a horrific shark attack.
>
> This past summer, when debating whether labeling environmental activists as "eco-terrorists" is justified, my opponents cited dozens of crimes associated with activists from 1995-2002. With my knowledge of WYSIATI, I looked past the numbers and searched for more memorable, image-based examples and discovered that most of the so-called terrorist acts were actually "pie-ings": environmental groups throwing pies to protest. So, instead of responding with only numbers, I declared that "the only thing that could make pie-ings terrorist acts is if the activists didn't know how to make a good key lime pie!"
>
> Much clearer. And perhaps, a little bit funnier.

**Technique bullets:**
- Hooks with a counterintuitive trivia question, then names the concept inside the first 50 words — no vague windup.
- The idea is not left abstract: it visibly changes how he debates, with a specific round as evidence.
- Demonstrates transfer (psychology → debate strategy) rather than describing interest in psychology.
- Closes on a joke that proves the thesis about memorable images doing persuasive work.

### Stanford #12 — Intellectual vitality: "Let's use sodium sulfide" (Intellectual vitality, ~302 words)

- **Prompt (verbatim, as published at time of submission):** *Stanford students are widely known to possess a sense of intellectual vitality. Tell us about an idea or an experience you have had that you find intellectually engaging.*
- **Source:** https://sites.google.com/view/admitsphere/stanford
- **Status:** ADMIT (Kay Cue / "Kevin," Chemistry, Yale '14; "Acceptances with these essay(s): Stanford")

> "Let's use sodium sulfide," I decided.
>
> I snatched the bottle labeled Na2S and placed three drops of the foul-smelling liquid into a well on the well-plate. Then, I added some of the clear unknown solution I had been given and watched the reaction unfold. Upon contact, the two combined to form an obvious white solid.
>
> "Not again!"
>
> I stared at the chart we had been provided: seven of the twelve metal cation combinations with sulfide were white. The teacher had also informed us of other methods to differentiate between cations such as the flame test, but many were too time-consuming to use for the compound-identification race. - Explicitly rejects the grade as the motive; the "so what" is transfer of classroom knowledge to a new situation.
