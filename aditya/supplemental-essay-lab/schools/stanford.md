## 1. OFFICIAL 2026-27 PROMPTS (verbatim)

Stanford calls these the **Stanford Questions**. They live inside the Common Application. The official page was last updated July 21, 2026, and the wording below is copied verbatim from it.

**Short Questions.** "There is a 3-word minimum and a 50-word maximum for each question."

1. "What is the most significant challenge that society faces today?"
2. "How did you spend your last two summers?"
3. "What historical moment or event do you wish you could have witnessed?"
4. "Briefly elaborate on one of your extracurricular activities, a job you hold, or responsibilities you have for your family."
5. "List five things that are important to you."

**Short Essays.** "There is a 100-word minimum and a 250-word maximum for each essay."

1. "The Stanford community is deeply curious and driven to learn in and out of the classroom. Reflect on an idea or experience that makes you genuinely excited about learning."
2. "Virtually all of Stanford's undergraduates live on campus. Write a note to your future roommate that reveals something about you or that will help your roommate—and us—get to know you better."
3. "Please describe what aspects of your life experiences, interests, and character would help you make a distinctive contribution as an undergraduate to Stanford University."

**Two framing sentences from the same official page (verbatim):**

- "When you apply to Stanford, you apply to the university as a whole, not to a particular major, department or school."
- "The essays are your chance to tell us about yourself in your own words; there are no right or wrong answers and you should allow your genuine voice to come through. These questions help us get to know you as a friend, future roommate, and classmate."

**Caution on third-party prompt lists.** Several consultant sites list a prompt such as "What matters to you, and why?" as a current Stanford question. That prompt is **not** on the official 2026-27 page (it was a real Stanford prompt in earlier years, and older accepted essays still carry it). Treat those citations as historical, not current. The three current short essays are the learning one, the roommate note, and the distinctive-contribution one.

## 2. WHAT THIS SCHOOL'S READERS SAY THEY WANT (verbatim)

**Official Stanford admission language**

- "We look for students who will make a strong contribution to the Stanford community by demonstrating intellectual vitality and academic leadership." (Stanford Admission, [Decision FAQ](https://admission.stanford.edu/faqs/decision.html))
- "The University admits students who love learning for its own sake; who exhibit energy, creativity, and curiosity; and who have distinguished themselves in and out of the classroom." (Stanford Bulletin, [Undergraduate Admissions policy](https://bulletin.stanford.edu/academic-polices/admissions/undergraduate))
- "The essays are your chance to tell us about yourself in your own words... you should allow your genuine voice to come through." ([Application and Essays](https://admission.stanford.edu/apply/first-year/apply.html))

**Former Stanford admissions officers (each identified as such at the source)**

- Grace Kim, former Stanford admissions officer: "We always said when I was an admissions officer, we want it to be so personal to the student that you couldn't put anyone else's name on that essay and have it still be true about that other student." (CNBC, [2017](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/12/20/former-stanford-admissions-officer-on-what-makes-a-college-essay-shine.html))
- Grace Kim, same source: "The best essays are the ones where you can very clearly tell what their values are what their personalities are like, perhaps what their sense of humor is and just what matters to them."
- Grace Kim, same source, on cliches: "I tell students to avoid writing about it just because it's often times a sort of cliche."
- Alix Coupet, former Stanford admissions officer: "Being well-rounded is not enough. And often, students who are a little bit more well-lopsided, or angular, fare better in the admissions process." (CNBC, [2021](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/10/22/former-stanford-admissions-officer-well-rounded-is-not-enough.html))
- Alix Coupet, same source: "lean into your weirdness" and "zag where everyone else is zigging."
- Irena Smith, former Stanford admissions officer: "Be yourself and do things that bring you joy authentically." (Polygence, [Former Stanford Admissions Officer on Intellectual Vitality](https://www.polygence.org/blog/showcasing-research-on-college-applications-irena-smith))
- Michael Colin Short, former Stanford admissions officer: "When I presented students to committee for consideration, I almost always read one of their best lines of writing." (College Confidential AMA, [2020](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/former-stanford-admissions-officer-answers-your-hardest-questions-ask-me-anything/2107557))

**What the pattern adds up to.** Stanford readers ask for three things at once: a person who is unmistakably specific (Kim's "no one else's name"), a person who is angular rather than a rounded résumé (Coupet), and a person whose writing carries one memorable line (Short). The roomiest prompt for a CS/AI applicant to satisfy all three at once is the learning essay, not the roommate note.

## 4. TECHNIQUE PATTERNS (each with a quoted line)

1. **Open inside a scene, not a thesis.** "Hey Roomie! Yesterday was insane." (Roommate #5, CollegeAdvisor) drops the reader into a specific day instead of a self-description.
2. **Use a paradox as the hook.** "I am an avid anti-annotationist." (CollegeVine 1984) The contradiction makes the reader stay for the explanation.
3. **Turn an object into a growth metric.** "I read the novel twenty-seven times, each reading becoming faster and revealing more information." (CollegeVine 1984) The book measures change without announcement.
4. **Let a micro-opinion carry the voice.** "Arial is objectively a bad font." (Roommate #4, CollegeAdvisor) A small, safe, specific stance does more for voice than a large claim.
5. **Convert biography into a transferable trait.** "So, I'm used to sharing space and managing shifting schedules." (Roommate #3, CollegeAdvisor) The household census earns the roommate-relevant skill.
6. **Ground the abstract in a concrete datum.** "scientists have recently completed the first successful head transplant on a monkey." (CollegeAdvisor #1) A real fact keeps a wild idea from floating away.
7. **Make the value persist across intimacy and ambition.** "The sense of harmony I feel when dancing in the car with my family, or painting with my friends, or working with my team on our solar car." (CollegeAdvisor #7) Same value, three scales.
8. **Give a list texture with verbs and humor.** "Baking a cake in physics class... playing with a friend's new puppy... Selfies with my Target coworkers." (Roommate #8, CollegeVine) Each item starts with an action and one of them lands a joke.
9. **Do not advertise rule-breaking.** "we can make hot pot in the dorm." (Roommate #10, CollegeVine) The database flags this line as a fire-code flag; charm should never read as disregard for shared rules.
10. **Close on tension, not a moral.** "The idea terrifies me, but perhaps that is why I am so drawn to it." (CollegeAdvisor #1) Ending in unresolved pull beats ending in a lesson.

## 6. PITFALLS FOR THIS SCHOOL (with quotes)

1. **The service-trip or "I helped the less fortunate" essay.** Grace Kim: "Often times we see essays about service trips that people do abroad... I tell students to avoid writing about it just because it's often times a sort of cliche." (CNBC, [2017](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/12/20/former-stanford-admissions-officer-on-what-makes-a-college-essay-shine.html))
2. **The interchangeable, résumé-restating essay.** Kim: "you couldn't put anyone else's name on that essay and have it still be true about that other student."
3. **Being safely well-rounded.** Alix Coupet: "Being well-rounded is not enough." (CNBC, [2021](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/10/22/former-stanford-admissions-officer-well-rounded-is-not-enough.html))
4. **Preaching on the "society's challenge" question.** Common, overused answers such as climate change and race relations are widely flagged as low-yield. (Ivy Coach, [Stanford supplemental essays](https://www.ivycoach.com/stanford-supplemental-essays/))
5. **Writing a prompt that no longer exists.** "What matters to you, and why?" reads as current in several guides but is not on the official 2026-27 list.
6. **Over-branding the roommate note.** The best roommate examples keep academics out entirely (Roommate #8, CollegeVine). Treat it as domestic, ordinary, and human.
7. **Sounding polished rather than genuine.** Stanford's own page asks for "your genuine voice." A perfectly smooth paragraph that could belong to anyone fails Kim's test.
8. **Making a promise you will not specifically keep.** "I want to get excited about everything you're passionate about" is a value, not a plan; the accepted versions pair the value with concrete behavior.

## 7. AUDIT OF THE CURRENT DRAFT

Auditing `supplements/us/stanford/essays.md` (Draft V1.1) against Sections 2 through 6. Quotes below are from the draft. No rewrite is offered; only diagnosis and required fact checks.

### 7.1 Society's most significant challenge (49w): A-
Fresh angle, avoids pitfall 4 by refusing the climate and race defaults, and matches Coupet's "lean into your weirdness." The mechanism (expertise as a test of who may speak) is distinctive. Weak spot: the closing "everyone loses the questions that never get asked" is the most abstract line and the least specific; the opening two sentences do the real work. Verify the personal claim: that this describes Aditya's own lived experience rather than an adopted idea.

### 7.2 Last two summers (48w): A-
Answers the prompt directly with two bounded summers ("Summer 2025," "Summer 2026"), which is exactly what readers want. Strong line: "I watched live market data move faster than my reasoning," which is specific and self-aware. Weak spot: "tried every new gadget we could find" is the most generic phrase in the answer. Fact checks: the brother being home for a "last long stretch," the May to June exam dates, and the summer 2026 internship timeline.

### 7.3 Historical moment (44w): A
Strong. It matches the top-ranked option in `historical-moments.md` and satisfies Kim's "no one else's name" test through the personal frame (headphones, the traffic argument afterward). Concrete and unrepeatable. Fact check: the Michael Jackson 1996 Andheri Sports Complex concert and its status as his only India performance.

### 7.4 Extracurricular activity (48w): A
Uses the football tournament with an honest turn (best goalkeeper, but "I barely wanted the trophy"), which is the angular, vulnerable move Coupet and the AO comments reward. Pairs action with consequence. Fact checks: the twelve penalties, the final result, the national-level opponent, and the best-goalkeeper award.

### 7.5 Five important things (43w): A-
Correctly domestic and ordinary, and the Dakshinayan idli-sambar detail is the kind of micro-specificity the accepted examples use. Within the 3 to 50 word window. Minor note: "quiet time at a temple" is the vaguest entry; the other four carry images. No factual risk beyond confirming the restaurant and habit are real.

### 7.6 Excited about learning / intellectual vitality (201w): A
The strongest piece in the set, and consistent with Section 2 and Section 4. It starts inside a recurring scene ("On Friday nights, before the Fantasy Premier League deadline"), uses one idea across four disciplines, and lands the standout line, "the dataset includes me," which reads like the kind of line Michael Short would read aloud to committee. Fact check: that the draft's "larger study around that gap" matches the real paper in `activities/activity-list.md`, so the claim of scale is accurate.

### 7.7 Future roommate (214w): B+
Domestic, voiced, and specific (10:30 sleep, losing things, the "Khana khaya ki nahi?" call, idli-sambar, football, pickleball). This follows the accepted pattern from Section 3.3 and 3.4. Two soft spots: the family-call paragraph runs slightly long relative to the rest, and "You will know what it means within a week" is a promise the essay does not need. Fact checks: the sleep and wake times, the family-call frequency, the missing-item habit, and the pickleball and football habits.

### 7.8 Distinctive contribution (170w): B+
The teaching vignette is specific and the moment of insight ("a roomful of answers I could not trust") is strong and matches the honesty pattern. The weakness is the fit paragraph: "At Stanford, I would bring that habit to seminars, project teams, and late-night explanations between roommates" is exactly the interchangeable, brand-neutral move that fails Kim's test and drifts toward pitfall 2. The essay is already under 200 words, and `research.md` argues against inserting Stanford resource names, so the fix is not a name-drop; it is replacing a generic list of venues with one concrete, specific contribution. Also confirm this vignette does not overlap the activities list or the Common App essay in a way that repeats material.
