### The three questions (verbatim)

Copied verbatim from UCAS official guidance.

> ""Question 1: Why do you want to study this course or subject?"
>
> "Question 2: How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?"
>
> "Question 3: What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?"""

Source URL: https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/writing-personal-statement/personal-statement-builder

Verbatim quotes about what UK admissions tutors look for, especially for Computer Science. Each with URL.

### Computer Science admissions tutors, via UCAS official CS guide

> "Admissions tutors at University of Portsmouth and University of Warwick both agreed that clichés like 'I want to do computing because it's the future' or lines like 'I got my first computer when I was three' are a big turn-off."

> "We read lots of statements that start "Ever since I was five years old I have been interested in computing". We do want to see passion for the subject, but be original. Try to tell us why you are passionate about computing now and how you can demonstrate this. Make sure you understand the aims and approach of the degree programmes you are applying for and make sure your application demonstrates that."
> — Robert Zimmer, Head of Computing, Goldsmiths University of London

> "I want to know that you know what computer science is actually about. At Oxford it's a mathematical subject, so show a bit of mathematical interest too. It's not so much the knowledge you get from maths that matters, but the skills you've developed through it and the ability to think logically and mathematically. I would also be interested to hear about any reading around the subject or any programming you've done. Even if it's just a simple programme, talk about it and show your enthusiasm. But remember that we will probably then delve into it at interview."
> — Professor Gavin Lowe, Admissions Co-ordinator for Computer Science, University of Oxford

> "Professor Lowe from the University of Oxford says that lots of the personal statements he sees 'are similar, bland, impersonal and don't stand out – so make it personal, specific, and concrete'."

> "Try to write about how you work with others. Have you been part of a team or led a team? Have you had to organise something complex such as an event, a play or a football league?"
> — engineering and computer science department, Durham University

> "Admissions tutors advise you to avoid phrases such as 'computers are important in the modern world' or just saying 'I'm fascinated by artificial intelligence (AI)' without explaining why."

Source: https://www.theuniguide.co.uk/advice/personal-statement-examples/computer-science-computing-and-it-personal-statement-examples/computer-science-12

> "From an early age I've always been deeply interested in computing. It was my dad, introducing me to the computer systems at his workplace that first sparked this interest. I can always remember the feeling of wanting to know just how computers worked, why they worked and what else they could do.
>

Through the setting up of the company and its subsequent running I have learnt many things. ## 4. TECHNIQUE PATTERNS

Each bullet is backed by a quoted line. These are UK conventions, not US ones.

1. **Lead with the discipline, not the autobiography.** Open on what computer science is or what you are doing inside it, not on your childhood. Accepted line: "I am fascinated by the efficiency and power of computers: we route ourselves through traffic using mapping programs in our pockets; postal offices route mail via handwriting recognition programs in their mainframes." Tutor line: avoid "lines like 'I got my first computer when I was three'." (UCAS CS guide)

2. **Show the mathematical spine.** UK CS is treated as a mathematical subject at the top schools. Tutor line: "At Oxford it's a mathematical subject, so show a bit of mathematical interest too." (Gavin Lowe, Oxford, UCAS CS guide) Accepted evidence: "I recently used calculus to derive the backpropagation learning algorithm."

3. **Name real technical work and its result, not your feelings about it.** Tutor line: "Even if it's just a simple programme, talk about it and show your enthusiasm." Accepted evidence: "I implemented a neural network which recognized handwritten digits; it has since achieved an accuracy of 97.49 on the MNIST testset."

4. **Two thirds to three quarters academic; keep extracurriculars short.** St Andrews line: "At St Andrews, we suggest that at least two thirds of your personal statement is focused on the subject area or areas you are applying to." Reviewer line on the Edinburgh example: "Extra-curricular section is a bit long, should be 20%."

5. **Delete grades and subject lists; the form already shows them.** St Andrews line: "We will see the subjects you have studied ... so please do not list these in your personal statement as well." LSE review of a weak statement: it "has wasted space listing their International Baccalaureate subjects."

6. **Reflect on what the work taught you, do not narrate the resume.** Glasgow line: "not just the details of the roles but what you learnt about yourself, the skills you have gained and how you will apply this to the course." UCAS line: "Don't just list out examples, subjects you study, or experiences you've had without any evidence." (UCAS CS guide)

7. **One statement, no single-university content, no "dream of studying at X."** LSE review: "The applicant has specifically mentioned LSE, which is likely to be unattractive to their other choices." UCAS line: "One statement goes to all five of your choices, so it should focus on your subject rather than on a single university." (StudyIn, UCAS-centre guidance)

8. **No jokes, no quirky US-style narrative, no famous quotes.** UCAS line: "Don't use quotes from someone else, or cliches." (UCAS guidance) The accepted UK examples contain near-zero humour; the register is formal and technical.

9. **Connect the subject to a plausible future without declaring a naive one-line takeaway.** Tutor line: "show that you understand the implications of your choice on your career aspirations." (Edinburgh) The accepted examples state direction ("draw me to study computer science") rather than a moral.

10. **Write in plain British English and proofread hard.** Glasgow line: "spelling and grammar do matter – so proof, proof and proof again." St Andrews postgraduate guidance (transferable rule): "Admissions tutors will reject applications with poor spelling or grammar."

11. **It will be read alongside grades and reference, as a whole.** UCAS line: "Admissions staff will still review your personal statement as a whole, so remember not to repeat information." Edinburgh is explicit that a weak statement is a rejection reason: "Where this is not evidenced in your personal statement, we may not consider your application any further."

## 6. PITFALLS FOR THIS SCHOOL

UK-statement cliches and errors, with quotes.

1. **"I have always been passionate about" / "Ever since I was five."** Tutor quote: Robert Zimmer, Goldsmiths, notes statements "that start 'Ever since I was five years old I have been interested in computing'." UCAS: avoid "a potted history describing when you got your first computer!" Source: UCAS CS guide.

2. **Abstract passion with no evidence.** UCAS CS guide: admissions tutors advise you "to avoid phrases such as 'computers are important in the modern world' or just saying 'I'm fascinated by artificial intelligence (AI)' without explaining why."

3. **Listing extracurriculars instead of reflecting on them.** Glasgow: "every bit of information included in your personal statement should be telling the tutor something relevant. Resist the urge to waffle!" UCAS: "Don't just list out examples, subjects you study, or experiences you've had without any evidence." Source: UCAS CS guide.

4. **Listing grades, subjects or qualifications that are already on the form.** St Andrews: "please do not list these in your personal statement as well. This wastes valuable space within your limited character-count." Reviewer on the Edinburgh example: "Too much content to do with A-levels."

5. **US-style narrative, quirky hooks and jokes.** The accepted UK CS examples carry no jokes and a formal register; reviewer praise is for tone ("Very suitable tone, formal but language is not over-exaggerated"). UCAS: "Don't use quotes from someone else, or cliches." (UCAS guidance)

6. **Naming one university when the statement goes to five.** UCAS: "One statement goes to all five of your choices, so it should focus on your subject rather than on a single university." (StudyIn, UCAS-centre guidance) LSE: mentioning one university "is likely to be unattractive to their other choices."

7. **"Bland, impersonal and don't stand out."** Professor Lowe, Oxford: lots of statements "are similar, bland, impersonal and don't stand out – so make it personal, specific, and concrete." Source: UCAS CS guide.

8. **Treating the three answers as three mini-essays with three introductions and three conclusions.** UCAS: "Admissions staff will still review your personal statement as a whole, so remember not to repeat information."

9. **Under-storying computer science as a discipline.** Gavin Lowe: "I want to know that you know what computer science is actually about." Edinburgh: "demonstrate a clear understanding of what the subject involves at degree level."

10. **Weak statement as an automatic rejection reason at Edinburgh.** "Where this is not evidenced in your personal statement, we may not consider your application any further." Source: Edinburgh unsuccessful-applicants page.

11. **Plagiarism and AI.** UCAS: "UCAS scans all personal statements through a similarity detection system... Any similarity greater than 30% will be flagged and we'll inform the universities and colleges to which you have applied." Edinburgh: submitting "content that is generated by others or by AI tools such as ChatGPT as your own may be treated as plagiarism or fraud." Sources: UCAS builder page; Edinburgh submitting-a-complete-application page.
