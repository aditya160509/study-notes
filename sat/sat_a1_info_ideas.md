# DSAT Reading & Writing | Domain A1: Information and Ideas

## Overview

**Weight:** ~26% of R&W section = 12 to 14 of 54 questions (27 per module, 2 modules of 32 minutes each, about 71 seconds per question)

**Domain cross-references:** A2 (Craft & Structure) for vocabulary, text structure, and purpose questions | A3 (Expression of Ideas) for transition logic in argument-based reading | A4 (Conventions) for understanding how sentence boundaries affect meaning

**Official definition:** "Students will use comprehension, analysis, and reasoning skills and knowledge as well as what is stated and implied in texts (including in any accompanying informational graphics) to locate, interpret, evaluate, and integrate information and ideas."

**Four sub-domains:**
- A1.1 Central Ideas and Details (3 to 6 questions)
- A1.2 Command of Evidence (6 to 8 questions total)
  - A1.2a Textual Evidence
  - A1.2b Quantitative Evidence
- A1.3 Inferences (2 to 4 questions)
- A1.4 Interpreting Uncertainty in Scientific Writing (embedded across all above)

**Difficulty progression:** Within each module, questions within a skill cluster are arranged from easiest to hardest. Module 2 adapts based on Module 1 performance. Harder modules get harder passages and harder answer choices, but the question formats stay the same.

**Official framework note:** The Assessment Framework (Table 11) lists three testing points for this domain: Central Ideas and Details, Command of Evidence (Textual and Quantitative), and Inferences. Uncertainty/hedging language is not a fourth listed testing point; it is a recurring pattern inside evidence and inference questions on science passages. We treat it as its own study section (A1.4) because it shows up so often and has its own traps.

## Most Common Question Types on the Actual Exam

These are the formats the real Digital SAT repeatedly uses for Information and Ideas. Based on analysis of official College Board practice tests (Bluebook), the Assessment Framework, the official question bank, and Khan Academy's SAT course. If you master this list, you cover most of the 12 to 14 questions you will see.

1. **Central idea question on a short passage** (1 to 3 per test). Stem: "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?" or "Which choice best captures the central idea of the text?" The passage is usually 1 to 2 paragraphs. The correct answer covers the WHOLE passage; the distractors are real details (too narrow), broad topics without a claim (too broad), or outside knowledge. Sometimes the same passage also gets a detail question.

2. **Detail question on a short passage** (1 to 2 per test). Stem: "According to the text, [question]" or "The text indicates that [blank]." The correct answer is a direct paraphrase of one or two sentences. Distractors repeat passage vocabulary but twist the claim (some becomes most, causes becomes prevents).

3. **Command of Evidence: Textual - quotation supports a claim** (2 to 4 per test). Stem: "Which quotation from [work] most effectively illustrates the claim?" or "Which choice best supports the claim that [blank]?" The claim is in the stem or in the sentence before the blank. The correct quotation directly anchors the SPECIFIC claim, not the topic. This is the format College Board uses for literature passages (poems, novels, plays).

4. **Command of Evidence: Textual - hypothetical finding supports/weakens a hypothesis** (2 to 4 per test). Stem: "Which finding, if true, would most directly support (or weaken) the hypothesis that [blank]?" The findings are hypothetical: you do NOT check whether they happened, you check what they WOULD mean for the claim. This is the format for science and social science passages. The correct answer is usually NOT in the passage. Watch the support vs weaken direction carefully.

5. **Command of Evidence: Quantitative - data completes a statement** (2 to 4 per test). Stem: "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table/graph to complete the statement?" or "...to support the claim that [blank]?" The graphic is always a table, bar graph, or line graph. The correct answer must do two things: report the data accurately AND match the specific claim (extreme, trend, change, threshold, contrast, or example). The most common trap is a correctly read data point that supports a DIFFERENT claim.

6. **Inference - "most logically completes the text"** (2 to 4 per test). Stem: "Which choice most logically completes the text?" The passage builds an argument and stops right before the conclusion. The correct answer is the conclusion the facts force, one logical step from the evidence. This is THE standard inference wording on the digital SAT.

7. **Inference - cross-text response** (0 to 2 per test, overlaps with A2 Cross-Text Connections). Stem: "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1?" The correct answer follows from the second author's stated position, usually as a qualification or refinement, not a full agreement or rejection.

8. **Hedging strength (embedded)**. Science passages regularly hedge: suggests, indicates, may, might, likely, appears, consistent with, implies, could, seems, possibly, presumably. Any question on a science passage can test whether your answer matches the passage's level of certainty. An answer that upgrades "suggests" to "proves" is always wrong, even if the rest of the answer is perfect.

**Distribution tip:** Command of Evidence (textual + quantitative combined) is the single biggest chunk of this domain, roughly half of the 12 to 14 questions. Central Ideas and Details and Inferences split the rest. If you only have time to drill two things, drill textual evidence and quantitative evidence.

## A1.1 Central Ideas and Details

### Definition in Simplest Terms

Two related jobs. **Central idea:** what is this whole passage saying in one sentence? **Detail:** what does the passage say about this one specific thing? Central idea questions ask about the forest; detail questions ask about one specific tree.

### What It Tests

Two distinct but related question types:

**Central Idea (Main Idea):** Identify the author's primary claim or argument - the single sentence that captures what the passage is doing. Not the topic. Not a supporting detail. The one thing the author wrote the passage to communicate.

**Detail (Specific Information):** Locate and interpret a specific piece of information from the passage. The correct answer is a direct paraphrase of what the text says.

### The Three-Way Distinction: Topic vs Main Idea vs Theme

Students confuse these three constantly. They sit at different levels of abstraction:

| Term | What it is | Form | Example (a passage about a town's annual flood) |
|------|-----------|------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Topic | The subject, in a word or short phrase | A noun or noun phrase, not a complete thought | Floods, flood control, rivers |
| Main idea (central idea) | What the text SAYS about the topic | A complete sentence that makes a claim | Repeated flooding has led the town to rethink where it allows new construction |
| Theme | A broader life lesson or message that goes beyond the specific text | A general statement transferable to other situations | Communities only change their habits after a crisis forces them to |

How to tell them apart on the test:
- **Topic trap:** the choice is just "pollution" or "the history of the postal service" - no verb, no claim. A main idea answer always makes a point ("Pollution in the river is getting worse because of agricultural runoff").
- **Theme trap:** the choice is a universal message ("Hard times reveal true character") that the passage never states and that is too general for the specific claim. On the SAT, main idea questions are about the text's claim, not its universal theme. If a choice sounds like a fortune cookie, it is probably wrong.
- **Main idea:** the choice that would still be true if you deleted every example and supporting detail - it is what the details are there to prove.

### Question Stems

- "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"
- "Which choice best captures the central idea of the text?"
- "Which choice best describes the main idea of the text?"
- "According to the text, [specific question about a detail]"
- "The text indicates that [blank]"
- "Based on the text, which of the following is true?" (detail question in disguise)

### Central Idea: Method

1. Read the passage.
2. BEFORE looking at choices, state the passage's job in one sentence: "The author is explaining..." / "The text mainly argues..." / "The passage contrasts..."
3. Match your summary to the choices.
4. Eliminate anything that is true but narrow, broad, or unsupported.

**How to find the central idea FAST (expanded):**
1. **Read the first and last sentences first.** In short SAT passages, the first sentence often announces the topic and stance; the last sentence often delivers the payoff or conclusion. The central idea is frequently a blend of both. If the first sentence says "Historians have long assumed X" and the last says "new evidence shows Y," the main idea is almost certainly "new evidence challenges the old assumption."
2. **Track repetition.** Whatever subject, verb, or concept the passage keeps returning to is probably what it is about. If the words "selection effect" appear three times, the main idea concerns the selection effect, not the one-off example used to illustrate it.
3. **Watch structural signals.** "However," "but," "yet," and "in contrast" flip the argument: the main idea usually lives AFTER the contrast word, because authors use the pre-contrast material as setup. "Therefore," "thus," "consequently," and "this suggests" announce conclusions - prime candidates for the main idea.
4. **Ask the deletion test.** Would the passage still make sense without this idea? If yes, it is a supporting detail. If the passage would collapse, it is the main idea.
5. **Coverage check.** The correct main idea must cover a majority of the passage's details and mention the points of emphasis. If your choice only accounts for the first paragraph, it is too narrow even if it is true.

### Central Idea: Trap Taxonomy

| Trap | What It Does | Looks Like |
|------|-------------|------------|
| Detail Trap | Quotes or paraphrases a real supporting detail that is true but NOT the main idea | A specific fact from the passage stated as if it were the main point |
| Topic Trap | Names the broad subject without the author's claim | "Crow behavior" when the passage is about a specific study on face recognition |
| Theme Trap | States a universal lesson the passage never argues | "Perseverance pays off" when the passage reports one experiment |
| Overreach Trap | Makes a claim broader than what the passage supports | Extends the author's claim to an entire category or general principle |
| Outside Knowledge | Adds a fact that is true in the real world but not in the passage | "Crows are highly intelligent" when the passage never says that |
| Opposite Claim | Contradicts the passage's actual point | Says the study disproved what it actually demonstrated |
| Half-Right | Covers part of the main idea correctly but adds an unsupported or wrong second clause | "The study found X, which proves Y" where Y is never established |

### Central Idea Examples

#### [Easy]

To dye wool, Navajo (Diné) weaver Lillie Taylor uses plants and vegetables from Arizona, where she lives. For example, she achieved the deep reds and browns featured in her 2003 rug In the Path of the Four Seasons by using Arizona dock roots, drying and grinding them before mixing the powder with water to create a dye bath. To intensify the appearance of certain colors, Taylor also sometimes mixes in clay obtained from nearby soil.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A) Reds and browns are not commonly featured in most of Taylor's rugs. (**contradicted** - the passage actually gives an example of those colors)

B) In the Path of the Four Seasons is widely acclaimed for its many colors and innovative weaving techniques. (**outside knowledge** - the passage never mentions acclaim or innovation)

C) Taylor draws on local resources in the approach she uses to dye wool. (CORRECT)

D) Taylor finds it difficult to locate Arizona dock root in the desert. (**outside knowledge** - no mention of difficulty)

**Why the correct answer works:** Every detail in the passage points one direction: Taylor dyes wool using things from where she lives (Arizona plants, Arizona dock root, nearby clay). Choice C is the sentence that survives when you delete the examples.

#### [Medium]

While no one doubts that politicians are influenced by a variety of incentives, it is generally agreed that they seek to support policies their constituents favor - after all, they risk losing office if they do not. Direct contact with constituents via such means as public events and emails from voters is a major source of politicians' beliefs about their constituents' views, but it is susceptible to a selection effect. There is little reason to presume that individuals with the time, resources, and strength of feeling to directly engage with their representatives are themselves broadly representative.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A) Although politicians have an incentive to act in accordance with their constituents' views, politicians' understanding of those preferences may be skewed. (CORRECT)

B) People who are likely to contact their elected representatives do not tend to be representative of politicians' constituents generally. (**too narrow** - this is a true supporting detail that explains WHY understanding is skewed, but it doesn't cover the full main idea)

C) Direct contact with constituents shapes politicians' beliefs about policies their constituents favor, and they try to act in accordance with those beliefs. (**too narrow** - states two facts but omits the critical contrasting point: the selection effect problem)

D) Although politicians have an incentive to act in accordance with their constituents' views, that is not the only incentive they have. (**too broad / misdirection** - the passage mentions "a variety of incentives" only as an introductory concession; the rest is about the specific problem with constituent understanding)

**Structural signal to notice:** the "but" in sentence two is where the argument turns. Everything before it is setup (politicians want to please constituents); everything after it is the real point (their information about constituents is skewed). The correct answer includes both halves because the passage needs both: the incentive matters only because it makes the skewed information a problem.

#### [Hard]

Marine biologist Edith Widder hypothesized that many deep-sea bioluminescent organisms use light not merely for communication or predation but specifically to startle and disorient predators, creating a brief window of escape. To test this, Widder developed an optical lure called the "e-jelly" that mimicked the bioluminescent display of the deep-sea jellyfish Atolla wyvillei, which emits a distinctive pinwheel-shaped burst of light when attacked. In submersible deployments across the Pacific, the e-jelly succeeded in attracting large predatory squid and sharks on multiple occasions - species that would not typically be drawn to a slow-moving jellyfish. Widder argues that this counterintuitive attraction supports the "burglar alarm" hypothesis: the bioluminescent burst alerts larger, secondary predators that can then prey upon the original attacker.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A) The e-jelly optical lure was successful in attracting large predatory squid and sharks in submersible deployments. (**too narrow** - this is one finding from the research, not the author's main point)

B) Widder's hypothesis about bioluminescence as a startle response has been complicated by evidence that the e-jelly attracts rather than repels predators. (**opposite** - the passage treats this attraction as SUPPORTING the hypothesis, not complicating it)

C) Widder's research using an optical lure provided evidence for the hypothesis that certain deep-sea organisms use bioluminescence to trigger a predator-escalation sequence that aids escape. (CORRECT)

D) Bioluminescence is primarily an anti-predator adaptation rather than a tool for communication or predation in deep-sea organisms. (**overreach** - the passage focuses on one specific hypothesis about one type of bioluminescent display, not a general claim about all bioluminescence functions)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice D):** A strong student reads "not merely for communication or predation" and latches onto the anti-predator idea, then picks D because it sounds like the passage's conclusion. But D quietly expands the claim from "some organisms, according to one hypothesis" to "bioluminescence is primarily an anti-predator adaptation." The passage never ranks bioluminescence functions globally. Whenever an answer adds the word "primarily" or "all" or "only," check whether the passage made that scope claim. Here it did not.

#### [Very Hard]

In 2021, linguists shed light on the historical evolution of grammatical gender in the Indo-Iranian language family by examining 37 modern and ancestral languages. Previous scholarship had assumed that grammatical gender loss in this family occurred gradually and independently across daughter languages. However, by applying computational phylogenetic methods typically used in evolutionary biology, the researchers demonstrated that the collapse of the gender system happened via a single shared innovation in a common ancestor of multiple modern Indo-Iranian languages, followed by differential retention patterns. This finding challenges the gradualist model and suggests that language change can sometimes occur through rapid, phylogenetically traceable events that reverberate across a family tree - a pattern more commonly associated with biological speciation than with linguistic drift.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A) Computational phylogenetic methods, originally developed for evolutionary biology, were applied to 37 Indo-Iranian languages to study grammatical gender. (**topic trap** - only describes the method and scope, not the finding)

B) Grammatical gender loss in the Indo-Iranian language family resulted from a single shared ancestral innovation with differential retention, contrary to the gradualist assumption of independent development. (CORRECT)

C) Previous scholarship had assumed that grammatical gender loss in Indo-Iranian languages occurred gradually, but this has now been conclusively disproven. (**overreach** - the passage says the finding "challenges" the gradualist model but stops short of claiming it is conclusively disproven)

D) Modern Indo-Iranian languages share a common ancestor in which the gender system collapsed, proving that linguistic change mirrors biological evolution. (**overreach** - the passage says the pattern is "more commonly associated with" biological speciation, not that it proves linguistic change mirrors biological evolution; also "proving" is too strong)

**The keyword you must not skip:** "However." The entire argument structure is "old assumption ... HOWEVER ... new finding." The central idea is the new finding (single shared innovation) contrasted with the old assumption (gradual independent loss). Choice A describes only the method; choices C and D inflate the finding beyond what the passage claims. On very hard questions the trap is usually the strength of the verbs: "challenges" is not "disproves," and "more commonly associated with" is not "proves that."

### Detail Questions: Method

1. Identify the specific detail the question asks about.
2. Locate the exact sentence(s) in the passage that address it.
3. Find the answer choice that paraphrases those sentences.
4. Eliminate options that twist the detail, add conditions, or contradict.

**Expanded:**
1. **Underline the question's key noun or concept** ("depletion gilding," "the significance of the warming periods"). That is your search term.
2. **Scan for that term, or for its paraphrase.** The passage may use different words ("nearly pure gold" for "gold enrichment"). Do not hunt for exact words only; hunt for the idea.
3. **Read the full sentence around the match, plus one sentence before and after.** Most detail questions are answered from one sentence, but the surrounding context prevents misreading. If the question asks WHY something happened, the reason is usually in the same sentence or the one before it.
4. **Paraphrase that sentence in your own words before looking at choices.**
5. **Eliminate** options that twist the detail, add conditions, or contradict. Check every quantity word (some, most, all, often, always) against the passage.

### Detail: Trap Taxonomy

| Trap | What It Does |
|------|-------------|
| Exact-word Mismatch | Repeats passage vocabulary but changes the claim about it |
| Scope Shift | Changes a quantity (some -> most, often -> always) |
| Invention | Adds a condition or relationship not in the passage |
| Negation | States the opposite of what the passage says |
| Cause/Effect Flip | Swaps what caused what, or what came first |
| Wrong Time Window | Uses the right concept but from the wrong period or study |

### Detail Examples

#### [Easy]

An analysis of sediment cores from Lake El'gygytgyn in northeastern Russia indicates that the Arctic experienced periods of rapid warming approximately 3.6 million years ago, during the mid-Pliocene. These warming events were associated with increases in atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations that align closely with projected future levels under moderate emission scenarios.

According to the text, what was the significance of the mid-Pliocene warming periods?

A) They were the first time the Arctic experienced any warming. (**contradicted** - the passage says "rapid warming," not "first warming")

B) They involved CO2 levels similar to future projections under moderate scenarios. (CORRECT)

C) They were caused by the same factors that drive modern climate change. (**invention** - the passage doesn't discuss causes of mid-Pliocene warming)

D) They occurred more recently than scientists had previously estimated. (**outside knowledge** - no comparison to previous estimates is made)

**Why the correct answer works:** The question asks about the "significance" of the warming periods. The second sentence delivers it: the CO2 increases "align closely with projected future levels." Choice B is a direct paraphrase. Every distractor adds information the passage never gave.

#### [Medium]

The Moche civilization of northern Peru (100-800 CE) produced some of the most sophisticated metalwork in the pre-Columbian Americas. Using a technique called depletion gilding, Moche metalsmiths selectively removed copper from the surface of gold-copper alloys, leaving a layer of nearly pure gold. This process required precise control of temperature and oxidation conditions, as the copper had to be oxidized without melting the gold.

According to the text, what was required to successfully perform depletion gilding?

A) A supply of nearly pure gold from northern Peru. (**opposite** - the technique was applied to alloys, not pure gold; the pure gold was the RESULT)

B) Precise control of temperature and oxidation conditions. (CORRECT - direct paraphrase of the last sentence)

C) The ability to melt gold without oxidizing copper. (**contradicted** - the passage says copper had to be oxidized without melting the gold)

D) Techniques imported from other pre-Columbian civilizations. (**outside knowledge** - no mention of imports)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice C):** This is the classic "exact-word flip." The passage says "the copper had to be oxidized without melting the gold." Choice C reverses the pairing: "melt gold without oxidizing copper." The same three concepts (copper, gold, oxidize, melt) are all present, but the relationships are inverted. When a detail answer uses ALL the passage's technical words, check the verbs and prepositions carefully - the trap is in the structure, not the vocabulary.

#### [Hard]

Researchers studying the vocal learning abilities of zebra finches (Taeniopygia guttata) discovered a surprising neural mechanism: the birds' ability to learn new songs depends not on the creation of new neurons (neurogenesis) but on the modification of existing neural circuits through synaptic pruning during a critical developmental window. This contrasts with the canary, which generates new neurons annually to support song learning. The finding suggests that evolution has produced at least two distinct neurological pathways to accomplish the same behavioral outcome - vocal learning.

According to the text, how does zebra finch song learning differ from canary song learning?

A) Zebra finches rely on synaptic pruning rather than neurogenesis. (CORRECT)

B) Zebra finches have a longer critical developmental window for song learning. (**invention** - no comparison of window length is made)

C) Canaries do not require neural modification of any kind to learn songs. (**contradicted** - canaries use neurogenesis, which IS a form of neural modification)

D) Zebra finches produce more new neurons during the critical window. (**opposite** - the passage says they do NOT rely on neurogenesis)

**Why the correct answer works:** The question sets up a comparison (zebra finch vs canary). The passage draws that exact comparison: finches use pruning, canaries use new neurons. Choice A names the finch mechanism and contrasts it with neurogenesis. Choices B and C invent or contradict; choice D states the opposite of the passage.

#### [Very Hard]

A 2024 survey of amateur astronomers measured how their interest in astronomy developed. Of the respondents who reported that a family member had introduced them to the hobby, 63% said that an early childhood experience, such as a meteor shower or a planetarium visit, had been the decisive influence. Among respondents who had discovered astronomy on their own, the figure was 41%. The survey also found that respondents who joined an astronomy club reported higher satisfaction with the hobby than those who did not, regardless of how they had been introduced to it.

According to the text, what can be inferred about the role of early childhood experiences in developing an interest in astronomy?

A) They are the most common way that amateur astronomers are introduced to the hobby. (**scope shift** - the passage never says how respondents were introduced overall; it only reports percentages WITHIN two introduction categories)

B) They were cited as the decisive influence by a majority of respondents who were introduced to astronomy by a family member. (CORRECT - 63% is a majority of that subgroup)

C) They are less important than joining an astronomy club for long-term satisfaction with the hobby. (**invention** - satisfaction and early experiences are never compared to each other; they are separate findings)

D) They had no effect on respondents who discovered astronomy on their own. (**contradicted** - 41% of that group cited an early childhood experience as decisive, which is not "no effect")

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice A):** This question reads like an inference question ("what can be inferred") but is really a detail question about subgroups. The trap in A is a quiet scope shift: the passage gives percentages within two subgroups (family-introduced: 63%; self-discovered: 41%) but never tells you the overall mix of the two groups in the survey. You cannot compute "the most common way" from subgroup percentages alone. Whenever an answer makes a claim about the whole ("most common," "majority of all respondents") and the passage only gives subgroup numbers, check whether the overall comparison is actually supported. It usually is not.

### Paired Questions: The Same Passage, Two Jobs

College Board sometimes gives one passage and then two questions: one central idea, one detail. Doing them together is a great test of the topic/main idea/detail distinction. The detail question's correct answer is often a piece of the central idea answer, and the central idea answer is the umbrella that contains the detail.

#### [Medium] Paired Set

The expansion of the United States Postal Service in the nineteenth century depended on a network of overland routes, steamship lines, and, beginning in 1858, transcontinental stagecoach service. Yet the postal system's growth was not simply a matter of new roads and faster ships. It required an enormous administrative apparatus - postal inspectors, railway mail clerks, and a sorting system that processed millions of letters a year - and it depended on the labor of thousands of workers, many of whom were women hired as clerks and sorters during the Civil War when men left for military service. The Post Office Department became, in effect, the largest employer in the federal government, and its operations shaped everything from newspaper distribution to the settlement of the American West.

**Question 1 (central idea):** Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A) The Post Office Department was the largest employer in the federal government. (**too narrow** - this is one supporting fact, not the point)

B) The growth of the US postal system in the 1800s depended on both new transportation and a large administrative and labor force. (CORRECT)

C) The United States Postal Service. (**topic trap** - names the subject, makes no claim)

D) Government institutions can change the course of national development. (**theme trap** - a general lesson the passage never states)

**Question 2 (detail):** According to the text, why were many women hired as clerks and sorters during the Civil War?

A) Because men had left to serve in the military. (CORRECT - direct paraphrase of "when men left for military service")

B) Because the volume of mail had doubled during the war. (**invention** - no mail-volume claim appears)

C) Because stagecoach service made it easier for women to reach post offices. (**invention** - no connection between stagecoach service and hiring women)

D) Because the postal system was expanding into the American West. (**invention** - expansion westward is mentioned as an EFFECT of the postal system, not a reason for hiring women)

**Why the pair works together:** Notice that the detail answer (A) is contained inside the central idea answer (B): the hiring of women is one example of the "large administrative and labor force." If you answered Question 1 correctly, Question 2's correct answer should feel familiar. If a detail question's correct answer does not fit inside your central idea, one of your two answers is probably wrong - go back and check the relationship.

## A1.2 Command of Evidence

### A1.2a Textual Evidence

### Definition in Simplest Terms

Someone makes a claim. Your job: pick the piece of text that most directly PROVES or SHOWS that specific claim. Not a related claim. Not the topic. That claim.

### What It Tests

Select the specific textual evidence that best supports a given claim, interpretation, or conclusion. The claim is stated in the question stem (or in a preceding paired question). The answer choices are quotations from the passage, or hypothetical findings ("if true").

There are two flavors:
- **Literature flavor:** a claim about a work (a poem, play, novel) and four quotations from that work. Pick the quotation that best illustrates the claim.
- **Science/social science flavor:** a hypothesis or claim, and four hypothetical findings ("Which finding, if true..."). Pick the finding that would best support (or weaken) the claim. IMPORTANT: the correct answer is usually NOT in the passage - the findings are new information. Your job is to judge what each finding would MEAN for the claim, not to find it in the text.

The official framework adds two useful notes: (1) the evidence links to the claim via reasoning - it does not simply restate the claim (if an answer just repeats the claim's words, it is usually too weak or circular); (2) you never need outside knowledge or prior familiarity with the literary work.

### Question Stems

- "Which choice best supports the claim that [blank]?"
- "Which quotation from the passage most effectively illustrates the claim?"
- "Which finding, if true, would most directly support the hypothesis that [blank]?"
- "Which finding, if true, would most weaken the hypothesis that [blank]?"
- "Which choice provides the strongest evidence for the conclusion that [blank]?"
- "Pair format" (first question: which claim is supported? second: which evidence supports it?)

### Method

1. Read the question stem FIRST. Identify the exact claim.
2. Simplify the claim: "What specific relationship or statement needs evidence?"
3. Test each choice: does this quote DIRECTLY support THAT claim (not some related claim)?
4. A correct evidence answer exists. Look for the choice that most directly anchors the claim's specific wording.

**Expanded, with the claim-component trick:**
1. **Find the claim sentence.** In literature questions it is usually the sentence right before the blank ("the speaker describes..."). In science questions it is the hypothesis ("The researchers hypothesized that X causes Y").
2. **Break the claim into its components.** Write down the 2 to 3 pieces the claim requires. Example: the claim "fermentation makes black beans both easier to digest and more nutritious" has TWO components: (a) easier to digest, (b) more nutritious. Evidence that only shows (a) is half-right and wrong. Evidence that shows (a) but also shows something that hurts (b) is also wrong. The strongest evidence addresses all components and contradicts none.
3. **Predict the evidence BEFORE looking at choices.** If the claim is "urban coyotes adapted to exploit human food more efficiently," predict: "a finding showing urban coyotes process human food faster than rural coyotes." Now the choices are easy to sort.
4. **Apply the directness test to each choice.** Ask: "If this were the only evidence I had, would it make ME believe the claim?" If you need to add two extra steps of reasoning to connect the evidence to the claim, it is not the strongest evidence.
5. **Watch the direction word.** "Support" and "weaken" are opposites. A choice that would weaken the claim is wrong when the stem asks for support, even if it is a brilliant piece of reasoning.

### The Three Job Types: Support, Weaken, Illustrate

| Job | Stem signal | What the correct answer does |
|-----|-------------|------------------------------|
| Support | "would most directly support," "strengthens," "is consistent with" | Makes the claim more likely to be true; matches what the claim predicts |
| Weaken | "would most weaken," "undermines," "challenges" | Makes the claim less likely to be true; contradicts what the claim predicts, or introduces a missing factor the claim ignored |
| Illustrate | "most effectively illustrates," "provides an example of" | Shows the claim in action with a concrete instance (usually a quotation) |

### Textual Evidence: Trap Taxonomy

| Trap | What It Does |
|------|-------------|
| Related-but-Different Claim | Supports a claim that sounds similar but is logically distinct |
| Wrong Direction | Supports the OPPOSITE of the claim (or weakens when asked to support) |
| General Support | Relates to the topic generally but doesn't address the specific claim |
| Partial Match | Only supports part of the claim while ignoring another critical part |
| Circular / Restatement | Just re-says the claim in other words, adding no evidence for it |
| Outside Knowledge | True in the world but not derivable from the passage or the claim |

### Textual Evidence Examples

#### [Easy]

Passage: "Ghosts of the Old Year" is an early 1900s poem by James Weldon Johnson. In the poem, the speaker describes experiencing an ongoing cycle of anticipation followed by regretful reflection: ______

Which quotation from "Ghosts of the Old Year" most effectively illustrates the claim?

A) "The snow has ceased its fluttering flight, / The wind sunk to a whisper light, / An ominous stillness fills the night, / A pause - a hush."

B) "And so the years go swiftly by, / Each, coming, brings ambitions high, / And each, departing, leaves a sigh / Linked to the past." (CORRECT)

C) "What does this brazen tongue declare, / That falling on the midnight air / Brings to my heart a sense of care / Akin to fright?"

D) "It tells of many a squandered day, / Of fields unreaped."

**Why:** The claim is "anticipation followed by regretful reflection." Choice B shows "ambitions high" (anticipation) and "leaves a sigh / Linked to the past" (regretful reflection). Choice A only describes stillness - no anticipation or regret. Choice C describes care and fright (fear, not regret). Choice D describes regret only (squandered days, fields unreaped) with no anticipation component.

#### [Medium]

Passage: A researcher hypothesized that urban-dwelling coyotes have adapted their foraging behavior to exploit human food sources more efficiently than their rural counterparts. The researcher predicted that urban coyotes would demonstrate shorter handling times for anthropogenic food items and would prefer these items over natural prey even when both were equally available.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researcher's hypothesis?

A) Urban coyotes consumed human food sources at a higher rate than rural coyotes when natural prey was scarce. (**related-but-different** - this supports that urban coyotes EAT more human food, not that they've ADAPTED to exploit it MORE EFFICIENTLY in terms of handling time or preference when both options are available)

B) Urban coyotes processed packaged food items 40% faster than rural coyotes did when presented with the same anthropogenic food types. (CORRECT - directly addresses "shorter handling times")

C) Rural coyotes avoided human-populated areas even when those areas contained accessible food waste. (**wrong direction** - this describes avoidance, not adaptation)

D) Both urban and rural coyotes showed equal preference for natural prey over anthropogenic food in controlled choice trials. (**opposite** - contradicts the predicted preference for anthropogenic food)

**Why the correct answer works:** The hypothesis has two predicted components: shorter handling times AND preference for human food when both options are available. Choice B nails the first component exactly ("40% faster" = shorter handling time). Choice A is the seductive one: it is about eating human food, which SOUNDS like the topic, but it adds a condition (natural prey scarce) that the hypothesis explicitly set aside ("even when both were equally available"). The hypothesis is about adaptation, not about what coyotes eat when they are hungry.

#### [Hard]

Passage: Historian M'hamed Oualdi challenges the conventional narrative that the transition from Ottoman rule to European colonial administration in North Africa represented a complete rupture in governance practices. By examining the careers of provincial administrators who served both Ottoman and French authorities, Oualdi demonstrates that many administrative practices, tax collection methods, and legal procedures persisted across the nominal regime change. He argues that this institutional continuity was not merely inertia but an active strategy by local elites who leveraged their administrative expertise to maintain social standing under changing political authorities.

Which choice best supports Oualdi's claim that institutional continuity was an active strategy rather than passive inertia?

A) Several former Ottoman administrators were retained by French authorities specifically because their knowledge of local customs facilitated colonial tax collection. (**general support** - this describes retention but not the ACTIVE STRATEGY by local elites)

B) Tax collection records from 1885 to 1900 show that the proportion of revenue allocated to local infrastructure was consistent before and after the French takeover. (**related-but-different** - shows continuity but doesn't distinguish active strategy from inertia)

C) Correspondence among Tunisian provincial officials during the transition period reveals deliberate coordination to preserve their administrative roles by framing existing practices as indispensable to French governance. (CORRECT - directly shows ACTIVE STRATEGY through planning and coordination, not passive inertia)

D) French colonial authorities introduced new legal codes that coexisted alongside existing Islamic legal practices, creating a hybrid system. (**wrong direction** - describes a change (hybrid system), not continuity; also French-initiated, not local-elite-driven)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice B):** B is the trap that catches students who only read the word "continuity" in the claim. The claim is NOT "things stayed the same." The claim is "things stayed the same ON PURPOSE, as a strategy by local elites." Choice B proves continuity (consistent revenue allocation) but says nothing about whether anyone planned it. An evidence answer must match the claim's distinguishing feature. Here the distinguishing feature is the ACTIVE, deliberate quality - which only C, with its "deliberate coordination," actually shows.

#### [Very Hard]

Passage: Urban planners in two mid-sized cities tested whether replacing a section of street parking with a protected bike lane changes local retail sales. The research team hypothesized that (1) businesses immediately adjacent to the new lane would see increased sales because cyclists stop more easily than drivers, but (2) this effect would be limited to food and beverage establishments, since cycle trips are typically shorter and more frequent than car trips. In City A, the lane replaced parking on a street with five restaurants and three clothing stores. In City B, a comparable street with the same business mix served as the control, with parking unchanged.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the research team's hypothesis?

A) In City A, sales at the restaurants rose by 9% after the lane was installed, while sales at the clothing stores were essentially unchanged, and no comparable change occurred in City B. (CORRECT - supports both components: adjacent businesses gained, and the gain was limited to food and beverage)

B) In City A, sales rose by 9% at the restaurants and by 8% at the clothing stores after the lane was installed, with no change in City B. (**partial match + contradiction** - supports component 1 but contradicts component 2, which predicted the effect would be LIMITED to food and beverage)

C) In City B, sales at the restaurants rose by 9% during the study period even though parking was unchanged. (**wrong direction** - this finding would actually WEAKEN the hypothesis, since it shows the sales gain happened without the bike lane)

D) In City A, cyclists were observed stopping at 40% of the businesses along the lane, but drivers had stopped at only 15% of the same businesses before the lane was installed. (**related-but-different** - this supports the mechanism (cyclists stop more) but does not connect stopping to actual SALES, which is what the hypothesis predicts)

**Why the correct answer works:** The hypothesis has TWO components, and the correct evidence must satisfy both. Choice A delivers: restaurants up (component 1) and clothing stores unchanged (component 2 - effect limited to food and beverage), plus the control city shows the change was really caused by the lane. Choice B is the trap: it gives you component 1 beautifully but violates component 2, so it is "half-right" and wrong. Choice C is a weaken-in-disguise. Choice D proves the mechanism, not the predicted outcome.

### A1.2b Quantitative Evidence

### Definition in Simplest Terms

Same job as textual evidence, but the evidence is numbers in a graph, table, or chart. Someone makes a claim. Your job: pick the choice that uses the data to prove THAT claim. The choice must be (1) accurate to the graphic AND (2) relevant to the specific claim.

### What It Tests

Select the specific data point or trend from a graph, table, or chart that best supports a specified claim. The graphic types are limited to tables, bar graphs, and line graphs. The digital SAT displays the graphic beside the passage; everything you need is in the visual - there is no math beyond simple differences and comparisons. The official framework notes that the passages are accurate (if describing real circumstances) or plausible (if hypothetical), and that informational "noise" is minimized: the graphic contains only what you need.

### Question Stems

- "Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the claim that [blank]?"
- "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?"
- "Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the example?"

### Method

1. Read the graphic FIRST: title, axis labels, units, legend. Understand what is measured.
2. Read the passage / question stem. Identify the specific claim.
3. Evaluate each choice: (a) Is this data point accurate in the graphic? (b) Does it directly support the SPECIFIC claim?
4. The trap is often a correctly read data point that supports a DIFFERENT claim.

**Expanded (the claim-to-data translation):**
1. **Orient to the graphic.** Read the title, column/axis labels, categories, units, and legend BEFORE anything else. Do not start with the tallest bar or the biggest number. Ask: what is being measured, in what units, over what period, and for which groups?
2. **Translate the claim into a data request.** Restate the blank or conclusion as a precise instruction: "find the largest increase," "find a value above 50%," "compare group A with group B in 2025," "find two categories that differ widely." The unfinished statement in the stem tells you which of these the evidence must do. This single step kills most traps, because it tells you what to look for before the choices try to distract you.
3. **Locate only the needed data.** Trace the correct row, column, line, bar, year, and unit. Write down or say the relevant values before reading the choices closely.
4. **Two-part proof test.** For each remaining choice, finish both sentences: "The graphic shows [exact values]..." AND "This supports the claim because [specific link]...". If either sentence fails, eliminate the choice.
5. **Verify, don't trust.** The correct choice almost always reports the data accurately, but occasionally a plausible-sounding choice contains a value the graphic does not actually show (or shows for a different category/year/unit). Check every number.

### Quantitative Task Types and Their Signals

| Task | Signal language in the stem | What to compare | Frequent trap |
|------|----------------------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Extreme | highest, lowest, greatest, least | All relevant values in the same unit and category | Picking the largest visible number from the wrong column |
| Trend | increased, declined, remained stable, fluctuated | The same measure across ordered time points | Citing correct endpoints but describing the direction backward |
| Change | largest rise, sharpest decline, doubled, increased by | Beginning and ending values for each series | Choosing the highest FINAL value instead of the greatest CHANGE |
| Threshold | majority, more than half, at least, no more than | Each candidate value against the exact cutoff | Treating 50% as "more than half," or confusing counts with percentages |
| Contrast | whereas, compared with, unlike, varied by | The named groups in the named period or condition | Using two accurate values from different years or categories |
| Claim test | supports, illustrates, is consistent with | The pattern the passage predicts vs what the graphic shows | A true data statement that does not address the prediction |

### Quantitative Evidence: Trap Taxonomy

| Trap | What It Does |
|------|-------------|
| Correct Data, Wrong Claim | Cites a real, correctly read data point but it supports a different claim (most common trap) |
| Correct Data, Wrong Direction | Data actually weakens or contradicts the claim |
| Reads Data Wrong | Misstates a value, trend, or comparison from the graphic |
| Single-Point Mismatch | Cites one data point when the claim requires a comparison or trend |
| Time-Period Mismatch | Cites data from the wrong time window |
| Highest Value, Not Largest Change | Picks the biggest final number when the claim asks for the biggest increase |
| Unit or Scale Error | Reads the number but ignores the unit (percent vs count, "in thousands," degrees F vs C) |
| Correct Number, Wrong Category | Quotes a real value but assigns it to the wrong group, year, or treatment |
| Overstated Conclusion | Data show an association or one sample, but the choice says always, causes, or all |

### Quantitative Evidence Examples

#### [Easy]

Table: Participants' Evaluation of the Likelihood That Robots Can Work Effectively in Different Occupations

| Occupation | % Unlikely | % Neutral | % Likely |
|------------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Television news anchor | 24 | 9 | 67 |
| Teacher | 37 | 16 | 47 |
| Firefighter | 62 | 9 | 30 |
| Surgeon | 74 | 9 | 16 |
| Tour guide | 10 | 8 | 82 |

Passage excerpt: "Researchers were interested in people's perceptions of robots' competence. They found that evaluations varied widely depending on what occupation was being considered; for example, ______"

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the example?

A) 82% of participants believe that it is somewhat or very likely that a robot could work effectively as a tour guide, but only 16% believe that it is somewhat or very likely that a robot could work as a surgeon. (CORRECT - directly illustrates "varied widely")

B) 47% of participants believe that it is somewhat or very likely that a robot could work effectively as a teacher, but 37% of respondents believe that it is somewhat or very unlikely that a robot could do so. (**wrong claim** - this cites data for ONE occupation, not a comparison showing variation ACROSS occupations)

C) 9% of participants were neutral about whether a robot could work effectively as a television news anchor, which is the same percent of participants who were neutral when asked about a robot working as a surgeon. (**wrong claim** - the same percentage shows SIMILARITY, not wide variation)

D) 62% of participants believe that it is somewhat or very unlikely that a robot could work effectively as a firefighter. (**single-point mismatch** - only one occupation; the claim is about variation "depending on occupation" which requires a comparison)

**Why the correct answer works:** The claim's data request is "show that evaluations VARIED WIDELY ACROSS occupations." That requires a comparison between at least two occupations with very different numbers. Choice A compares the highest "likely" value (tour guide, 82%) with the lowest (surgeon, 16%) - maximum contrast. The other choices are all individually true to the table but answer a different question: B and D describe a single occupation, and C shows similarity.

#### [Medium]

Line graph: "Average Annual Global Temperature Anomaly (1880-2020)" - x-axis: year, y-axis: temperature anomaly in degrees Celsius relative to 1951-1980 baseline. Line shows gradual upward trend with steep acceleration post-1970. Specific data points: 1880: -0.2C, 1920: -0.3C, 1950: -0.1C, 1980: +0.2C, 2000: +0.4C, 2020: +1.0C.

Passage excerpt: "Climate scientists have noted that the rate of global warming has accelerated significantly since the mid-20th century. Whereas the temperature anomaly increased by only about 0.1C over the seventy years from 1880 to 1950, the subsequent seventy-year period saw a much more dramatic increase: ______"

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the statement?

A) the temperature anomaly reached +1.0C in 2020. (**single-point mismatch** - this gives an endpoint but doesn't illustrate the RATE increase across the period)

B) the temperature anomaly increased by about 1.1C from 1950 to 2020. (CORRECT - 1950: -0.1C, 2020: +1.0C, net change of 1.1C, compared to 0.1C in the earlier 70-year period)

C) the temperature anomaly was +0.2C in 1980 and +0.4C in 2000. (**time-period mismatch** - cites specific years but doesn't capture the full 70-year window from 1950 to 2020)

D) the temperature anomaly in 2020 was 1.0C higher than in 1880. (**wrong claim** - compares 2020 to 1880, which spans 140 years, not the 70-year window the claim specifies)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice A):** A is a real, correctly read data point (+1.0C in 2020 is on the graph), which is why it tempts. But the claim is about the RATE - how much the anomaly increased over the second 70-year window versus the first. The claim's data request is "compute the change from 1950 to 2020." Choice A gives an absolute value, not a change, and gives no comparison to the earlier window. On change/trend questions, always ask: does the choice state the CHANGE (difference between two points) or just one point? One point cannot show a change.

#### [Hard]

Bar graph: "Species Richness in Restored Wetland Plots by Treatment Type" - x-axis: four treatment groups (Control, Seeded, Transplanted, Seeded + Transplanted), y-axis: mean number of plant species after 3 years. Error bars shown. Data: Control: 8, Seeded: 12, Transplanted: 18, Seeded + Transplanted: 34.

Passage excerpt: "Ecologists testing restoration strategies for degraded wetlands hypothesized that active intervention would produce higher species richness than passive recovery, and that combining methods would have a synergistic effect exceeding the sum of individual treatments. The three-year results supported both predictions: ______"

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the statement?

A) the Transplanted plots (18 species) outperformed the Control plots (8 species), but the Seeded plots (12 species) showed only a modest improvement. (**partial support** - supports the first prediction (active intervention > passive) but doesn't address the second prediction about synergy)

B) the Seeded + Transplanted plots (34 species) had higher richness than the Control plots (8 species), confirming that combined methods work best overall. (**wrong claim** - this supports "combined is best" but doesn't address whether the effect is SYNERGISTIC (exceeding the sum of individual treatments); "work best overall" is not the same as "exceed the sum of the parts")

C) the Seeded + Transplanted plots (34 species) exceeded the sum of the Seeded (12) and Transplanted (18) plots' individual results (30 species), indicating that combining methods produced a synergistic effect. (CORRECT - 34 is greater than 12 + 18 = 30, so the combination beat the additive expectation; this directly supports both predictions: active intervention works, and combining methods does more than the parts would predict)

D) the Seeded + Transplanted plots (34 species) exceeded the sum of the individual treatment results, suggesting that the two methods interfered with each other. (**wrong conclusion** - exceeding the sum of the parts is the DEFINITION of synergy; interference would produce a result BELOW the sum, so the conclusion contradicts the data it cites)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice D):** D is a pure logic trap: it cites the correct relationship (34 > 30) and then draws the wrong conclusion from it. Interference means the combined result is LESS than the sum of the parts (like the original data showing 24, which is below 12 + 18 = 30). A result ABOVE the sum means the methods amplified each other - synergy. Whenever a choice pairs a correct data fact with a conclusion word (indicating, suggesting, proving), check that the conclusion matches the direction of the math. The same numbers can support opposite conclusions depending on which side of the sum they land.

#### [Very Hard]

Line graph: "Concentration of Atmospheric Trace Gases (1750-2020)" - three overlaid lines: CO2 (ppm), CH4 (ppb), N2O (ppb). CO2: rises from ~280 (1750) to ~415 (2020), with sharp inflection around 1950. CH4: rises from ~720 (1750) to ~1900 (2020), with plateau from 1990-2005 then resumed increase. N2O: rises from ~270 (1750) to ~330 (2020), with steady increase post-1950.

Passage excerpt: "While carbon dioxide receives the most public attention, methane and nitrous oxide are substantially more potent greenhouse gases per molecule. A 2023 study examining mitigation strategies noted that reducing anthropogenic methane emissions could produce a more rapid climate response than equivalent CO2 reductions, because methane has a shorter atmospheric lifetime. However, the study also cautioned that to achieve the deepest long-term temperature stabilization, all three gases must be addressed simultaneously. Using data from the graph, a student argues that despite growing awareness of these issues, mitigation efforts have not yet produced a clear sustained decline in any of the three gases on a global scale."

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's argument?

A) CO2 concentrations increased from approximately 315 ppm in 1950 to 415 ppm in 2020, while CH4 increased from approximately 1500 ppb to 1900 ppb over the same period. (**wrong claim** - shows that all three increased, which indicates a LACK of mitigation efficacy, but the claim is specifically about the ABSENCE of a "clear sustained DECLINE" post-awareness; this answer doesn't address whether there was any decline or just continued increase)

B) CH4 concentrations showed a period of relative stability between 1990 and 2005, with values fluctuating between 1750 and 1800 ppb, before resuming their upward trend. (**wrong direction** - if anything, this WEAKENS the student's argument by showing a period where CH4 was NOT increasing, possibly reflecting early mitigation effects)

C) N2O concentrations rose steadily from approximately 270 ppb in 1750 to 330 ppb in 2020, with no multi-year period showing a sustained decrease despite increased awareness of greenhouse gas impacts since the 1990s. (CORRECT - directly supports "no clear sustained decline in any of the three gases" by showing N2O's uninterrupted increase, specifically referencing the awareness period)

D) CO2 concentrations in 2020 (415 ppm) were nearly 50% higher than pre-industrial levels (280 ppm), demonstrating that the cumulative effect of emissions far exceeds any reductions achieved. (**partially correct but imprecise** - this supports the broader idea of insufficient mitigation but uses a pre-industrial comparison (1750-2020) rather than addressing the "post-awareness" period the student's argument hinges on)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice A):** A is the tempting choice because it is 100% accurate to the graph and it mentions the right gases. But it argues the wrong point. The student's claim is not "the gases increased" - it is "there has been no SUSTAINED DECLINE during the awareness period." Choice A's increases are consistent with that claim but do not demonstrate the specific thing the claim asserts (absence of decline). Choice C demonstrates it directly by pointing at the steady N2O line across the awareness period. On claim-test questions, the accurate-but-generic choice (A) loses to the accurate-and-specific choice (C). The two-part proof test catches this: A's second sentence ("this supports the claim because...") cannot be completed without adding an extra inference.

#### [Medium] Threshold Example

Table: "Voter Turnout by Age Group in the 2024 Municipal Election"

| Age group | Registered voters | Ballots cast | Turnout rate |
|-----------|-------------------|--------------|--------------|
| 18-29 | 12,400 | 4,340 | 35% |
| 30-44 | 18,900 | 8,130 | 43% |
| 45-59 | 16,200 | 8,910 | 55% |
| 60 and over | 14,300 | 9,005 | 63% |

Passage excerpt: "After the election, analysts noted that turnout was not evenly distributed across age groups. In particular, they observed that a majority of registered voters actually cast ballots only among older voters: ______"

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?

A) 35% of registered voters aged 18 to 29 cast ballots, the lowest rate of any age group. (**correct data, wrong claim** - true, but the claim is about which groups reached a MAJORITY, not about the lowest rate)

B) 43% of registered voters aged 30 to 44 cast ballots. (**wrong claim** - 43% is below a majority, so this actually contradicts the claim that only older voters reached majority turnout)

C) 55% of registered voters aged 45 to 59 and 63% of registered voters aged 60 and over cast ballots. (CORRECT - both exceed 50%, and they are the two groups named as "older voters")

D) Registered voters aged 60 and over cast 9,005 ballots, more than any other age group in raw numbers. (**wrong claim** - the raw count comparison is a different claim; the passage's claim is specifically about MAJORITY turnout rates, and the 60+ group's raw advantage is partly due to registration size)

**Why the correct answer works:** The claim contains a threshold word: "a majority." The data request is "find the groups whose turnout rate is above 50%." Only the 45-59 group (55%) and the 60+ group (63%) clear it. Choice A is a real fact about the table but answers a different question (lowest rate). Choice D mixes raw counts into a claim about rates - a classic unit/category trap.

#### [Hard] Highest Value vs Largest Change Example

Bar graph: "Enrollment at Four Community Colleges, 2015 and 2024" - two bars per college (light gray = 2015, dark gray = 2024). Values: College A: 2015: 2,400; 2024: 3,000. College B: 2015: 1,200; 2024: 2,400. College C: 2015: 3,800; 2024: 4,100. College D: 2015: 900; 2024: 1,600.

Passage excerpt: "A researcher studying enrollment patterns at community colleges observed that although overall enrollment grew at every institution in the sample, the growth was uneven. The researcher noted that one college in particular experienced the most dramatic growth over the nine-year period: ______"

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the statement?

A) College C's enrollment grew from 3,800 to 4,100, remaining the largest college throughout the period. (**highest value, not largest change** - College C has the biggest final number but the smallest increase, 300)

B) College B's enrollment doubled from 1,200 to 2,400 over the period. (CORRECT - an increase of 1,200 on a base of 1,200, the largest relative growth, and among the largest absolute growth as well)

C) College A's enrollment grew by 600 students, while College D's grew by 700 students. (**correct data, wrong claim** - gives two increases but does not identify the "most dramatic growth" as the claim requires; also neither of these is the largest increase overall)

D) College D's enrollment grew from 900 to 1,600, an increase of 700 students. (**single-point mismatch / wrong claim** - the largest absolute increase is College B's 1,200, not College D's 700; College D is a real increase but not "the most dramatic")

**Why the correct answer works:** The claim's data request is "find the most dramatic growth." That is a CHANGE comparison: for each college, compute 2024 minus 2015. College A: 600. College B: 1,200 (a doubling). College C: 300. College D: 700. College B has both the largest absolute increase and the largest relative increase (100%). Choice A is the classic trap: College C LOOKS dominant because its bars are tallest, but the question asks about growth, not size. Always distinguish "biggest number" from "biggest change" - the stem's word "grew" or "increase" means you compute differences.

## A1.3 Inferences

### Definition in Simplest Terms

The passage states facts. The correct inference is the conclusion those facts FORCE you to accept - not merely a conclusion they allow. If the conclusion could be false while the passage stays true, it is not the inference.

### What It Tests

Draw a logical conclusion from information stated or implied in the text. The correct inference MUST follow from the text. It is a conclusion the text forces you to accept, not a guess, not what sounds plausible in the real world. The official framework describes the format precisely: each question distills a longer argument down to a single unit, presented "up to the point at which the conclusion is introduced," and you select "the most logical conclusion among the answer choices given." You never need outside knowledge - the framework guarantees that everything required is in the question itself.

There are two official wordings on the digital SAT:
- "Which choice most logically completes the text?" (the standard format)
- "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1?" (cross-text)

A third variant appears in practice: "Which choice is most strongly supported by the text?" - same logic, softer wording.

### Question Stems

- "Which choice most logically completes the text?"
- "Based on the text, which choice is most strongly supported?"
- "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1?"
- "The researchers' findings imply that ______"

### Method: Mini Logic Proof

1. Identify the facts the passage establishes.
2. Ask: If Fact A is true and Fact B is true, what conclusion MUST be true?
3. State that conclusion in your own words BEFORE looking at choices.
4. Match, do not stretch. The correct answer is a maximum of one logical step from the evidence.

**Expanded:**
1. **List the facts like premises.** Write each established claim as a short sentence: "Fact 1: sleep is widespread. Fact 2: deep sleep leaves animals vulnerable. Fact 3: known benefits seem too small to explain why sleep spread." Do this in your head or on scratch paper.
2. **Ask the forcing question.** "If all these facts are true, what MUST also be true?" Not "what might be true?" or "what would be nice if true?" - what is LOGICALLY REQUIRED. In the sleep example, facts 2 and 3 force the conclusion that there must be some benefit scientists have not found yet; otherwise the known trade-off makes no sense.
3. **Say it in your own words first.** "There must be undiscovered advantages to sleep." Now the choices are easy to rank. This step matters because the SAT's wrong answers are designed to look reasonable; your own summary is your anchor.
4. **Apply the one-step rule.** The correct inference is at most ONE logical step from the evidence. If an answer requires you to add an intermediate assumption (people must have migrated, a measurement must have been flawed, a variable must have been held constant), it is usually a "skips a step" trap.
5. **Trust the obvious answer.** On inference questions, the right answer often feels almost explicit - like a paraphrase of what the passage was building toward. Khan Academy's own advice: "The right answer is often the one that feels most explicit - it won't be a 'reach.'" If you are choosing between a safe, directly-supported answer and a clever, speculative one, the safe one is correct.

### The Negation Test (The Logic-Proof Check)

This is the most powerful verification tool in the whole domain. It works like this:

**If a choice is the correct inference, then making that choice FALSE must create a contradiction with the passage. If the passage can still be true while the choice is false, the choice is not forced - so it is wrong.**

Test each candidate answer by mentally inserting "NOT" into it:
- Choice: "The artifacts originated elsewhere and reached the settlement through trade or migration."
- Negate it: "The artifacts did NOT originate elsewhere - they were made at the settlement."
- Does the passage still hold? NO - the passage says the settlement was founded in the 14th century but contains 13th-century artifacts. If the artifacts were made at the settlement, the settlement would have existed in the 13th century, contradicting the founding date. The negation breaks the passage, so the choice is forced: CORRECT.
- Now try a distractor: "The settlement was founded by people from a different region than assumed."
- Negate it: "The settlement was founded by people from the SAME region as assumed."
- Does the passage still hold? YES - nothing in the passage says anything about where the founders came from. The negation changes nothing. Therefore this choice is NOT forced: wrong.

The negation test separates "must be true" from "could be true" cleanly. Use it whenever two answer choices are both plausible.

### The "Most Strongly Supported" Variant

Some questions ask which choice is "most strongly supported" rather than "most logically completes." The logic is identical, but the answer choices may all be partially supported - your job is to rank them and pick the one with the strongest textual anchor and the least added baggage. Apply a strength checklist:
1. Does the choice use only concepts from the passage?
2. Does it require zero outside knowledge?
3. Does its certainty level match the passage's (no "must" where the passage says "may")?
4. Would the negation test break the passage? (If yes, it is strongly supported.)

### Inference: Trap Taxonomy

| Trap | What It Does |
|------|-------------|
| Goes Beyond | Adds a claim the text doesn't back up; the most common wrong answer |
| Skips a Step | Requires assuming an intermediate fact the passage never states |
| Outside Knowledge | True in the real world but not supported by or derived from the passage |
| Extreme Language | Uses always, never, all, none, proves when the passage is tentative |
| Half-Right | Has one correct clause and one unsupported clause |
| Opposite / Reverse | Flips cause-effect, direction, or stance of the passage |
| Plausible but Unsupported | Sounds reasonable but has no textual anchor |
| Misleading Keyword | Uses vocabulary from the passage but builds a different meaning |

### Inference Examples

#### [Easy]

Many animals, including humans, must sleep, and sleep is known to have a role in everything from healing injuries to encoding information in long-term memory. But some scientists claim that, from an evolutionary standpoint, deep sleep for hours at a time leaves an animal so vulnerable that the known benefits of sleeping seem insufficient to explain why it became so widespread in the animal kingdom. These scientists therefore imply that ______

A) it is more important to understand how widespread prolonged deep sleep is than to understand its function. (**outside knowledge** - no comparison of importance is made or implied)

B) prolonged deep sleep is likely advantageous in ways that have yet to be discovered. (CORRECT - the scientists acknowledge known benefits are insufficient to explain its evolutionary success, so there must be unknown advantages that make the trade-off worthwhile)

C) many traits that provide significant benefits for an animal also likely pose risks to that animal. (**too broad** - this general statement could be true, but the passage is specifically about deep sleep, not "many traits")

D) most traits perform functions that are hard to understand from an evolutionary standpoint. (**goes beyond** - the passage limits its claim to the specific case of deep sleep, not "most traits")

**Negation test on the correct answer:** If deep sleep were NOT advantageous in undiscovered ways, then the known benefits would have to explain its spread - but the passage says they seem insufficient. Contradiction. The answer is forced.

#### [Medium]

Researchers studying the settlement of Kuulo Kataa, in modern Ghana, recovered artifacts that date from the thirteenth century CE. This may lend credence to claims that the settlement was founded before or around that time. There is other evidence, however, strongly supporting a fourteenth century CE founding date for Kuulo Kataa. If both the artifact dates and the fourteenth century CE founding date are correct, that would imply that ______

A) artifacts from the thirteenth century CE are more commonly recovered from excavations than artifacts from the fourteenth century CE are. (**outside knowledge** - no support for frequency comparison)

B) the artifacts originated elsewhere and eventually reached Kuulo Kataa through trade or migration. (CORRECT - if the settlement was founded in the 14th century but contains 13th-century artifacts, those artifacts must have come from somewhere else)

C) Kuulo Kataa was founded by people from a different region than had previously been assumed. (**skips a step** - the migration of artifacts doesn't necessarily mean the PEOPLE migrated; artifacts can trade without population movement)

D) the excavations may have damaged or displaced some of the artifacts recovered at Kuulo Kataa. (**outside knowledge** - introduces a methodological criticism not suggested or implied in the passage)

**Why the correct answer works:** This is a forced-conclusion puzzle. Two facts: (1) some artifacts date to the 13th century; (2) the settlement was founded in the 14th century. If the settlement did not exist in the 13th century, the 13th-century artifacts cannot have been produced there - so they must have come from elsewhere. There is no other way to hold both facts together. Choice C adds an unsupported leap (artifact movement does not imply people movement), and the negation test demolishes it: "the settlement WAS founded by people from the assumed region" changes nothing in the passage.

#### [Hard]

Text 1: When companies in the same industry propose merging with one another, they often claim that the merger will benefit consumers by increasing efficiency and therefore lowering prices. Economist Ying Fan investigated this notion in the context of the US newspaper market. She modeled a hypothetical merger of Minneapolis-area newspapers and found that subscription prices would rise following a merger.

Text 2: Economists Dario Focarelli and Fabio Panetta have argued that research on the effect of mergers on prices has focused excessively on short-term effects, which tend to be adverse for consumers. Using the case of consumer banking in Italy, they show that over the long term (several years, in their study), the efficiency gains realized by merged companies do result in economic benefits for consumers.

Based on the texts, how would Focarelli and Panetta (Text 2) most likely respond to Fan's findings (Text 1)?

A) They would argue that over the long term the expenses incurred by the merged newspaper company will also increase. (**outside knowledge** - introduces a new claim about expenses not discussed in either text)

B) They would recommend that Fan compare the near-term effect of a merger on subscription prices in the Minneapolis area with the effect of a merger in another newspaper market. (**skips a step** - this is an unrelated methodological suggestion; their actual argument is about TIME HORIZON, not market selection)

C) They would encourage Fan to investigate whether the projected effect on subscription prices persists over an extended period. (CORRECT - their central claim is that long-term effects differ from short-term effects, so they would want to know whether the price increase Fan found is temporary or permanent)

D) They would claim that mergers have a different effect on consumer prices in the newspaper industry than in most other industries. (**outside knowledge** - nothing in either text compares industries)

**Why the correct answer works:** Text 2's authors have one central position: short-term merger effects look bad, but long-term effects can be good. Fan found a short-term-style result (prices rise). Given their position, the natural response is: "check what happens in the long run" - which is exactly C. The correct cross-text answer is always derivable from the second author's stated position; if you cannot derive it, it is wrong.

#### [Very Hard]

In 1966, psychologists asked 84 undergraduates who had never met before to rate each other on five personality traits, based entirely on appearance. For three traits - extroversion, conscientiousness, and openness - the observers' rapid judgments matched real personality scores significantly more often than chance. However, when British psychologists Anthony Little and David Perrett re-ran the experiment using mugshots rather than live subjects, they only found a link between facial appearance and personality for extroversion and conscientiousness. This supports the idea that ______

A) accuracy of personality judgments based on appearance depends on whether the target is in motion or static. (CORRECT - the key difference between the two experiments was live subjects (in motion, dynamic) vs. mugshots (static). The different results suggest that the moving quality of live subjects provides additional information - specifically about openness - that is lost in static images)

B) the original 1966 study's findings were invalid due to methodological flaws. (**goes beyond** - re-ran with one variable changed and got somewhat different results; this doesn't "invalidate" the original, it refines the conditions under which the effect holds)

C) personality judgments based on appearance are generally unreliable and should not be trusted. (**overreach** - both studies found ABOVE-CHANCE accuracy for most traits, so judgments are NOT generally unreliable)

D) extroversion and conscientiousness are more accurately judged from appearance than openness is. (**half-right** - this is true of the second study specifically, but it's a RESTATEMENT of the second study's results, not an inference about WHY the results differed; the question asks for what the COMPARISON supports)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice D):** D is the trap that separates hard from very-hard students. Everything in D is TRUE: in the mugshot study, extroversion and conscientiousness were judged accurately and openness was not. But the question asks what the COMPARISON of the two studies supports - and D just describes one study's outcome. The inference lives in the DIFFERENCE: the only thing that changed between the studies is live motion vs static images, so the difference in results (openness dropped out) points to motion as the variable that matters. Choice A names that variable. When a "very hard" inference answer restates an obvious fact from the passage, be suspicious: the correct inference is usually one level up, explaining the pattern rather than repeating it.

#### [Hard] The Official Framework Example (Science)

Ecologists Anna Traveset and Nuria Riera investigated a decline in the population of the shrub Daphne rodriguezii from some areas of Spain's Balearic Islands. Traveset and Riera observed that the greatest population of D. rodriguezii is found in the area where Lilford's wall lizard (Podarcis lilfordi), which has been reduced in many parts of the islands, still thrives; that P. lilfordi appears to be the only natural disperser of D. rodriguezii seeds; and that seeds that are not consumed by P. lilfordi tend to accumulate beneath parent plants, where they are easily consumed by other animals and where they may struggle to thrive due to competition. Taken together, these observations suggest that ______

A) the decline of the P. lilfordi population has contributed to the decline in the D. rodriguezii population. (CORRECT)

B) the decline in the population of D. rodriguezii may be attributable to an increase in the consumption of the plants' seeds by P. lilfordi. (**opposite** - P. lilfordi is the DISPERSER; more seed consumption by the lizard means more seed dispersal, which would help D. rodriguezii, not hurt it)

C) potential dispersers of D. rodriguezii seeds have been outcompeted by P. lilfordi, leading to a decline in the population of D. rodriguezii. (**outside knowledge / unsupported** - no competition between dispersers is mentioned; the passage says P. lilfordi is the only natural disperser)

D) the islands' population of D. rodriguezii must have been established before the island's population of P. lilfordi. (**skips a step** - the observations establish a relationship, not a chronology; nothing in the passage orders the two populations in time)

**Why the correct answer works:** This is a three-premise chain: (1) the healthiest D. rodriguezii population coexists with the healthiest P. lilfordi population; (2) P. lilfordi is the only natural seed disperser; (3) undispersed seeds pile up under parent plants and struggle. Chain them: where the lizard declines, seeds go undispersed, and the shrub declines. That is choice A, stated at exactly the passage's strength ("has contributed to" - not "caused entirely," which the evidence could not support). Note how the correct answer matches the hedge: the stem says "suggest," and the answer says "has contributed to" - no "proves," no "must have."

#### [Very Hard] The "Most Strongly Supported" Variant

Passage: "A team of neuroscientists examined how sleep deprivation affects emotional memory. In one experiment, 60 participants learned a set of emotionally neutral images and a set of emotionally arousing images. Half were then allowed a full night of sleep; the other half were kept awake. Twenty-four hours later, the sleep group correctly recognized 88% of the arousing images but only 64% of the neutral ones. The sleep-deprived group recognized 71% of the arousing images and 62% of the neutral ones. The researchers note that the arousing images were rated by a separate panel as uniformly high in emotional intensity, while the neutral images varied somewhat in how 'neutral' participants found them."

Based on the text, which choice is most strongly supported?

A) Sleep deprivation impairs memory for emotionally arousing images more than it impairs memory for neutral images. (CORRECT - the sleep group beat the deprived group by 17 points on arousing images (88 vs 71) but by only 2 points on neutral images (64 vs 62), so sleep's benefit - and deprivation's cost - is concentrated in arousing material)

B) A full night of sleep improves memory for all images, regardless of their emotional content. (**overreach** - the sleep group scored higher on both image types, but the passage gives no reason to claim ALL images; also the neutral-image gap (2 points) could be noise)

C) The participants' ratings of emotional intensity affected how well they remembered the images. (**skips a step / half-right** - the ratings came from a SEPARATE panel, not from the participants; the passage never measures participants' own intensity ratings)

D) Sleep deprivation has no effect on memory for neutral images. (**contradicted** - the deprived group scored 62% vs the sleep group's 64% on neutral images; the passage cannot establish "no effect," and the numbers do not support a zero-effect claim)

**Why the correct answer works:** "Most strongly supported" means: rank the choices by how tightly the data back them. Choice A is the direct reading of the two gaps - the deprivation cost is huge for arousing images (17 points) and tiny for neutral ones (2 points). Choice B inflates a two-category result into "all images." Choice C misattributes the rating panel. Choice D overstates a small gap into "no effect" - the negation test kills it: "sleep deprivation DOES affect neutral memory" is consistent with the numbers (62 vs 64 could be a real small effect). The strongest answer is the one that stays closest to the measured differences.

### Cross-Text Inference (Paired Passages)

These require determining how one author would respond to another's claim. The correct answer must be derivable from the second author's stated position.

**Method:**
1. Identify the specific claim from Text 1 that is referenced.
2. Identify the second author's overall argument or position.
3. Ask: Given that position, would they agree, disagree, or qualify? What specifically would they say?
4. The correct answer is almost always more moderate than a full agreement or rejection - it typically suggests a refinement, qualification, or alternative interpretation.

**Additional rules:**
- If Text 2's author is skeptical of short-term studies, they respond to short-term findings by asking about the long term (as in the Fan/Focarelli example above).
- The correct answer never introduces a fact neither text discusses.
- The correct answer never attributes to the second author a position they explicitly reject.

## A1.4 Interpreting Uncertainty in Scientific Writing

### Definition in Simplest Terms

Scientists rarely say "X causes Y" flat out. They say "X may cause Y," "the data suggest X is linked to Y," or "these findings are consistent with Y." The hedges - may, suggests, appears, likely - are not noise. They are the claim's strength setting. Your answers must use the same strength setting. If the passage says "suggests," your answer may not say "proves."

### What It Tests

Recognize and correctly interpret hedging language (also called epistemic modality) that indicates the degree of certainty with which a claim is made. The DSAT tests whether students can distinguish between a tentative claim and an absolute one, and whether they can identify when an answer choice overstates or understates the certainty expressed in the passage. This pattern appears inside A1.2 and A1.3 questions on science passages: the passage hedges, and one or two answer choices quietly remove the hedge.

### Core Concept

Scientific writing rarely deals in absolutes. The language a writer uses encodes their level of confidence. Different verbs, modals, and adverbs signal different degrees of certainty. The SAT expects you to match your answer's certainty to the passage's certainty.

### Signal-Word Table (the words the College Board actually uses)

The master syllabus lists these hedging signals: suggests, indicates, may, might, likely, appears, consistent with, implies, could, seems, possibly, presumably. Here is each one, what it does, and how strong it is:

| Signal word | What it signals | Strength | Example |
|-------------|----------------|----------|---------|
| demonstrates, proves, establishes | Conclusive evidence; no reasonable doubt | Strongest | "The trial demonstrates that the vaccine prevents infection." |
| confirms | A prior hypothesis was verified | Strong | "The results confirm the earlier finding." |
| indicates | The evidence points in a direction, with reasons behind it | Medium-strong | "The data indicate a correlation." |
| shows | Similar to indicates; direct but not absolute | Medium-strong | "The study shows a measurable effect." |
| suggests | Evidence leans one way but alternatives remain | Medium | "The pattern suggests a connection." |
| implies | The conclusion follows by logic, but is not stated outright | Medium | "The findings imply that the mechanism matters." |
| is consistent with | Compatible with; does NOT prove - other explanations also fit | Medium | "The results are consistent with the hypothesis." |
| likely / probably | Probabilistic; more likely than not | Medium | "The species likely arrived by raft." |
| appears to / seems to | Observation is hedged; what you see may not be what is | Medium-weak | "The cell appears to respond to the signal." |
| may / might / could | Possibility only; no probability asserted | Weak | "This may explain the decline." |
| possibly / perhaps | Bare possibility | Weakest | "Possibly, the effect is seasonal." |
| presumably | The author is assuming something without proof | Weak-assumptive | "Presumably, the samples were stored cold." |

### Hedging Language Spectrum

| Strength | Verbs | Modals | Adverbs | Example |
|----------|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| Strongest | demonstrates, proves, establishes | must, will | certainly, undoubtedly, clearly | "The data demonstrates that temperature increases cause glacial retreat." |
| Medium-Strong | indicates, shows, confirms | should | strongly, consistently | "The results indicate a correlation between the variables." |
| Medium | suggests, implies, is consistent with | would | likely, probably | "The evidence suggests that the treatment is effective." |
| Medium-Weak | appears to, seems to, may indicate | may | possibly, perhaps | "The pattern appears to be associated with seasonal changes." |
| Weakest | might suggest, could indicate, tentatively suggests | might, could | conceivably, in some cases | "These findings might suggest a mechanism worth further investigation." |

### Key Distinctions Tested

- **suggests vs. demonstrates**: "Suggests" leaves room for alternative explanations; "demonstrates" implies conclusive evidence.
- **may vs. will**: "May" indicates possibility; "will" indicates prediction or certainty.
- **appears to be vs. is**: "Appears to be" hedges the observation; "is" presents a fact.
- **is consistent with vs. proves**: "Consistent with" allows multiple interpretations; "proves" claims exclusivity.
- **likely vs. certainly**: "Likely" is probabilistic; "certainly" is categorical.
- **indicates vs. confirms**: "Indicates" is directional evidence; "confirms" implies prior hypothesis verified.
- **could vs. must**: "Could" is one possibility among many; "must" claims the only possibility.
- **presumably vs. demonstrably**: "Presumably" flags an assumption; "demonstrably" claims proof.

### Overstatement Trap

The most common error: replacing the passage's hedging language with stronger language in the answer choice.

Example:
- Passage: "The results suggest that the compound may reduce tumor growth."
- Wrong answer: "The compound reduces tumor growth." (**overstates certainty**)
- Right answer: "The results indicate a possible reduction in tumor growth." (matches the hedge)

The rule: an answer may be weaker than the passage (within reason), but it may never be stronger. When two answers are otherwise equal, the one that preserves the passage's hedging is correct.

### Understatement Trap (rare but tested)

Occasionally, a passage makes a strong claim, and an answer choice hedges it.

Example:
- Passage: "The experiment conclusively demonstrated that the protein is essential for neural development."
- Wrong answer: "The protein may play a role in neural development." (**understates certainty**)
- Right answer: "The experiment established that the protein is necessary for neural development."

### Conditional Logic

Many scientific claims are conditional - true only if certain conditions are met. The SAT tests recognition that a conditional claim does not apply when the conditions are not met.

Key signal words: assuming, provided that, given, if...then, under these conditions, when (used conditionally).

Example: "Under these hypothetical conditions, all potential compounds could be synthesized by 2050." The claim is ONLY valid under those conditions. An answer that says "all potential compounds will be synthesized by 2050" drops the condition and is wrong.

### How to Apply This to Evidence and Inference Questions

1. **Scan the passage for hedge words before you answer.** Underline suggests, may, appears, likely, consistent with, could.
2. **State the passage's strength level:** strong, medium, weak.
3. **Test each answer's strength level.** Words like proves, demonstrates, establishes, must, always, certainly raise the level. Words like suggests, may, possibly, consistent with keep it level.
4. **Eliminate any answer whose strength does not match.** This works even when you are unsure about the content - a "proves" answer on a "suggests" passage is wrong no matter how true it sounds.
5. **Also check conditions.** If the passage says "assuming X," an answer that drops "assuming X" is wrong.

### Uncertainty Examples

#### [Easy]

Passage: "Preliminary analysis of sediment samples from the Gale Crater suggests that liquid water may have existed on the Martian surface more recently than previously believed."

Which choice best reflects the level of certainty in the passage?

A) Liquid water definitely existed on the Martian surface more recently than believed. (**overstates certainty** - "suggests" and "may" are tentative, "definitely" is absolute)

B) The analysis proves that liquid water existed on Mars. (**overstates certainty** - "proves" is far stronger than "suggests")

C) The analysis indicates the possibility that liquid water existed on Mars more recently than believed. (CORRECT - "possibility" matches "may have")

D) The sediment samples conclusively demonstrate the recent presence of liquid water. (**overstates certainty** - "conclusively demonstrate" is much stronger than "suggests")

**Why the correct answer works:** The passage has two hedges: "suggests" (verb) and "may have" (modal). The answer must not exceed either. Choice C converts both into "indicates the possibility" - same strength. Every other choice escalates.

#### [Medium]

Passage: "A 2023 meta-analysis of 47 randomized controlled trials indicates that cognitive-behavioral therapy is consistently effective for treating generalized anxiety disorder, with effect sizes comparable to pharmacological interventions. However, the authors note that long-term follow-up data remain limited, and the results should be interpreted with caution as most trials had follow-up periods of less than 12 months."

Which choice best reflects the author's stance on the effectiveness of cognitive-behavioral therapy for generalized anxiety disorder?

A) The therapy is effective but more research is needed to confirm long-term outcomes. (CORRECT - matches both the positive "indicates...consistently effective" and the caution "long-term follow-up data remain limited")

B) The therapy is as effective as medication, but the research is too flawed to draw conclusions. (**overstates the weakness** - the meta-analysis found consistent effectiveness across 47 RCTs; this dismisses the evidence as insufficient)

C) The therapy has been conclusively proven to be more effective than medication. (**overstates certainty** - "conclusively proven" ignores the hedging language; also "more effective" is not stated)

D) The therapy shows promise, but the evidence is too preliminary to support clinical use. (**understates the strength** - "preliminary" undercuts that this is a meta-analysis of 47 RCTs finding "consistently effective" results)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice D):** Overstating is the famous trap, but D shows the rarer understating trap. The passage's evidence is strong (47 RCTs, consistent effectiveness); D calls it "too preliminary." Students pick D because "cautious" feels like the right SAT attitude. It is not: the passage's caution is narrow (long-term data), not global (the whole evidence base). Match the SIZE of the hedge to the passage - a limited hedge ("long-term data are limited") is not permission to downgrade everything to "preliminary."

#### [Hard]

Passage: "The synthesis of novel superheavy elements has led theorists to consider the feasibility of synthesizing all possible chemical compounds. In 2022, Guillermo Restrepo calculated the historical rates at which newly discovered compounds have entered the scientific literature, expanding the 'chemical space.' Using a conservative estimate of the number of viable compounds and assuming a doubling of the historical pace of discovery, Restrepo found that under these hypothetical conditions, all potential compounds could be synthesized and documented by the year 2050."

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined portion ("Using a conservative estimate...assuming a doubling...")?

A) It provides a comparison between historical and current rates of compound discovery. (**wrong function** - the portion describes a HYPOTHETICAL scenario, not a comparison)

B) It establishes the conditions under which Restrepo's projected timeline is valid. (CORRECT - the finding is explicitly conditional on meeting these assumptions)

C) It highlights the role of human inefficiency in slowing the pace of chemical discovery. (**outside knowledge** - the passage doesn't discuss inefficiency)

D) It explains why Restrepo's method differs from those used by earlier theorists. (**wrong function** - no comparison to earlier methods is made)

**Why the correct answer works:** This is conditional logic in action. The prediction (all compounds by 2050) is wrapped in conditions: "using a conservative estimate" and "assuming a doubling." Choice B names exactly what those words do - they set the conditions for the claim. Choice A misreads the hypothetical as a comparison; C and D import topics that never appear.

#### [Very Hard]

Passage: "A longitudinal study tracking 12,000 participants over 25 years examined the relationship between midlife dietary patterns and cognitive decline in later life. After controlling for socioeconomic status, education, and baseline health, researchers found that adherence to a Mediterranean-style diet was consistently associated with a slower rate of cognitive decline. Specifically, participants in the highest quintile of Mediterranean diet adherence scored on average 2.3 points higher on cognitive assessments at age 70 than those in the lowest quintile. The authors note that while these results are robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, the observational design precludes causal conclusions, and residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Nevertheless, these findings are consistent with prior work suggesting that inflammation and oxidative stress may mediate the relationship between diet and cognitive outcomes."

A student claims that the study provides strong evidence that a Mediterranean diet prevents cognitive decline. Is this claim supported?

A) Yes, the study found a 2.3-point difference between high and low adherence groups after controlling for confounders. (**overstates** - the study found an ASSOCIATION, not evidence of prevention; also the design precludes causal conclusions)

B) No, because an observational study cannot establish causation; the link may be due to other factors not measured. (CORRECT - the passage explicitly states "observational design precludes causal conclusions" and "residual confounding cannot be ruled out")

C) Yes, because the results were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, indicating the effect is likely causal. (**misreads hedging** - "robust across sensitivity analyses" strengthens the evidence but does not change the fundamental limitation of observational design)

D) No, because the 2.3-point difference is clinically insignificant. (**outside knowledge** - the passage doesn't establish what difference is clinically significant; this introduces a criterion not discussed)

**Wrong-answer autopsy (Choice C):** C teaches the most sophisticated lesson in this section: not every strengthening word overrides the passage's fundamental hedge. "Robust across sensitivity analyses" is a real positive, but the passage itself says the observational design "precludes causal conclusions." The word "precludes" is absolute: no amount of robustness within the same design can make the study causal. When the passage uses a hard limit word (precludes, cannot, rules out), no answer may claim the thing that is precluded. The student's word "prevents" is causal - the passage never supports it - so the correct answer is a firm "No," stated at the passage's own strength.

#### [Medium] "Consistent With" Example

Passage: "Researchers studying bumblebee foraging observed that colonies located near flowering hedgerows returned to the hive with more pollen per trip than colonies in open fields. The researchers note that the results are consistent with the hypothesis that landscape features providing continuous floral resources improve colony foraging efficiency, though the observational design means other explanations for the difference cannot be excluded."

Which choice best reflects what the study's results show?

A) The results prove that hedgerows improve colony foraging efficiency. (**overstates** - the passage explicitly says other explanations "cannot be excluded"; "prove" is far too strong)

B) The results are compatible with the hedgerow hypothesis but do not rule out alternative explanations. (CORRECT - "consistent with" means compatible; "cannot be excluded" confirms alternatives remain)

C) The results disprove the hypothesis that hedgerows matter for foraging. (**opposite** - the results lean WITH the hypothesis, not against it)

D) The results show that hedgerows improve foraging only when pollen is scarce. (**invention** - no condition about pollen scarcity appears in the passage)

**Why the correct answer works:** "Consistent with" is the easiest hedge to overread. It means the data fit the hypothesis - nothing more. Any number of other hypotheses could also fit the same data. Choice B says exactly that. This is the word College Board loves to test because "consistent with" LOOKS like an endorsement when it is really just compatibility.

#### [Hard] "Presumably" Example

Passage: "In reconstructing the diet of the extinct giant ground sloth, paleontologists analyzed dental microwear on fossil teeth recovered from a cave in the Andes. The wear patterns resembled those of modern leaf-eating mammals, and the cave sediments contained pollen from highland grasses. Presumably, the sloths migrated seasonally between forested lowlands and highland grasslands, following the availability of preferred forage."

Which choice best describes the function of the word "Presumably"?

A) It signals that the migration claim is an assumption inferred from the evidence rather than a directly observed fact. (CORRECT - "presumably" flags an inference the author believes but cannot confirm)

B) It emphasizes that the migration claim is the most important finding of the study. (**wrong function** - "presumably" weakens certainty; it does not add emphasis)

C) It indicates that the migration claim was verified by direct observation of the sloths. (**contradicted** - the sloths are extinct; direct observation is impossible, and "presumably" means the opposite of verified)

D) It concedes that the evidence supports a different conclusion. (**wrong function** - the evidence points TOWARD the migration claim; "presumably" means the author accepts it, not that the evidence points elsewhere)

**Why the correct answer works:** "Presumably" is the purest assumption-flag in the SAT's hedge vocabulary. The author has evidence (microwear, pollen) that suggests seasonal movement but no direct proof. Choice A names that exact epistemic status. Choice C is the trap: it reads "presumably" as if the claim were confirmed, which is the opposite of what the word does.

## Pair Format: Command of Evidence (Textual)

Some DSAT questions appear as a pair: the first question asks which claim is supported, and the second asks which evidence proves it.

### Method for Pair Questions

1. Read the passage.
2. First question: identify the supported claim (like an inference).
3. Second question: find the specific quote that most directly supports that claim.
4. Cross-check: the evidence you select for Q2 must actually prove the answer you chose for Q1.
5. If the first question asks about "the claim" and the second asks for "the evidence," the Q2 answer MUST directly refer to information that supports Q1's claim.

**Expanded:**
1. **Answer Q1 as an inference question.** The first question ("Which choice best describes a claim supported by the passage?") is really an inference in disguise: what must be true given the facts? Use the mini logic proof.
2. **Treat Q2 as a textual evidence question.** The second question ("Which choice provides the strongest evidence for the answer to the previous question?") asks for the quotation that anchors the claim you picked.
3. **Do the cross-check explicitly.** After choosing both, verify: does the Q2 quotation contain the exact idea the Q1 claim asserts? If Q1 says "the speaker cycles between hope and regret," Q2's quote must contain hope AND regret. If your Q2 quote only shows regret, one of your answers is wrong.
4. **Use Q2 to correct Q1.** If you are torn on Q1, look at which claims the Q2 choices could support. The Q2 answer choices are often drawn from the passage; whichever Q1 claim has a slam-dunk quotation in Q2 is probably correct.

### Pair Trap

**Circular Evidence Mismatch:** You pick a claim in Q1, then try to find evidence for a DIFFERENT claim in Q2. The correct pairing requires internal consistency - the evidence must map to the claim you selected.

## Cross-Domain Note: Uncertainty in Inferences

Inference questions and uncertainty interpretation overlap significantly. An inference answer choice that upgrades the passage's hedging language ("may suggest" -> "proves") is always wrong. This is the most common high-difficulty trap across both sub-domains.

Similarly, quantitative evidence questions often include an answer that correctly reads the data but uses overconfident causal language (e.g., "proves" instead of "supports," "causes" instead of "is associated with"). Eliminate these even when the data point is correct.

**The strength-check habit:** before you submit ANY answer on a science passage, ask two questions: (1) Does my answer match the passage's certainty level? (2) Does my answer drop any condition the passage set (assuming, provided that, under these conditions)? If yes to either, find another answer.

## Numbered Traps - Complete List

1. **Detail Trap (Main Idea):** A true supporting detail is dressed as the main idea. It happened in the passage, but it is not what the passage is ABOUT.

2. **Topic Trap (Main Idea):** The answer names the general subject but does not capture the author's specific claim about it. A topic is a noun phrase; a main idea is a complete claim.

3. **Theme Trap (Main Idea):** The answer states a universal life lesson ("Perseverance pays off") that the passage never argues. The SAT asks for the text's claim, not its universal message.

4. **Overreach Trap (Main Idea):** The answer extends the passage's claim beyond its scope - from specific to general, from one case to all cases, from correlation to causation. Watch scope words: primarily, all, only, always.

5. **Outside Knowledge Trap:** An answer that is true in the real world but not stated or implied in the passage. The SAT does not reward what you know; it rewards what the passage says.

6. **Opposite / Contradiction Trap:** The answer states the reverse of what the passage claims.

7. **Related-but-Different Claim (Textual Evidence):** The answer supports a claim that sounds similar to the one in the question but is logically distinct. Requires precision reading.

8. **Wrong Direction (Textual Evidence):** The answer actually supports the opposite of the claim - or weakens it when the stem asks for support. Always check the direction word first.

9. **Correct Data, Wrong Claim (Quantitative Evidence):** The answer cites a real, correctly read data point that supports a different claim than the one specified. The most frequently missed trap in quantitative questions.

10. **Single-Point Mismatch (Quantitative Evidence):** The answer cites one data point when the claim requires a comparison, trend, or relationship.

11. **Time-Period Mismatch (Quantitative Evidence):** The answer uses data from the wrong time window or comparison point.

12. **Highest Value, Not Largest Change (Quantitative Evidence):** The answer picks the biggest final number when the claim asks for the biggest increase. Compute differences when the stem says grew, rose, declined, or increased.

13. **Unit or Scale Error (Quantitative Evidence):** The answer reads the number but ignores the unit - percent vs raw count, "in thousands," degrees F vs C, ppm vs ppb. Read the axis label and column header before trusting any value.

14. **Correct Number, Wrong Category (Quantitative Evidence):** The answer quotes a real value but assigns it to the wrong group, year, treatment, or measure.

15. **Overstated Conclusion (Quantitative Evidence):** The data show an association or one sample, but the choice says always, causes, proves, or all. A graph can support; it cannot declare.

16. **Goes Beyond (Inference):** The conclusion adds information or leaps beyond what the passage logically forces you to accept. The most common inference error.

17. **Skips a Step (Inference):** The conclusion requires an intermediate assumption that the passage never provides (artifact movement does not prove people movement).

18. **Extreme Language (Inference & Uncertainty):** The answer uses always, never, all, none, proves, definitely when the passage is measured or tentative.

19. **Half-Right Trap:** The answer has one clause that is supported and one that is not. Every clause of the answer must be justified. Common in evidence questions where the evidence covers only one component of a two-component claim.

20. **Overstates Certainty (Uncertainty):** Replaces the passage's hedging language (suggests, may, appears, consistent with) with stronger language (proves, will, demonstrates, causes). The #1 uncertainty trap.

21. **Understates Certainty (Uncertainty):** Hedges a claim the passage presents as solid (rare but tested). Downgrades "47 trials found consistent effectiveness" to "preliminary evidence."

22. **Ignores Conditional Language (Uncertainty):** Treats a conditional finding (assuming X, provided that Y, under these conditions) as unconditional or universally applicable.

23. **Plausible-but-Unsupported:** The answer sounds reasonable and intelligent but has no direct textual anchor. Especially common in inference and main-idea questions.

24. **Misleading Keyword:** Uses vocabulary from the passage but builds a different or inverted claim around it (same words, flipped relationship).

25. **Circular Evidence (Pair Format):** In a paired question set, selects a claim in Q1 that does not match the evidence chosen in Q2, or vice versa. Do the explicit cross-check.

26. **Restatement-As-Inference:** The answer merely restates an obvious fact from the passage instead of drawing the inference the comparison or pattern supports. The correct inference is one level up: it explains the pattern.

## Difficulty-Progression Practice Set

Try these before checking the answer key. Each one applies a method from this file. Answers and one-line explanations are at the end.

1. **[Easy] Central idea.** "A study of 400 remote workers found that those who set a fixed end time for their workday reported significantly lower stress than those who worked until tasks were finished. The researchers also found that the fixed-end-time group was more likely to take regular breaks. The study's authors conclude that clear boundaries between work and personal time may reduce stress among remote workers."
Which choice best states the main idea? (A) Remote workers who take regular breaks report lower stress. (B) Setting a fixed end time for the workday was associated with lower stress among remote workers. (C) Remote work is more stressful than office work. (D) All remote workers should set fixed end times for their workdays.

2. **[Easy] Detail.** Using the same passage: According to the text, what did the fixed-end-time group report? (A) Lower stress than the group that worked until tasks were finished. (B) Higher productivity than the group that worked until tasks were finished. (C) A preference for working in an office. (D) Lower stress than they had felt before starting remote work.

3. **[Medium] Textual evidence.** "In her memoir, the narrator describes her childhood home as a place of both warmth and constraint: ______" Which quotation best illustrates the claim? (A) "The kitchen smelled of bread, and the door was never locked." (B) "We were loved, but the rules were iron, and the windows were kept shut." (C) "My sister and I shared a room the color of lemons." (D) "The house sat at the end of a dirt road, half a mile from the nearest neighbor."

4. **[Medium] Quantitative evidence.** Table: "Average Commute Time by City (minutes)" - City A: 22, City B: 34, City C: 45, City D: 41. Passage: "Commuters in the region reported widely different average commute times depending on the city: ______" Which choice best completes the statement? (A) City B's average commute was 34 minutes. (B) City C's average commute was 45 minutes, while City A's was 22 minutes. (C) City D's average commute was 41 minutes, 4 minutes less than City C's. (D) The average commute in all four cities was under 50 minutes.

5. **[Medium] Inference.** "A museum acquired a painting attributed to a 17th-century master. Carbon dating of the canvas places its production between 1650 and 1670, which matches the master's known working years. However, the museum's conservators found that the pigment used for the sky was not commercially available until 1720. If both findings are correct, it would most logically follow that ______" (A) the painting was produced by the master's students after his death. (B) the sky of the painting was painted no earlier than 1720, which is after the master's known working years. (C) carbon dating of canvas is unreliable. (D) the master sometimes worked with pigments that were not yet commercially available.

6. **[Hard] Uncertainty.** "Analysis of seismic data suggests that the fault line may be capable of producing a major earthquake, though the authors caution that the historical record is too short to estimate the probability reliably." Which choice best reflects the certainty level? (A) The fault line will produce a major earthquake. (B) The fault line could produce a major earthquake, but the probability cannot be reliably estimated. (C) The fault line has produced major earthquakes in the past. (D) The fault line is definitely capable of producing a major earthquake soon.

7. **[Hard] Textual evidence - weaken.** "Public health researchers hypothesized that offering free bus passes to low-income residents would increase their use of public transit. To test this, they distributed free passes to a randomly selected group and tracked transit use for six months." Which finding, if true, would most weaken the hypothesis? (A) Transit use increased by 30% among pass holders. (B) Transit use among pass holders was identical to use among residents who paid full fare. (C) Most pass holders reported liking the convenience of the buses. (D) Transit use increased among all residents, including those without passes.

8. **[Very Hard] Quantitative evidence.** Line graph: "Annual Rainfall in Two Regions (2015-2024)" - Region X: 2015: 40 cm, 2018: 52 cm, 2021: 38 cm, 2024: 70 cm. Region Y: 2015: 30 cm, 2018: 45 cm, 2021: 35 cm, 2024: 55 cm. Passage: "Researchers comparing rainfall trends noted that while both regions fluctuated, Region X showed a stronger long-term increase over the decade: ______" Which choice best completes the statement? (A) Region X received 70 cm of rain in 2024, its highest total of the decade. (B) Region X's rainfall rose from 40 cm in 2015 to 70 cm in 2024, an increase of 30 cm, compared with Region Y's increase of 25 cm. (C) Region Y's rainfall rose by 25 cm over the decade, a larger increase than Region X's. (D) Both regions received more rain in 2024 than in 2015.

**Answer key:**
1. B (A is too narrow - one finding; C is outside knowledge; D overreaches with "should").
2. A (direct paraphrase of the first sentence; B, C, D are inventions).
3. B (contains both "warmth" - loved - and "constraint" - rules were iron, windows shut; A is warmth only, C and D are setting details).
4. B (the claim needs a comparison showing wide variation; B contrasts the longest and shortest; A and D are single facts, C is a small difference).
5. B (the canvas dates to 1650-1670, but the sky pigment was unavailable until 1720, so the sky cannot have been painted during the master's known working years; A invents students, C overreaches, D contradicts the pigment availability fact).
6. B (matches "suggests" + "may" + "too short to estimate"; A and D overstate, C adds history not given).
7. B (identical use means the free passes changed nothing - directly contradicts the hypothesis; A supports, C is irrelevant, D is ambiguous but weaker than B).
8. B (the claim asks which region had the stronger long-term INCREASE: X rose from 40 to 70 cm (+30), while Y rose from 30 to 55 cm (+25), so X's increase is larger; A cites the endpoint but no change, C reverses the comparison, D is true but addresses neither region's increase specifically).

## Final Self-Check

Before you finish any Information and Ideas question, run this checklist:

**Central ideas and details:** Did I state the main idea in my own words first? Does my main idea cover the whole passage, not one paragraph? Does my detail answer paraphrase the exact sentence, or does it just repeat the passage's vocabulary with a twisted relationship?

**Textual evidence:** Did I identify the EXACT claim (and every component of it) before looking at choices? Does my evidence directly anchor that claim, or only relate to the topic? Did I respect the direction word (support vs weaken)? Does my evidence avoid overstating the passage's certainty?

**Quantitative evidence:** Did I read the title, labels, and units first? Did I translate the claim into a data request? Is my chosen value accurate to the graphic? Does it support the SPECIFIC claim (not a true-but-different one)? Did I compute the change when the claim asks for change, and respect thresholds like "majority"?

**Inferences:** Did I list the facts and ask what MUST follow? Did I say the conclusion in my own words first? Does the negation test break the passage for my choice? Am I within one logical step, with no outside knowledge and no extreme language?

**Uncertainty:** Does my answer's strength match the passage's hedging? Did I drop any condition (assuming, provided that)? If the passage says "suggests" or "consistent with," does my answer say "proves" or "causes" anywhere? If yes, eliminate it.

**Timing:** You have about 71 seconds per question on average. Short passages should take 45 to 60 seconds. If a question is taking over 90 seconds, mark it, guess, and return - there is no penalty for guessing, and the adaptive module rewards finishing the questions you can answer quickly.
