# MOST COMMON QUESTION TYPES ON THE ACTUAL EXAM

College Board's own test blueprint puts Standard English Conventions at **approximately 26% of the Reading and Writing section: 11-15 questions**. The official breakdown across the two skill areas:

| Official category | Share of SEC questions | Approx. questions | What it looks like on screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boundaries** | ~35% | 4-6 | Punctuation at a blank: period vs semicolon vs comma vs colon vs dash vs nothing |
| **Form, Structure, and Sense** | ~65% | 7-9 | Word-form choices at a blank: verb forms, pronouns, possessives, modifiers |

Within those two buckets, released College Board questions (the Assessment Framework's own sample set plus official practice tests) show this frequency ranking of the *named* testing points:

1. **Verb Finiteness (A4.2c) and Verb Tense/Aspect (A4.2d)** - the most frequently tested FSS points. Every test has several "choose the right verb form" questions, including the finite-verb-vs-participle trap and the "had done vs was doing vs had been doing" sequencing choices.
2. **Within Sentences (A4.1b)** - the biggest Boundaries bucket: comma splices, FANBOYS vs conjunctive adverbs, supplements (appositives, parentheticals), lists, and the officially confirmed "no punctuation needed" case.
3. **Subject-Verb Agreement (A4.2a)** - almost always with an inserted phrase (prepositional phrase, appositive, or clause) between subject and verb.
4. **Between Sentences (A4.1a)** - period/semicolon/question-mark boundaries, including direct vs indirect questions.
5. **Genitives and Plurals (A4.2f)** - its/it's, their/they're/there, your/you're, plural vs possessive apostrophes.
6. **Subject-Modifier Placement (A4.2e)** - dangling and misplaced modifiers, often as the opening-phrase pattern.
7. **Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement (A4.2b)** - number agreement plus reference clarity.

**Practical expectation for test day:** in a typical 27-question module you will see roughly 6-7 SEC questions. Expect 2-3 Boundaries questions (one of them almost always a "no punctuation needed" case) and 4-5 FSS questions (usually including one verb-finiteness, one tense/aspect, and one agreement or pronoun question). The remaining two skill areas of the section (Craft & Structure, Information & Ideas, plus Transitions/Rhetorical Synthesis under Expression of Ideas) fill out the rest of the module.

# MASTER RULE SHEET (memorize this page first  -  everything below explains and justifies it)

**Clause types:**
- **IC** = subject + finite verb + complete thought, stands alone.
- **DC** = subject + finite verb + cannot stand alone (opens with a subordinator).

**Joining two [IC]s (all four are *grammatically legal* for joining two independent clauses):**
```
IC . IC      |   IC ? IC (if first is a question)   |   IC ; IC   |   IC , FANBOYS IC   |   IC ; conj. adverb , IC
```
> These are not "equivalent laws." They are *legal options*. Whether one is the unique correct answer depends on what the other choices look like  -  see §1.2.

**Joining [IC] + [DC]:**
```
DC , IC          (dependent clause FIRST → comma after it)
IC DC            (dependent clause SECOND → often no comma if essential/tightly linked)
```

**Colon / single dash:**
```
IC : (list, explanation, elaboration, OR a full IC  -  the right side has NO completeness requirement)
```
> Colon and dash are *stylistic* cousins, not identical. The SAT can test the difference (see §1.4).

**Supplements (non-essential info), matching pairs only:**
```
,... ,      -  ...  -      ( ... )     never mixed
```

**FANBOYS** (comma-joinable): for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so
**Conjunctive adverbs** (semicolon/period required): however, therefore, moreover, thus, consequently, nevertheless, furthermore

**No punctuation needed** when: (a) subject and verb are never split by a comma regardless of subject length, (b) compound predicate sharing one subject gets no comma.

**Subject-verb:** verb agrees with TRUE subject, ignore all inserted phrases
**Pronoun-antecedent:** agree in number; singular "they" accepted; check ambiguity, distance, dummy pronouns
**Verb Finiteness:** every independent clause needs exactly one finite (conjugated) verb. A gerund/participle/infinitive cannot power a clause alone.
**Comparatives (2 things):** *-er* / *more*  -  never both
**Superlatives (3+ things):** *-est* / *most*  -  never both
**Parallel structure:** same grammatical form across list/comparison items
**Modifiers:** must sit immediately next to the noun they describe; no squinting/ambiguity
- **Possessives:** it's = it is; its = belonging to it; whose = belonging to whom; who's = who is; their = belonging to them; they're = they are; there = adverb (place/existence); your = belonging to you; you're = you are

# PART 1: BOUNDARIES  -  FULL EXPANSION

> **Why College Board tests this:** Boundaries questions measure whether you can *see sentence structure*  -  the single most important skill for reading complex writing. They test whether you know which marks legally connect clauses and which create fragments or run-ons. This is structural legality, not style.

## 1.0 Finite Verbs First (learn this BEFORE the IC definition)

Before you can tell an [IC] from a [DC], you must recognize a **finite verb**  -  the part that makes a clause "real."

### The core distinction

| Type | Definition | Examples | Can power a clause? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Finite verb** | Conjugated for tense and agrees with its subject | *runs, ran, will run, has run, is running, was studied, are analyzed* | **YES**  -  every clause needs one |
| **Gerund** | *-ing* form used as a NOUN | *Swimming is healthy* (gerund as subject) | **NO** |
| **Participle** | *-ing* (present) or *-ed/-en* (past) used as an ADJECTIVE | *Swimming across the river, she smiled* (participle describing "she"); *The data collected were analyzed* (participle describing "data") | **NO** |
| **Infinitive** | *to* + base form, used as noun/adjective/adverb | *She wants to swim* (infinitive as object); *The best method to use is titration* (infinitive as adjective) | **NO** |
| **Bare infinitive** | Base form with NO *to*, used after certain verbs | *She made him leave* (not "to leave"); *We saw the results improve* | **NO** |

### Recognition guide

```
Running home.                → NOT a clause (participle, no finite verb, no subject)
She is running home.         → IC (finite verb "is running", subject "she")
To succeed in science.       → NOT a clause (infinitive phrase)
To succeed requires hard work. → IC (finite verb "requires", subject "to succeed")
The data collected.          → NOT a clause (participle "collected", no finite verb)
The data were collected.     → IC (finite verb "were collected")
```

**Why this matters:** students constantly think *"Running home, the dog barked"* has two clauses. It doesn't  -  *"Running home"* has **no finite verb and no subject**; it's a participial phrase. You can only call something a clause if it has a **subject + finite verb**. Teach this first so the [IC]/[DC] test below actually makes sense.

### The SAT Verb Finiteness pattern (FSS A4.2c)

This is an officially named testing point. The question gives a sentence with a blank, and choices include finite and non-finite verb forms. The decision framework:

```
Find the subject of the clause containing the blank.
        |
        v
Does that subject ALREADY have a finite verb elsewhere in the clause?
   YES --> blank needs a VERBAL (gerund, participle, or infinitive)
   NO  --> blank needs a FINITE verb (conjugated to match the subject)
```

**Worked example [Medium] (AF-style):**
*"Rabinal Achí [BLANK] the story of a conflict between two Mayan city-states and remains an important work of K'iche' culture."*
Choices: (A) tells (B) telling (C) to tell (D) having told
- Subject "Rabinal Achí" has no other finite verb in the clause.
- (B), (C), (D) are all non-finite → cannot power a clause alone.
- **(A) tells** is the only finite verb → **CORRECT**.

**Worked example [Hard]:**
*"The experiment, [BLANK] over a period of three years, confirmed the researchers' original hypothesis."*
Choices: (A) conducted (B) was conducted (C) conducting (D) to conduct
- Subject is "The experiment." The clause's finite verb is "confirmed." The blank sits inside a comma-set-off phrase between subject and its verb → needs a PARTICIPLE, not another finite verb.
- (B) "was conducted" is finite → **WRONG  -  would create two finite verbs in one clause**.
- (C) "conducting" is present participle → **WRONG  -  experiment doesn't conduct itself**.
- (D) "to conduct" infinitive → **WRONG  -  doesn't fit as adjective**.
- **(A) "conducted"** (past participle, passive) → **CORRECT**.

> **The critical insight:** Verb finiteness is the intersection of Boundaries and FSS. A sentence with only non-finite verbs (gerunds, participles, infinitives) and no finite verb anywhere is a **fragment**  -  this is a Boundaries violation. The Framework files it under FSS because the error is choosing the wrong *form*, not the wrong *punctuation*.

## 1.1 The Independent/Dependent Clause Test (Foundation of Everything)

### The definition, precisely
An **[IC]** has three properties simultaneously:
1. A **subject** (someone/something doing something).
2. A **finite verb** (conjugated for that subject  -  not a bare "-ing" or "to ___").
3. A **complete thought**  -  said alone to a stranger, it makes sense.

A **[DC]** has (1) and (2) but fails (3), almost always because it opens with a **subordinating word**: *because, although, since, while, if, when, unless, whereas, after, before, though, even though, whenever, wherever, that, which, who* (in some uses).

### Method 1.1  -  The Cover Test
```
Find the blank / punctuation mark.
        |
        v
Cover the RIGHT side. Read LEFT alone.
   Complete sentence? --YES--> LEFT is [IC]
        |NO
        v
   LEFT is [DC] (or fragment)
        |
        v
Cover the LEFT side. Read RIGHT alone.
   Complete sentence? --YES--> RIGHT is [IC]
        |NO
        v
   RIGHT is [DC] (or fragment)
        |
        v
BOTH [IC]? --> period / semicolon / comma+FANBOYS are all LEGAL
EITHER side [DC]? --> only comma (or nothing) is legal there
                      semicolon, period, colon are ILLEGAL
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy]:**
*"The experiment failed[BLANK]the team revised their hypothesis."*
- Cover right, read left: *"The experiment failed"*  -  subject "experiment," finite verb "failed," complete. → **[IC]**
- Cover left, read right: *"the team revised their hypothesis"*  -  subject "team," finite verb "revised," complete. → **[IC]**
- Both **[IC]** → legal: period, semicolon, comma+FANBOYS (e.g. ", so"). Illegal: comma alone (splice).

**Worked example 2 [Easy]:**
*"The experiment failed[BLANK]because the equipment was faulty."*
- Right: *"because the equipment was faulty"*  -  subject + finite verb, BUT opens with subordinator "because." A stranger hears "...because WHAT?" → **[DC]**
- Left: *"The experiment failed"* → **[IC]**
- One side **[DC]** → only comma or nothing legal. Correct: *"The experiment failed because the equipment was faulty."* (no comma; dependent clause is second and essential)

**Worked example 3 [Easy] (dependent clause FIRST):**
*"Because the equipment was faulty[BLANK]the experiment failed."*
- Left **[DC]** (subordinator "because"), right **[IC]**.
- Rule: **[DC]** first → comma after it. Correct: *"Because the equipment was faulty, the experiment failed."*

> **Trap:** position changes everything. **[DC]**-first almost always needs a comma after it. **[DC]**-second sometimes needs no punctuation, especially if essential (see restrictive/nonrestrictive in §1.5).

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"If it has a verb, it's a full sentence."* No  -  it must have a **finite verb AND a subject AND stand alone**. *"because it rained"* has a verb but is a **[DC]**.

> **Timing:** an IC/DC Cover Test should take **~15 seconds**. If you're spending longer, you're overthinking  -  just cover and read.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.1
- ✅ [IC] = subject + finite verb + complete thought. [DC] = fails the stand-alone test (subordinator).
- ✅ Cover Test: read each side alone. Both [IC] = `.` / `;` / `, + FANBOYS`. One side [DC] = comma or nothing only.
- ❌ "It has a verb so it's a sentence"  -  [DC]s have verbs but don't stand alone.
- ⚡ Subordinator word opens a side? → that side is automatically [DC].

## 1.1b The Question Mark as a Sentence Boundary (A4.1a)

A4.1a officially names three end-of-sentence marks: the **period**, the **semicolon** (used as a period-equivalent), and the **question mark**. The question mark is a full-stop boundary exactly like the period, with one addition: it signals that the sentence is a question.

### The three rules that actually get tested

**Rule 1 - A question mark can end a sentence, and the next sentence starts fresh.**
The question mark is a legal boundary between two [IC]s, exactly like a period:
- *"Did the experiment succeed? The data were conclusive."* - two [IC]s; the first ends with a question mark, the second begins with a capital letter.

**Rule 2 - Direct questions get a question mark; indirect questions do not.**
- **Direct (exact words):** *"The committee asked, 'Will the funding be renewed?'"* - the quoted words are a real question, so they end with a question mark.
- **Indirect (reported, not word-for-word):** *"The committee asked whether the funding would be renewed."* - reported speech takes a period, NEVER a question mark.

**Rule 3 - One question mark per sentence, placed by the logic of the outer sentence.**
- *"Did the researcher really say, 'The data are conclusive'?"* - the whole sentence asks; the quote does not. One question mark, OUTSIDE the closing quotes.
- *"The researcher asked, 'Are the data conclusive?'"* - the quote asks. Question mark INSIDE the quotes.

### Method 1.1b

```
Is the sentence (or the quoted part) actually ASKING something?
        |
        v
Is the question DIRECT (quoted verbatim, or the sentence itself is the question)?
   YES --> question mark at the end of the question (one mark total)
   NO (indirect/reported: "asked whether", "wondered if") --> period
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy]:**
*"The archaeologist asked [BLANK] the site had been disturbed before the team arrived."*
Choices: (A) whether (B) whether? (C) whether: (D) whether;
- The clause after the blank is reported speech ("asked whether..."), not a direct question.
- (B) "whether?" → **WRONG - question mark in an indirect question**. (C) "whether:" → **WRONG - colon needs a complete [IC] on the left; "asked whether" trails off**. (D) "whether;" → **WRONG - semicolon needs [IC]s on both sides; the right side is a [DC]**.
- **(A) "whether"** (no punctuation) → **CORRECT**: *"The archaeologist asked whether the site had been disturbed before the team arrived."*

**Worked example 2 [Medium]:**
*"Why did the expedition turn back early [BLANK] the team's supplies were nearly exhausted."*
Choices: (A) ? The (B) ? the (C) ; the (D) , the
- Left: *"Why did the expedition turn back early"* → **[IC]** (a question). Right: *"the team's supplies were nearly exhausted"* → **[IC]**.
- (B) "? the" → **WRONG - a question mark ends a sentence, so the next word must be capitalized**. (C) ";" → **WRONG - a semicolon would swallow the question; you cannot join a question and a statement while keeping the "?"**. (D) "," → **WRONG - comma splice**.
- **(A) "? The"** → **CORRECT**: *"Why did the expedition turn back early? The team's supplies were nearly exhausted."*

**Worked example 3 [Hard]:**
*"The dean asked [BLANK] the students had completed the prerequisite course."*
Choices: (A) if (B) if? (C) "if?" (D) whether or not?
- Indirect question again ("asked if...").
- (B) "if?" → **WRONG - question mark for an indirect question**. (C) → **WRONG - quotation marks with no quoted source**. (D) → **WRONG - same question-mark error plus needless wordiness**.
- **(A) "if"** → **CORRECT**.

> **Key trap:** "asked whether" and "asked if" are *indirect* questions - they report a question rather than asking one. Students hear a question in their head and reach for "?", but the only thing that matters is the grammar on the page: direct = "?", indirect = ".".

### Key Takeaways - §1.1b
- ✅ Question mark = period-equivalent boundary between two [IC]s; next word is capitalized.
- ✅ Direct question → "?". Indirect question ("asked whether/if") → ".".
- ✅ One question mark per sentence; a quoted question inside a statement takes the mark inside the quotes.
- ❌ "asked whether..." with a question mark = indirect-question error.
- ⚡ See "whether"/"if" after a reporting verb? → period, not question mark.

## 1.2 Choosing Among Period, Semicolon, and Comma+FANBOYS (when BOTH sides are [IC])

### The accurate rule (this replaces the old "eliminate both" shortcut)
Periods and semicolons perform the **same grammatical function** when separating two independent clauses: both are legal full-stop boundaries. **If two answer choices differ ONLY by a period versus a semicolon and are otherwise completely identical, they cannot both be the single correct answer**  -  the SAT has exactly one right choice.

**But never eliminate them automatically.** First confirm the rest of each choice is truly identical. Very often the choices that *look* like "just period vs semicolon" actually differ in:
- **capitalization** of the next word (period forces a capital; semicolon does not),
- **a transition word** present in one but not the other,
- **different wording** elsewhere in the choice.

```
Two choices:   (A) failed. The team ...     (B) failed; the team ...
Ask: are they IDENTICAL except for . vs ; ?
   YES  → they cannot BOTH be correct; the real answer lies elsewhere
          (look for a comma+FANBOYS or semicolon+conj.adverb option you may have missed)
   NO   → compare the ACTUAL differences; one may be grammatically wrong for another reason
```

**Worked example [Medium]:**
Choices: (A) failed, the team (B) failed; the team (C) failed. The team (D) failed, so the team
- (A) comma alone joining two [IC]s → **WRONG  -  comma splice**.
- (B) semicolon joining two [IC]s → grammatically **legal**.
- (C) period joining two [IC]s → grammatically **legal**.
- (D) comma + "so" (FANBOYS) joining two [IC]s → grammatically **legal**.
- Among (B), (C), (D): none is "identical except period vs semicolon," so no automatic elimination applies. You now check *meaning/style*: if the two ideas are causally linked, (D) ", so" is the best fit; if merely sequential, (B) or (C). The SAT will make exactly one of these the intended answer by how the surrounding sentence reads.

**Worked example [Hard] (capitalization breaks the "identical" shortcut):**
Choices: (A) failed. The team (B) failed; the team (C) failed. the team (D) failed, the team
- (C) "failed. the team" → **WRONG - a period forces a capital letter; lowercase after a period is illegal**.
- (D) comma alone between two [IC]s → **WRONG - comma splice**.
- (A) and (B) are both grammatically legal and identical in wording. **When two choices are otherwise identical except period vs semicolon, they cannot both be the intended single answer** - so the real answer is whichever boundary the context supports. Here the sentence pairs a cause with its immediate consequence and the two clauses are tightly linked, which is exactly what a semicolon signals: *"The experiment failed; the team revised the hypothesis."*
- **Correct: (B)** - and the lesson is that you must check capitalization before declaring two choices "identical": (C) looked like a period version but was fatally different.

> **Biggest takeaway:** periods and semicolons are *functionally the same boundary*. Use that knowledge to **spot when they're being used as bait**, not as a mechanical delete key.

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"Period and semicolon are interchangeable, so cross them both out."* Wrong  -  they're only bait when **everything else is identical**. Most of the time they aren't, and one of them (or a comma+FANBOYS) is the real answer.

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds once you've run the Cover Test.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.2
- ✅ Period and semicolon = same boundary between [IC] + [IC].
- ✅ Auto-delete only when choices are IDENTICAL except `.` vs `;`.
- ❌ "Period and semicolon always interchangeable"  -  NO, they're bait only when identical-else. Check capitalization/transitions first.
- ⚡ If `.` and `;` both appear and nothing else differs → look for `, + FANBOYS` or `; + conj.adv.`  -  that's the real answer.

## 1.3 FANBOYS vs. Conjunctive Adverbs (Critical Distinction)

Both FANBOYS and conjunctive adverbs can express similar logic (contrast, cause, addition), so they *feel* interchangeable. Grammatically they behave completely differently.

- **FANBOYS** (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) are **coordinating conjunctions**  -  true glue. A comma + FANBOYS legally joins two [IC]s.
- **Conjunctive adverbs** (however, therefore, moreover, consequently, nevertheless, furthermore, thus, meanwhile, otherwise) are **adverbs**  -  they comment on a clause but have **no glue power**. Joining two [IC]s with just a comma before one is a **comma splice**.

### Method 1.3
```
Identify the transition word joining the two clauses.
        |
        v
Is it on the 7-word FANBOYS list?
   YES --> comma + word is LEGAL between two [IC]s
        |
        NO (however, therefore, etc.)
        v
   needs semicolon or period BEFORE it, comma AFTER it
   (", however," joining two [IC]s = comma splice = WRONG)
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy]:**
*"The UK issued polymer banknotes[BLANK] France still uses paper euros."* Testing "but" vs "however":
- **WRONG  -  comma splice:** *"...banknotes, however France still uses..."* (comma before "however" joining two [IC]s).
- **CORRECT:** *"...banknotes, but France still uses..."* ("but" is FANBOYS; comma+FANBOYS legal).
- Also valid: *"...banknotes; however, France still uses..."* (semicolon before, comma after).

**Worked example 2 [Medium]:**
*"The theory was elegant; however, it lacked experimental support."*  -  **CORRECT** (semicolon before "however," comma after). If a choice gave *", however,"* with just a comma before, that would be a splice and **WRONG**, no matter how natural it sounds spoken.

**Worked example 3 [Hard] (the "therefore" bait):**
*"The results were statistically significant[BLANK]therefore, the hypothesis was supported."*
Choices: (A) , (B) ; (C) . Therefore (D) , and
- "therefore" is a conjunctive adverb, NOT a FANBOYS word.
- (A) comma before "therefore" → **WRONG - comma splice**. (D) ", and" → **WRONG - inserts a word and makes "therefore" redundant; "and therefore" needs the semicolon-plus-connector structure, not this hybrid**.
- (B) semicolon → **CORRECT**: *"...significant; therefore, the hypothesis was supported."*
- (C) period + capital "Therefore" → also grammatically legal. If (B) and (C) are otherwise identical (same words, same capitalization apart from what the punctuation forces), they cannot both be correct - the semicolon keeps the two tightly-linked statements in one sentence, which is the better fit, so (B) is the intended answer.

> **The hard rule, stated cleanly:** On the SAT, two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction should be joined using a comma before the conjunction. Every other transition word needs the stronger semicolon/period boundary.

> **Real SAT trap  -  "What makes students choose B?":** your ear says "however" and "but" are interchangeable (casual speech comma-splices with "however" all the time). The test exploits that. Train your eye to catch the *mark before the word*, not the word's meaning.

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds once you've memorized the 7 FANBOYS words.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.3
- ✅ FANBOYS (7 words) = comma before, legal between two [IC]s.
- ✅ Conjunctive adverbs = semicolon/period before, comma after.
- ❌ `, however,` between [IC]s = comma splice (even though spoken English accepts it).
- ⚡ Spot a transition → is it on the FANBOYS list? No → needs stronger boundary before it.

## 1.4 Colons and Dashes-as-Colons (Introducing Explanations/Lists)

A colon (and a single dash used the same way) is an announcement: *"what follows explains, defines, or lists what I just said."* Because it announces, the part doing the announcing must be a complete [IC]  -  otherwise the announcement itself is broken.

> **Colon vs dash are NOT identical.** Both can introduce explanation/elaboration/list, but they are **stylistically different**: a colon is more formal/direct; an em dash is more dramatic/insertional. The SAT can test that difference  -  so treat them as *related but distinct*, never as a mechanical "eliminate both."

### Method 1.4
```
Is everything LEFT of the colon/dash a complete [IC]?  (Cover Test)
   NO  --> colon/dash ILLEGAL, no matter how natural it reads
   YES --> colon or single dash is legal
        |
        v
Check the RIGHT side: it has NO completeness requirement, BUT it must
DIRECTLY explain / define / list the left. Off-topic = misuse.
```

**The RIGHT side can be ANY of these, provided it explains the left:**
- a **phrase / fragment**: *"The team had one option: start over."*
- a **list**: *"The lab required three items: a centrifuge, a spectrometer, and sterile pipettes."*
- a **full [IC]**: *"The result was clear: the hypothesis was wrong."*
- a **single word**: *"There was only one problem: cost."*

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (legal, fragment on right):**
*"The team had one option: start over."*
- Left: *"The team had one option"* → **[IC]** ✓. Right: *"start over"* (fragment) directly explains the option ✓. Legal colon.

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (illegal  -  left incomplete):**
*"The team's only option was to: start over."*
- Left: *"The team's only option was to"* trails off ("was to" needs completion) → not **[IC]** ✗. **WRONG  -  colon after incomplete left side.** Fix: *"The team's only option was to start over."*

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (right side is also a full [IC]):**
*"The result was clear: the hypothesis was wrong."*  -  Left **[IC]** ✓; right also **[IC]** ✓ (colons don't require a fragment). Legal.

**Worked example 4 [Easy] (list):**
*"The recipe requires three ingredients: flour, sugar, and butter."*  -  Left **[IC]** ✓; right is a list directly explaining ✓. Legal.

> **Trap:** "such as," "including," or "for example" already introduce lists  -  do **NOT** also add a colon. *"ingredients such as: flour..."* is **WRONG**; correct: *"...ingredients such as flour..."*

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"The right side of a colon must be a complete sentence."* No  -  it can be a fragment, list, word, or full [IC]. Only the **left** must be a complete [IC].

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.4
- ✅ LEFT of colon/dash = must be a complete [IC].
- ✅ RIGHT = can be fragment, list, word, or [IC]  -  as long as it explains the left.
- ❌ Colon after incomplete left / colon after "such as"/"including" = always wrong.
- ❌ Colon and dash = NOT identical; the SAT can test the stylistic difference.
- ⚡ Cover Test the LEFT side only. If it doesn't stand alone, colon is illegal regardless of the right.

## 1.5 Supplements: Essential vs. Non-Essential (Restrictive vs. Nonrestrictive)

Some descriptive info is *load-bearing*  -  remove it and the sentence no longer identifies what/who it means. Other info is *decorative*  -  remove it and the sentence still points to the exact same noun. English punctuates these completely differently.

### Method 1.5  -  The Deletion Test
```
Identify the descriptive phrase/clause (usually right after a noun).
        |
        v
Delete it completely.
        |
        v
Does the sentence STILL identify the exact same specific thing?
   YES --> non-essential / nonrestrictive --> punctuation (commas or dashes)
   NO  --> essential / restrictive --> NO punctuation, ever
```

### Sub-case A: Relative clauses (that/which/who/whom)
**Worked example 1 [Easy] (essential, "that"):**
*"The notebook that is covered in doodles is mine."*  -  Delete *"that is covered in doodles"*: *"The notebook is mine."* If there's a pile of notebooks, now ambiguous (WHICH one?). → essential → **no commas**, "that" preferred.

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (non-essential, "which"):**
*"My laptop, which is covered in stickers, is on the desk."*  -  Delete: *"My laptop is on the desk."* Still clear (only one "my laptop"). → non-essential → **commas both sides**, "which."

> **Precise wording (not an absolute law):** On the SAT, restrictive clauses are *usually* introduced with "that" and nonrestrictive with "which." The **essential question is whether commas are required**  -  that's what the Deletion Test decides. If you see "which" with **no commas** around its clause, that is very likely the flagged error.

### Sub-case B: Appositives (noun phrases renaming another noun)
**Worked example 3 [Easy] (essential appositive):**
*"My friend Rebecca is coming to dinner."*  -  Delete "Rebecca": ambiguous if you have >1 friend. → essential → **no commas**.

**Worked example 4 [Easy] (non-essential appositive):**
*"J.R.R. Tolkien, a member of the Inklings, wrote The Hobbit."*  -  Delete: still identifies him. → non-essential → **commas both sides**.

**Worked example 5 [Medium] (the "single comma before a name" trap  -  documented SAT error):**
- **WRONG:** *"Researchers led by engineering professor, Vikram Iyer, have created..."*  -  Delete "Vikram Iyer": *"Researchers led by engineering professor...have created..."* doesn't stand alone (professor WHO?). Name is **essential** → **no commas**.
- **CORRECT:** *"Researchers led by engineering professor Vikram Iyer have created..."*

> **Trap:** a single comma before a name/title with nothing after it is a reliable red flag  -  genuine supplements need **matching pairs**, never one dangling comma.

### Method 1.5b  -  The Matching-Pair Check
```
Find the supplement's start and end.
        |
        v
Same punctuation opens AND closes?  comma...comma / dash...dash / paren...paren
   YES --> legal
   NO  (comma...dash, dash...paren, comma...nothing) --> WRONG
```

**Worked example 6 [Easy]:**
- **WRONG  -  mixed pair:** *"The result, much to everyone's surprise  -  confirmed the hypothesis."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The result, much to everyone's surprise, confirmed the hypothesis."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The result  -  much to everyone's surprise  -  confirmed the hypothesis."*

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"Commas and dashes do the same thing, so mixing is fine."* No  -  supplements must open and close with the **same** mark.

> **Timing:** ~20 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.5
- ✅ Deletion Test: delete the phrase → still identify the exact noun? → non-essential (commas).
- ✅ Essential → NO punctuation ever. Non-essential → matching pair.
- ❌ Single comma before a name ("engineer, Vikram") = missing the second comma  -  and the name is usually essential anyway.
- ❌ Mixed pair (comma open, dash close) = always wrong.
- ⚡ Matching-Pair Check: same mark opens AND closes the supplement.

## 1.5b No Punctuation Needed (College Board explicitly tests this)

The SAT regularly includes questions where the correct answer is **no punctuation at all**. Most students over-punctuate because they think every gap needs something. The Framework names this as its own sub-skill: *"recognize circumstances in which no punctuation is needed to set off sentence elements."*

### Trap 1: Never separate a subject from its verb  -  no matter how long the subject

Even a very long subject must flow directly into its verb with zero punctuation between them.

- **WRONG:** *"The reason the experimental results fell short of expectations, is that the sample size was inadequate."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The reason the experimental results fell short of expectations is that the sample size was inadequate."*

- **WRONG:** *"A study published in 2023 by researchers at Stanford University, found that the treatment had no measurable effect."*
- **CORRECT:** *"A study published in 2023 by researchers at Stanford University found that the treatment had no measurable effect."*

> **The rule:** If a comma sits between the subject and its verb, it's wrong  -  full stop. No exception.

### Trap 2: No comma before a compound predicate (one subject, two verbs)

When one subject does two things, the verbs are NOT separated by a comma.

- **WRONG:** *"The nineteenth-century novelist wrote extensively about industrialization, and criticized its effects on rural communities."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The nineteenth-century novelist wrote extensively about industrialization and criticized its effects on rural communities."*

- **WRONG:** *"The researchers collected the samples, and analyzed them in the laboratory."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The researchers collected the samples and analyzed them in the laboratory."*

> **The test:** If the second verb shares the same subject and there's no new subject introduced, the comma is WRONG. Only add a comma if what follows is a full [IC] with its own subject: *"The novelist wrote extensively, and her work influenced a generation of writers."* (here "her work" = new subject → comma legal).

### Trap 3: No punctuation before essential / restrictive elements

Already covered in §1.5  -  this is the same rule from the other direction.

### Method 1.5b
```
Identify the two elements around the blank.
        |
        v
Is the blank between subject and its verb?
   YES --> NO punctuation (ever)
        |
        NO
        v
Does the blank separate a compound predicate (one subject, two verbs)?
   YES --> NO punctuation
        |
        NO
        v
Is the blank before an essential element that identifies the noun?
   YES --> NO punctuation (see §1.5)
```

**Worked example [Medium] (long subject):**
*"An analysis of data from over two hundred archaeological sites across the region [BLANK] suggests that the civilization declined due to prolonged drought."*
- The blank falls between the subject ("An analysis") and its verb ("suggests").
- Any choice with a comma/colon/semicolon at the blank = **WRONG  -  subject-verb split**.
- Only the choice with nothing (or that cannot be seen as punctuation) is correct.
- **Correct:** no punctuation.

**Worked example [Medium] (compound predicate):**
*"The painter studied the frescoes of the Italian Renaissance [BLANK] and adapted their compositional techniques to her own work."*
- One subject ("the painter"), two verbs: "studied" and "adapted."
- No new subject appears before "adapted" → this is a compound predicate, not two ICs.
- Any comma at the blank = **WRONG  -  comma before compound predicate**.
- **Correct:** no punctuation.

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds. If you see a long subject before the blank, your first instinct should be "check for subject-verb split."

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.5b
- ✅ Subject and verb = NEVER split by punctuation (no matter how long the subject).
- ✅ Compound predicate (one subject + two verbs) = NO comma between them.
- ❌ "The study…, found that" = comma between S and V → always wrong.
- ❌ "The painter studied…, and adapted" = comma before compound predicate → always wrong.
- ⚡ Long subject before blank? → first instinct: check for subject-verb split.

## 1.6 Participial Phrases (the -ing/-ed phrase without its own subject)

A participial phrase describes a result or accompanying action but has **no subject of its own**  -  it borrows the main clause's subject. It is never an [IC], so it can ONLY attach with a comma, never a semicolon.

### Method 1.6
```
Spot "-ing"/"-ed" opening word with no subject attached.
        |
        v
Does it describe a RESULT/accompanying circumstance of the main clause?
   YES --> comma ONLY. Semicolon ILLEGAL (it's not an [IC]).
```

**Worked example [Medium]:**
*"Urban heat islands occur[BLANK]causing a spike in higher temperatures, increased air pollution, and heat-related illness."*
- *"causing a spike..."* has no subject → participial phrase (result of "occur").
- Only a comma legal: *"Urban heat islands occur, causing a spike..."*
- A semicolon would be **WRONG  -  semicolon joining to a non-[IC]**.

**Worked example [Medium] (past participle form in a full question):**
*"The artifacts, [BLANK] from the riverbed, were analyzed in the laboratory."*
Choices: (A) recovered (B) were recovered (C) recovering (D) to recover
- The clause's finite verb is "were analyzed." The blank sits inside the comma-set-off phrase between subject and verb.
- (B) "were recovered" → **WRONG - two finite verbs in one clause**. (C) "recovering" → **WRONG - artifacts don't recover themselves (active vs passive)**. (D) "to recover" → **WRONG - infinitive doesn't work as an adjective here**.
- **(A) "recovered"** (past participle, passive) → **CORRECT**: *"The artifacts, recovered from the riverbed, were analyzed in the laboratory."*

**Worked example [Hard] (having + past participle):**
*"[BLANK] the initial trials, the team redesigned the experiment."*
Choices: (A) Having completed (B) Completed (C) To complete (D) Complete
- The blank opens the sentence; the main clause is "the team redesigned the experiment" (finite verb "redesigned").
- (B) "Completed" → **WRONG - reads as if "the team" completed something or as a dangling passive; a bare past participle needs to sit directly next to the noun it modifies**. (C) "To complete" → **WRONG - infinitive signals purpose ("in order to complete"), which changes the meaning**. (D) "Complete" → **WRONG - finite imperative form creates a second clause with no connector**.
- **(A) "Having completed"** (perfect participle = "after completing") → **CORRECT**: *"Having completed the initial trials, the team redesigned the experiment."*

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.6
- ✅ Participial phrase = no subject of its own; describes the main clause's result or circumstance.
- ✅ Attaches with comma ONLY. Semicolon/period = illegal (it's not a clause).
- ❌ "…occur; causing" = semicolon before a participial phrase → always wrong.
- ⚡ -ing/-ed at blank? Does it have its own subject? No → comma only.

## 1.7 Lists: Simple Commas vs. Semicolons-as-Super-Commas

### Method 1.7
```
Count the items.
        |
        v
Does ANY item contain an internal comma?
   NO  --> plain commas between all items
   YES --> semicolons between MAIN items (super-commas)
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (simple list):**
*"We bought apples, bananas, and bread."*  -  no internal commas → plain commas.

**Worked example 2 [Medium] (complex list):**
*"...increased risk of anxiety; increased physical pain, including chronic migraines; and a negative self-image."*
- Item 2 has an internal comma ("including chronic migraines") → main items separated by semicolons so the reader knows where each item ends.

**Worked example 3 [Hard] (complex list with an inserted phrase inside an item):**
*"The museum's collection includes nineteenth-century French paintings[BLANK] works by contemporary African sculptors[BLANK] and, most notably, a set of ancient Greek vases."*
- Item 3 ("a set of ancient Greek vases") contains an internal insertion ("most notably") set off by commas.
- The main items must be separated by semicolons so the reader can tell where each item ends: *"...French paintings; works by contemporary African sculptors; and, most notably, a set of ancient Greek vases."*
- Keeping plain commas at both blanks → **WRONG - ambiguous item boundaries** (the reader can't tell whether "most notably" is its own list item).

> **Note:** the SAT accepts both the Oxford comma and no Oxford comma in a simple series (*"apples, bananas, and bread"* and *"apples, bananas and bread"* are both legal; the test never makes the Oxford comma the deciding issue). What IS tested is the semicolon upgrade when any item contains an internal comma.

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §1.7
- ✅ No internal commas in any item → plain commas between items.
- ✅ Any item has an internal comma → semicolons between main items.
- ❌ Semicolons when no item has internal commas = over-punctuation.
- ⚡ Scan each list item. Spot a comma inside one? → switch to semicolons.

## 1.8 Quotation Punctuation (integrating a direct quote)

Quotation questions test four things: (1) the comma before a direct quote, (2) where commas and periods sit relative to closing quotation marks, (3) where question marks sit, and (4) how a quote is introduced (comma vs "that" vs colon).

### Method 1.8

```
1. Is the quote introduced by "that"? → NO comma before the quote.
2. Otherwise, does a short lead-in flow directly into a quoted phrase? → comma before the opening mark.
3. Commas and periods: ALWAYS inside the closing quotation marks (American usage).
4. Question marks and exclamation points: inside if they belong to the quote, outside if they belong to the surrounding sentence.
5. Quote inside a quote: switch to single quotation marks.
6. Full [IC] lead-in before a quote (e.g., "She offered one conclusion:") → colon is legal.
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] ("that" + quote, period placement):**
*'The report states that "the treatment showed no measurable effect."'*
- "that" introduces the quote → **no comma** before the opening mark.
- Period goes INSIDE the closing quotes.
- **WRONG:** *"...that \"the treatment showed no measurable effect\"."* - period OUTSIDE the closing quotes (British style) = wrong on the SAT.

**Worked example 2 [Medium] (comma before a direct quote):**
*"The director announced, \"The exhibit will open in March.\""*
- Direct quote follows a short lead-in → comma before the opening mark; period inside the closing quotes.
- **WRONG:** *"The director announced \"The exhibit will open in March.\""* - missing comma before a direct quote.

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (question mark placement - inside vs outside):**
- *"Did the researcher really say, \"The data are conclusive\"?"* - the whole sentence asks; the quote does not → question mark OUTSIDE the closing quotes.
- *"The researcher asked, \"Are the data conclusive?\""* - the quote asks → question mark INSIDE the closing quotes.

**Worked example 4 [Hard] (quote inside a quote):**
*"The critic wrote, \"The novel's narrator observes, 'Memory is unreliable,' and then abandons the idea.\""*
- Outer quote: double marks. Inner quote: single marks. The comma after "unreliable" goes inside the single closing mark (American style).

**Worked example 5 [Hard] (colon before a long quote):**
*"The researcher offered one conclusion: \"The findings do not support the initial hypothesis.\""*
- The lead-in ("The researcher offered one conclusion") is a complete [IC] → colon before the quote is legal. A comma would also be acceptable; a semicolon would be **WRONG** (a semicolon can't introduce a quotation this way).

**Worked example 6 [Medium] (the original-style SAT pattern):**
*'...claims that "El Niño could lead to new records for temperatures," an indication that abnormal weather patterns may be escalating.'*
- Comma inside the closing quote (after "temperatures") - American usage.
- *"an indication that..."* = non-essential appositive, correctly set off with a comma (see §1.5).

### Key Takeaways - §1.8
- ✅ "that" + quote → no comma before the quote.
- ✅ Commas and periods always INSIDE closing quotation marks (American usage).
- ✅ Question marks: inside if the quote asks, outside if the sentence asks.
- ✅ Quote inside a quote → single marks for the inner quote.
- ✅ Full-[IC] lead-in → colon before the quote is legal.
- ❌ Period/comma outside closing quotes (British style) = wrong on the SAT.
- ⚡ See "that" right before the quote? → a comma before the quote would be WRONG.

# PART 1  -  MIXED PRACTICE (Boundaries)

*Each item: one correct answer, three wrong, with error labels.*

**1 [Easy]** *"The council approved the plan[BLANK]the mayor signed it the same day."*
- (A) , (B) ; (C) . (D) , and
- Cover Test: both **[IC]**. (A) comma alone → **WRONG  -  comma splice**. (B) semicolon → legal. (C) period → legal. (D) comma+FANBOYS → legal. When period/semicolon look like bait (else identical), the intended answer is usually the connector that fits the logic; here the ideas are sequential, so (B) or (C). (D) adds "and" not in original, so the cleanest is **(B) semicolon** (or (C) period). → **Correct: (B)**.

**2 [Medium]** *"Published in 2023[BLANK]the study analyzed data from over two hundred archaeological sites."*
- (A) , (B) ; (C) . (D) because
- Left *"Published in 2023"* = participial phrase, **not** a clause. (B) semicolon → **WRONG  -  semicolon needs an [IC] on both sides**. (C) period → **WRONG  -  creates fragment before it**. (D) "because" → **WRONG  -  would make left a [DC] but it has no subject+finite verb**. (A) comma → **CORRECT** (participial phrase + comma + [IC]). → **Correct: (A)**.

**3 [Medium]** *"The study was conclusive[BLANK]the hypothesis was rejected."*
- (A) , however, (B) ; however, (C) ; because (D) , and
- Both **[IC]**. (A) ", however," → **WRONG  -  comma splice (conj. adverb after bare comma)**. (C) "; because" → **WRONG  -  "because" makes right a [DC], illegal after semicolon**. (D) ", and" → legal but illogical (study conclusive yet hypothesis rejected = contrast, not addition). (B) "; however," → **CORRECT** (semicolon before conj. adverb, comma after). → **Correct: (B)**.

**4 [Hard]** *"The festival - which attracts thousands[BLANK]will begin at noon."*
- (A) , (B)  -  (C) ; (D) .
- *"which attracts thousands"* is non-essential → needs matching dash (already opened with ` - `). (A) comma → **WRONG  -  mixed pair**. (C) semicolon / (D) period → **WRONG  -  break a non-essential supplement illegally**. (B) dash → **CORRECT** (matching ` -  ...  - `). → **Correct: (B)**.

## Part 1: Boundaries at a Glance

| Situation | Legal | ❌ Trap | ⚡ Quick Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IC + IC** | `.` or `?` (if first is a question) or `;` or `, + FANBOYS` | comma alone = **splice** | Cover Test → both stand alone? |
| **IC + IC (transition)** | `.` or `; + conj.adv.,` or `, + FANBOYS` | `, + conj.adv.` = **splice** | Is word on FANBOYS list? |
| **DC + IC** | comma | semicolon/period = **fragment** before DC | Cover Test → one side fails? |
| **IC + DC (essential)** | nothing | comma = **breaks essential link** | Deletion Test → meaning lost? |
| **IC + DC (non-essential)** | comma | semicolon = **illegal** | Deletion Test → still clear? |
| **Colon or dash** | left = [IC], right explains left | colon after **incomplete** left | Cover Test left side only |
| **Non-essential aside** | matching `,…` or ` - … - ` or `(…)` | **mixed pair** or one bare comma | Matching-Pair Check |
| **Participial phrase** | comma only | semicolon = **illegal** (no subject) | Does -ing/-ed have its own subject? |
| **List** | commas (simple); `;` (complex) | `;` without internal commas = **wrong** | Scan items for internal commas |
| **Subject-verb split** | **NO** punctuation | any punctuation = **illegal** | Is blank between S and its V? |
| **Compound predicate** | **NO** punctuation between verbs | comma = **wrong** | Two verbs, one subject? |

> **Master pattern:** 90% of boundaries errors reduce to: (1) not running the Cover Test, (2) assuming a word is FANBOYS when it isn't, (3) forgetting that supplements need matching pairs.

# PART 2: FORM, STRUCTURE, AND SENSE  -  FULL EXPANSION

> **Why College Board tests this:** FSS questions measure whether you can enforce *logical consistency* inside a sentence  -  agreement, tense logic, pronoun clarity, modifier logic, and parallel form. These mirror the editing skills real writers use; the test isolates one variable at a time so the right answer is unambiguous.

## 2.1 Subject-Verb Agreement

### Why this rule exists
A verb's ending changes with its subject's number ("runs" vs "run"). The SAT lengthens the distance between subject and verb using insertions specifically to break your instinct.

### Method 2.1
```
Find the verb in question.
        |
        v
"Who/what is DOING this verb?" → trace to the true noun (not nearest noun)
        |
        v
Cross out EVERY prepositional phrase, appositive, parenthetical between them.
        |
        v
Match the verb to the TRUE subject's number.
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (prepositional phrase):**
*"The collection of artifacts [is/are] on display at the museum."*  -  Cross out "of artifacts." True subject "The collection" (singular) → **is**.

**Worked example 2 [Hard] (nonrestrictive appositive  -  real SAT trap):**
*"A Sheaf Gleaned in French Fields, a volume of English translations of French poems, [has/have] enhanced the poet's reputation."*  -  Cross out appositive (commas confirm non-essential). True subject = the title (ONE book, singular) → **has**. *"translations," "poems"* are plural nouns inside the appositive, invisible for agreement  -  that's the trap.

**Worked example 3 [Easy] (indefinite pronouns):**
*"Each of the students [has/have] a book."*  -  Cross out "of the students." "Each" = always singular → **has**.

**Worked example 4 [Easy] (inverted order):**
*"There [is/are] three reasons for this outcome."*  -  "There" is a placeholder; real subject after verb = "three reasons" (plural) → **are**.

**Worked example 5 [Medium] (compound with or/nor):**
*"Neither the coach nor the players [is/are] ready."*  -  Verb agrees with the **closer** noun: "players" (plural) → **are**. Flipped → "coach" (singular) → **is**.

**Worked example 6 [Easy] (collective nouns):**
*"The committee [decide/decides] on Fridays."*  -  Collective noun = one unit = singular (US usage) → **decides**.

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"The verb matches the nearest noun."* No  -  it matches the **true subject**, even if it's far away. The nearest noun is usually the trap.

> **Timing:** ~15–20 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.1
- ✅ Strip all inserted phrases (prep phrases, appositives, parentheticals) → match true subject.
- ✅ Indefinite pronouns (each, everyone, someone) = singular.
- ✅ or/nor → verb matches the CLOSER subject.
- ✅ Collective nouns (team, committee) = singular (US).
- ❌ "Verb matches the nearest noun"  -  the nearest noun is usually the trap.
- ⚡ "Who/what does this verb?" → trace to the true noun, ignoring everything in between.

**Reference Table  -  Subject-Verb Agreement**

| Subject type | Number | Example |
|---|---|---|
| each, either, neither, one | singular | Each is ready. |
| everyone, everybody, everything | singular | Everyone knows. |
| someone, somebody, no one, nobody | singular | Someone is here. |
| both, few, many, several | plural | Both are correct. |
| all, any, most, none, some (+ "of") | matches the "of" noun | Some of the cake is / Some of the cookies are |
| collective (team, committee, family) | singular (US) | The team wins. |
| compound with "and" | plural | The dog and cat run. |
| compound with "or/nor" | agrees with closer noun | Neither she nor they are wrong. |

---

### Five agreement traps the SAT repeats (add these to your checklist)

**Trap 1 - "as well as / along with / together with / in addition to / plus" do NOT make the subject plural.**
- **WRONG:** *"The director, along with the two producers, were at the premiere."* ("along with" is a parenthetical, not a conjunction)
- **CORRECT:** *"The director, along with the two producers, was at the premiere."*
- Compare: *"The director and the two producers were at the premiere."* ("and" truly joins → plural)

**Trap 2 - "The number of..." is singular; "A number of..." is plural.**
- **CORRECT:** *"The number of participants was small."* (the number = one number)
- **CORRECT:** *"A number of participants were absent."* (a number of = several)

**Trap 3 - "One of the [plural noun]..." takes a SINGULAR verb, but "...one of the [plural noun] WHO..." takes a PLURAL verb.**
- **CORRECT:** *"One of the students was late."* (subject = "one" → singular)
- **CORRECT:** *"She is one of the students who were selected."* (the relative clause "who were selected" modifies "students" → plural; "one of the students who was selected" would mean only one student was selected)

**Trap 4 - "What" clauses: the verb agrees with the complement that follows.**
- **CORRECT:** *"What the team needs is more funding."* (complement "funding" is a singular concept)
- **CORRECT:** *"What the team needs are clear guidelines."* (complement "guidelines" is plural)

**Trap 5 - "There is/are" with a list: match the first noun after the verb.**
- **CORRECT:** *"There is a pen, a notebook, and a ruler on the desk."* (first item singular → "is")
- **CORRECT:** *"There are three reasons and a footnote in the report."* (first item plural → "are")
- Some style guides allow "There are a pen, a notebook, and a ruler," but on the SAT the first-noun rule is the safe bet.

**Worked example [Very Hard] (full question format):**
*"The discovery, along with the artifacts unearthed at the site, [BLANK] scholars to revise their account of the region's early history."*
Choices: (A) has led (B) have led (C) lead (D) were leading
- Cross out ", along with the artifacts unearthed at the site," (parenthetical). True subject = "The discovery" (singular).
- (B) "have led" → **WRONG - treats "along with" as a conjunction**. (C) "lead" → **WRONG - plural form + present tense breaks the past context**. (D) "were leading" → **WRONG - plural + wrong aspect**.
- **(A) "has led"** → **CORRECT**.

## 2.2a Verb Tense, Form, and Mood

### Method 2.2a
```
List all verbs in the sentence/passage.
        |
        v
Is there a clear TIME logic requiring different tenses?
   NO  --> match tenses within the same timeframe
   YES --> use the right forms (incl. past perfect for the EARLIER past event)
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (unjustified shift):**
- **WRONG  -  tense shift:** *"The archaeologist examined the artifacts and publishes her findings."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The archaeologist examined the artifacts and published her findings."*

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (justified shift, signal present):**
- **CORRECT:** *"The excavation concluded last season, but the analysis will continue for several more years."* (explicit times named)

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (past perfect sequencing):**
*"By the time the research team [arrived/had arrived], the experiment [ended/had ended]."*  -  Two past events; experiment ended FIRST → "had ended" (past perfect); arrival stays simple past → *"By the time the research team arrived, the experiment had already ended."*

**Worked example 4 [Medium] (present perfect vs simple past):**
- *"The researcher has studied this species for over a decade."* (still studying) → present perfect.
- *"The researcher studied this species for over a decade [before retiring]."* (finished) → simple past.

**Worked example 5 [Easy] (irregular participles):**
- **WRONG:** *"The committee had went…"* / **CORRECT:** *"The committee had gone…"*
- **WRONG:** *"The author has wrote…"* / **CORRECT:** *"The author has written…"*

**Worked example 6 [Hard] (subjunctive):**
- **WRONG:** *"If the treatment was effective, more patients would recover."* (indicative implies it's NOT effective)
- **CORRECT:** *"If the treatment were effective, more patients would recover."* (hypothetical → "were" regardless of number)

### Aspect: the difference between "has done," "was doing," and "had been doing"

"Aspect" (officially named in A4.2d) is about *how* an action unfolds in time, not just *when*. The SAT tests four aspects against each other:

| Aspect | Form | What it signals | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple | runs / ran | a single event or habit | *The team ran the trial.* |
| Progressive | is/was running | in progress at that time | *The team was running the trial when the power failed.* |
| Perfect | has/had run | completed before now/then | *The team has run three trials this month.* |
| Perfect progressive | has/had been running | ongoing up to a point, then completed or interrupted | *The team had been running trials for two years before the funding ended.* |

**The decision method for aspect:**
```
1. Is the action IN PROGRESS at the reference time? → progressive (was doing)
2. Is it COMPLETED before the reference time? → perfect (had done / has done)
3. Is it BOTH ongoing AND completed at a point? → perfect progressive (had been doing)
4. Does a "since/for" phrase attach to an action still continuing? → present perfect (has done)
```

**Worked example [Medium] (perfect progressive):**
*"The researchers [BLANK] the population for a decade when the new census data were released."*
Choices: (A) had been monitoring (B) monitored (C) were monitoring (D) have monitored
- The action started before the past reference point ("when the new census data were released") and was ongoing up to it.
- (B) "monitored" → **WRONG - simple past doesn't signal the ongoing-up-to-that-point duration**. (C) "were monitoring" → **WRONG - progressive alone doesn't capture the "for a decade" completion**. (D) "have monitored" → **WRONG - present perfect must connect to the present, not to a past event**.
- **(A) "had been monitoring"** → **CORRECT** (past perfect progressive = ongoing up to a past point).

**Worked example [Easy] (present tense for general truths):**
- **WRONG - unjustified shift:** *"The study showed that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius and that the boiling point decreases as altitude increased."*
- **CORRECT:** *"...and that the boiling point decreases as altitude increases."* (scientific facts stay in present tense even inside a past-tense sentence)

**Worked example [Hard] (conditional sequence: will vs would):**
- **WRONG:** *"If the treatment will prove effective, doctors will adopt it."* ("if" + "will" is ungrammatical for a condition; the condition clause takes the present tense)
- **CORRECT:** *"If the treatment proves effective, doctors will adopt it."*
- **CORRECT:** *"If the treatment proved effective, doctors would adopt it."* (hypothetical past + "would")

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"Past events all use simple past."* No  -  when two past events are sequenced, the **earlier** one takes past perfect ("had done").

> **Timing:** ~20 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.2a
- ✅ No time-logic signal → keep all verbs in the same tense.
- ✅ Two past events: earlier one = past perfect (had done), later one = simple past.
- ✅ Present perfect = action starting in the past continuing now.
- ✅ Hypothetical/unreal condition → subjunctive "were" (even for singular).
- ❌ "All past events use simple past"  -  wrong when one happened before the other.
- ⚡ List all verbs in the sentence → is there a clear earlier/later sequence?

## 2.2b Verb Finiteness (Official AF A4.2c  -  Named Testing Point)

This is its own section because the Framework names it separately. The question is always: does the sentence need a **finite** verb (conjugated, powers the clause) or a **non-finite** verbal (gerund, participle, infinitive)?

### Method 2.2b
```
Find the subject of the clause containing the blank.
        |
        v
Does that subject ALREADY have a finite verb in the clause?
   YES --> blank needs a VERBAL (participle, gerund, infinitive)
   NO  --> blank needs a FINITE verb (conjugated to match subject)
        |
        v
Check commas: if the blank is between subject and its existing verb,
set off by commas → PARTICIPLE (not a second finite verb)
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy]:**
*"The theory [BLANK] three key predictions about the behavior of subatomic particles."*
Choices: (A) makes (B) making (C) to make (D) having made
- Subject "The theory" has no other finite verb in the clause.
- (B), (C), (D) are all non-finite → cannot power a clause.
- **(A) makes** is finite → **CORRECT**.

**Worked example 2 [Medium]:**
*"The sculpture, [BLANK] from a single block of marble, weighs over two tons."*
Choices: (A) carved (B) was carved (C) carving (D) to carve
- Subject "The sculpture" already has finite verb "weighs."
- The blank is in a comma-set-off phrase between subject and verb → needs participle, not finite verb.
- (B) "was carved" is finite → **WRONG  -  two finite verbs in one clause**.
- (C) "carving" → **WRONG  -  sculpture didn't carve itself (active vs passive)**.
- (D) "to carve" → **WRONG  -  infinitive doesn't fit as adjective modifier**.
- **(A) "carved"** (past participle, passive) → **CORRECT**.

**Worked example 3 [Hard] (the fragment trap):**
*"The author [BLANK] extensively about the ethical implications of artificial intelligence."*
Choices: (A) written (B) writing (C) wrote (D) having written
- Subject "The author" has no finite verb. The clause needs one.
- (A) "written" = past participle (needs auxiliary: "has written") → **WRONG  -  fragment**.
- (B) "writing" = gerund/participle → **WRONG  -  fragment**.
- (D) "having written" = participial phrase → **WRONG  -  fragment**.
- **(C) "wrote"** = finite, simple past → **CORRECT**.

**Worked example 4 [Very Hard] (gerund vs infinitive after certain verbs):**
*"The committee decided [BLANK] the funding for the remaining fiscal year."*
Choices: (A) to allocate (B) allocating (C) allocate (D) having allocated
- Subject "The committee" has finite verb "decided." The blank is an object/complement.
- Specific verb "decide" takes an infinitive complement ("decide to do"), not a gerund.
- (B) "allocating" → **WRONG  -  "decide allocating" is ungrammatical**.
- (C) "allocate" → **WRONG  -  bare infinitive doesn't fit after "decide"**.
- (D) "having allocated" → **WRONG  -  perfect participle doesn't fit**.
- **(A) "to allocate"** → **CORRECT**.

> **Key insight:** Verb finiteness bridges Boundaries and FSS. A sentence with only a participle and no finite verb is a **fragment** (Boundaries error), but the error is caused by choosing the wrong *verb form* (FSS error). The Framework files it under FSS because the tested skill is form selection, not punctuation.

> **Timing:** ~20 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.2b
- ✅ Every clause needs exactly one finite verb. Non-finite alone = fragment.
- ✅ Blank between subject and its existing verb → participle. No existing verb → finite verb.
- ✅ Memorize: decide to, refuse to, want to (infinitive) vs enjoy -ing, avoid -ing, suggest -ing.
- ❌ "written" / "writing" alone = fragment (needs auxiliary or conjugation).
- ⚡ Does subject already have a conjugated verb? Yes → verbal. No → finite.

## 2.3 Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement

### Method 2.3
```
Find the pronoun.
        |
        v
Trace to its antecedent (the noun it replaces).
        |
        v
Check:
  [1] Number match (singular/plural)
  [2] Ambiguity (could it point to >1 noun?)
  [3] Distance (is the antecedent too far back?)
  [4] Dummy pronoun? ("it" / "there" as placeholder  -  no antecedent needed)
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (singular "they" accepted):**
- Traditional flag: *"Each participant must submit their consent form"* (singular "each" vs plural "their").
- **Modern digital SAT accepts:** singular "they" when gender is unspecified → NOT automatically wrong. Traditional fix also valid: *"his or her."*

**Worked example 2 [Medium] (ambiguous referent):**
- **WRONG  -  ambiguous:** *"When the curator spoke to the conservator, she raised concerns about the painting's condition."* ("she" could be either curator or conservator).
- **CORRECT:** *"When the curator spoke to the conservator, the curator raised concerns about the painting's condition."* (specific noun).

**Worked example 3 [Easy] (NOT ambiguous):**
*"When Dr. Moreau spoke to the committee, she raised concerns."*  -  "she" can only be Dr. Moreau (specific person) → not ambiguous. The committee is a group, not a "she."

**Worked example 4 [Medium] (distance  -  antecedent too far):**
- **WRONG  -  unclear antecedent:** *"The report on the environmental impact of the new development near the protected wetlands was delayed because they needed additional review."* ("they" is far from whatever it refers to; likely "the researchers" or "the developers"  -  neither mentioned recently).
- **CORRECT:** *"The report on the environmental impact of the new development was delayed because the researchers needed additional review."*

**Worked example 5 [Medium] (dummy pronoun  -  no antecedent needed):**
- **CORRECT (no antecedent):** *"It is essential that the data be verified before publication."* ("It" = expletive/dummy, no antecedent needed  -  perfectly fine.)
- **CORRECT (no antecedent):** *"There are several explanations for this discrepancy."* ("There" = existential placeholder, not a real subject needing antecedent.)

> **Distinguishing dummy from real pronouns:** If you can replace "it" or "there" with nothing and the sentence still makes structural sense, it's likely a dummy pronoun. Example: *"It is raining"* → *"Is raining"* still works structurally. But *"The experiment failed because it was flawed"* → "it" refers to the experiment (real antecedent).

**Worked example 6 [Medium] (person consistency - a real digital SAT pattern):**
- **WRONG - pronoun shift:** *"If a student wants to succeed on the exam, you must practice daily."* ("student" = third person, "you" = second person)
- **CORRECT:** *"If a student wants to succeed on the exam, they must practice daily."* (or "he or she," or switch both to "you")
- **WRONG:** *"One can improve one's score by practicing, but you should also review the rules."* ("one" then "you")
- **CORRECT:** *"One can improve one's score by practicing, but one should also review the rules."*

> **The rule:** pronouns must agree with their antecedent in PERSON as well as number. Shifting from "students" to "you" or from "one" to "we" mid-sentence is an error even when the numbers match.

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"Two nouns nearby = automatic ambiguity."* No  -  check grammatically: if only ONE is compatible (e.g., a specific named person vs a group), it's fine. Also: if the pronoun is far from any clear noun, the error may be about distance, not ambiguity.

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.3
- ✅ Match number. Singular "they" = accepted for unspecified gender.
- ✅ True ambiguity = two grammatically compatible antecedents. Named-person vs group = usually NOT ambiguous.
- ✅ Distance: pronoun far from any clear noun → rewrite with specific noun.
- ✅ Dummy pronouns (it/there as expletives) = no antecedent needed.
- ❌ "Two nouns nearby = automatic ambiguity"  -  only if both grammatically fit.
- ⚡ Trace the pronoun backward. Points to exactly one clear noun? Fine. Zero or two? Wrong.

## 2.4 Possessives, Contractions, and Plurals

### Method 2.4
```
Find the word. Expand it to its two-word contraction (where applicable).
        |
        v
Does the expanded form make sense?
   YES --> contraction     NO --> possessive
        |
        v
For their/there/they're: check meaning, not just expansion.
   "their" = belonging to them (possessive)
   "they're" = they are (contraction)
   "there" = adverb meaning place/existence
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy]:** *"The study confirmed [it's/its] hypothesis."*  -  "it is hypothesis" nonsense → **its** (possessive).

**Worked example 2 [Easy]:** *"[It's/Its] essential that the data be verified."*  -  "it is essential" makes sense → **It's** (contraction).

**Worked example 3 [Easy]:** *"[Whose/Who's] conclusions were cited in the paper?"*  -  "who is conclusions" nonsense → **whose**.

**Worked example 4 [Medium] (plural vs possessive):**
*"The [students/student's/students'] projects were displayed in the [gallerys/gallery's/galleries'] main hall."*  -  multiple students, one gallery → **students'** + **gallery's**.

**Worked example 5 [Medium] (their/there/they're  -  three-way trap):**
- *"The researchers submitted [their/there/they're] findings for peer review."* → "belonging to them" → **their**.
- *"[Their/There/They're] are three possible explanations for the discrepancy."* → place/existence → **There**.
- *"[Their/There/They're] planning to replicate the study next year."* → "they are" → **They're**.

**Worked example 6 [Easy] (its vs it's in academic context):**
*"The theory has [it's/its] critics, but [it's/its] widely accepted among climate scientists."*
- First blank: "belonging to the theory" → **its**.
- Second blank: "it is" → **it's**.

**Worked example 7 [Easy] (your/you're - officially named in A4.2f):**
- *"[Your/You're] about to see the exhibit's final room."* → "you are about to" → **You're**.
- *"[Your/You're] hypothesis is the one we tested."* → "belonging to you" → **Your**.
- The Framework names this exact set: possessive determiners (its, their, **your**), contractions (it's, they're, **you're**), and the adverb (**there**). All three pairs are fair game.

**Worked example 8 [Medium] (plural vs possessive on irregular plurals):**
- **CORRECT:** *"The children's section of the library was renovated."* (irregular plural "children" + 's)
- **CORRECT:** *"The women's suffrage movement changed the nation."* (irregular plural "women" + 's)
- **WRONG:** *"The childrens' section..."* (there is no "childrens")

**Worked example 9 [Hard] (compound possession - shared vs separate):**
- **CORRECT (shared ownership, one 's at the end):** *"Rosa and Maria's research was published."* (one shared research project)
- **CORRECT (separate ownership, 's on each):** *"Rosa's and Maria's conclusions differed."* (each has her own conclusions)
- **WRONG:** *"Rosa and Maria's conclusions differed."* (reads as one shared set of conclusions, but "differed" requires two sets)

**Worked example 10 [Easy] (decades - the no-apostrophe plural):**
- **CORRECT:** *"The 1990s saw rapid growth in internet use."* (plural of a decade = no apostrophe)
- **WRONG:** *"The 1990's saw rapid growth..."* (apostrophe in a plain plural)

> **The apostrophe rule in one line:** apostrophe + s (or s + apostrophe) means OWNERSHIP or a contraction; a plain plural NEVER takes an apostrophe.

> **The their/there/they're distinction matters because the Framework explicitly names "there (adverb)" as a distinct tested item alongside possessive determiners and contractions.**

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.4
- ✅ Expand the contraction: does "it is" / "who is" / "they are" make sense? Yes → contraction. No → possessive.
- ✅ Plural alone = NO apostrophe. Ownership = apostrophe before s (singular) or after s (plural).
- ✅ their = possessive, they're = they are, there = place/existence.
- ❌ "it's = belonging" vs "its = it is"  -  most common homophone error on the SAT (reverse the real definitions).
- ⚡ Read aloud substituting the two-word form. Sounds natural? Contraction. Sounds wrong? Possessive.

## 2.5 Modifier Placement (Dangling, Misplaced, Squinting, and Ambiguous)

Four distinct modifier error types appear on the SAT:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Dangling** | The noun being modified isn't in the sentence at all | *"Walking home, the rain started."* (who walked?) |
| **Misplaced** | The modifier is next to the wrong noun | *"Cookies for children in plastic wrap"* (children aren't wrapped) |
| **Squinting** | The modifier could describe either what precedes or follows it | *"Students who study often get good grades."* |
| **Ambiguous** | Unclear which of two nearby nouns the modifier describes | *"She interviewed the candidate with experience."* (who has experience?) |

### Method 2.5
```
Identify the modifier (-ing / -ed / descriptive phrase / adverb).
        |
        v
DANGLING: Is the noun being described EVEN IN the sentence?
   NO  --> dangling → add the missing noun right after the modifier
        |
        YES
        v
MISPLACED: Is that noun the VERY NEXT word after the modifier?
   NO  --> misplaced → move modifier next to its intended noun
        |
        YES
        v
SQUINTING: Could the modifier apply to EITHER side?
   YES --> squinting → reposition to make reference unambiguous
        |
        NO
        v
AMBIGUOUS: Does the modifier logically fit more than one noun?
   YES --> rewrite to specify which noun
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (classic dangling):**
- **WRONG  -  dangling:** *"Walking to the excavation site, the rain started falling."* (rain can't walk)
- **CORRECT:** *"Walking to the excavation site, the team felt the rain start falling."*

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (dangling):**
- **WRONG:** *"Having studied the specimens for weeks, the results were conclusive."* (results didn't study)
- **CORRECT:** *"Having studied the specimens for weeks, the researchers found conclusive results."*

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (misplaced):**
- **WRONG:** *"The archaeologist displayed the artifact in the museum that was thousands of years old."* (sounds like museum is thousands of years old)
- **CORRECT:** *"The archaeologist displayed the artifact that was thousands of years old in the museum."*

**Worked example 4 [Hard] (squinting modifier):**
- **WRONG  -  squinting:** *"The researchers who study often publish their findings quickly."* (Does "often" modify "study" or "publish"? Two possible readings.)
- **CORRECT (repositioned):** *"The researchers who study often, publish their findings quickly."* (comma makes clear "often" goes with "study")
- **STILL WRONG:** *"The researchers who study frequently publish their findings quickly."* (just replaced "often" with "frequently"  -  same squinting problem)
- **CORRECT:** *"The researchers who study regularly publish their findings quickly."* (or: *"Researchers who frequently study publish their findings quickly."*)

**Worked example 5 [Hard] (SAT-realistic):**
- **WRONG:** *"Primarily celebrated for his masterpieces like the Mona Lisa, the scientific pursuits of Leonardo da Vinci are less well known."* ("pursuits" can't be "celebrated")
- **CORRECT:** *"Primarily celebrated for his masterpieces like the Mona Lisa, Leonardo da Vinci is less well known for his scientific pursuits."*

**Worked example 6 [Very Hard] (limiter placement - "only"):**
- **WRONG - misplaced limiter:** *"The new policy only affects the research division's summer interns."* (ambiguous: does "only" modify "affects," "the research division's," or "summer interns"?)
- **CORRECT (unambiguous):** *"The new policy affects only the research division's summer interns."* ("only" directly before the element it limits)
- The SAT tests this as an ambiguity question: the limiter (only, just, almost, nearly, even) must sit directly before the element it modifies. *"She almost drove the entire route"* (didn't drive at all?) vs *"She drove almost the entire route"* (drove nearly all of it).

> **Real SAT trap:** the cleanest fix is often a choice that gives the modifier its **own subject** (a full [DC]), sidestepping the dangling problem entirely.

> **Timing:** ~20 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.5
- ✅ Dangling: noun being described isn't in the sentence → the first noun after the comma doesn't fit.
- ✅ Misplaced: modifier sits next to the wrong noun → move it next to the right one.
- ✅ Squinting: modifier could apply to what precedes OR follows → reposition.
- ✅ Ambiguous: unclear which of two nearby nouns the modifier describes → rewrite.
- ✅ Best fix for dangling: give the modifier its own subject (full [DC]).
- ❌ "The results were conclusive…" with an -ing opener  -  can "results" perform the action? If no, dangling.
- ⚡ Find the first noun after the comma modifier. Can it perform the -ing action? No → dangling.

## 2.6 Parallel Structure

Parallelism requires items in a list, comparison, or paired construction to share the **same grammatical form**. The SAT tests this across multiple patterns.

### The five parallelism patterns

| Pattern | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---|---|---|
| **Nouns** | *The study examined migration, trade, and how colonies governed.* | *…migration, trade, and governance.* |
| **Gerunds (-ing as noun)** | *Reading, writing, and to calculate are core skills.* | *Reading, writing, and calculating are core skills.* |
| **Infinitives** | *To succeed, one must study, practice, and have determination.* | *…must study, practice, and persevere.* |
| **Clauses** | *The report noted that emissions rose, that temperatures increased, and the ice caps melting.* | *…that emissions rose, that temperatures increased, and that ice caps melted.* |
| **Adjectives** | *The treatment was effective, affordable, and had minimal side effects.* | *…effective, affordable, and safe.* |

### Method 2.6
```
Identify list / comparison / paired conjunction.
        |
        v
Strip each element to its grammatical skeleton (verb? noun? infinitive? gerund? adjective? clause?).
        |
        v
All elements must share the SAME skeleton.
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (gerund list):**
- **WRONG:** *"The artist's work involves sculpting, painting, and to create installations."* (gerund / gerund / infinitive)
- **CORRECT:** *"The artist's work involves sculpting, painting, and creating installations."* (all gerunds)

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (not only…but also):**
- **WRONG:** *"The policy was not only costly but also it was ineffective."* (adjective vs full clause)
- **CORRECT:** *"The policy was not only costly but also ineffective."* (both adjectives)

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (infinitive parallelism):**
- **WRONG:** *"The goal of the expedition was to map the coastline, document wildlife, and establishing a research station."* (infinitive / infinitive / gerund)
- **CORRECT:** *"The goal of the expedition was to map the coastline, document wildlife, and establish a research station."* (all bare infinitives after "to")

**Worked example 4 [Medium] (clause parallelism):**
- **WRONG:** *"The survey revealed that consumers preferred sustainable packaging, that they were willing to pay more, and prioritizing eco-friendly brands."* (clause / clause / phrase)
- **CORRECT:** *"…that consumers preferred sustainable packaging, that they were willing to pay more, and that they prioritized eco-friendly brands."* (all clauses)

**Worked example 5 [Hard] (logical comparison  -  "that of"/"those of"):**
- **WRONG  -  category mismatch:** *"The novels of Toni Morrison are more widely studied than William Faulkner."* (novels vs person)
- **CORRECT:** *"The novels of Toni Morrison are more widely studied than those of William Faulkner."* ("those of" = the novels of)

**Worked example 6 [Hard] (logical comparison  -  as…as):**
- **WRONG:** *"The temperature on Mars is not as moderate as Earth."* (temperature vs planet)
- **CORRECT:** *"The temperature on Mars is not as moderate as that of Earth."* ("that of" = the temperature of)

**Worked example 7 [Hard] (comparison structures):**
SAT-tested comparison word pairs:
- *as…as*  -  "The results were **as** significant **as** those of the previous study."
- *more/less…than*  -  "The experimental group showed **more** improvement **than** the control group."
- *rather than*  -  "The committee chose to expand funding **rather than** reduce it."
- *between X and Y*  -  "The study distinguished **between** correlation **and** causation."
- *compared with / compared to*  -  "The new method was more efficient **compared with** the traditional approach."

**Worked example 8 [Hard] (paired conjunctions: not only...but also):**
- **WRONG:** *"The policy not only reduced emissions but also it lowered costs."* (second half is a full clause; first half is a verb phrase)
- **CORRECT:** *"The policy not only reduced emissions but also lowered costs."* (verb phrase + verb phrase)
- **CORRECT:** *"Not only did the policy reduce emissions, but it also lowered costs."* (inversion + clause + clause)

**Worked example 9 [Medium] (either...or / neither...nor):**
- **WRONG:** *"The committee will either extend the deadline or they will cancel the project."*
- **CORRECT:** *"The committee will either extend the deadline or cancel the project."* (verb + verb after "either...or")

**Worked example 10 [Very Hard] (full question format):**
*"The museum plans to expand its collection, renovate its main gallery, and [BLANK] a new education wing."*
Choices: (A) to construct (B) constructing (C) construct (D) the construction of
- The list is "expand..., renovate..., and ___" - all bare verbs after "to."
- (A) "to construct" → **WRONG - breaks the bare-infinitive chain**. (B) "constructing" → **WRONG - gerund in a verb list**. (D) "the construction of" → **WRONG - noun in a verb list**.
- **(C) "construct"** → **CORRECT**.

**Worked example 11 [Medium] (the "colder than Miami" trap):**
- **WRONG - category mismatch:** *"The weather in New York is colder than Miami."* (weather vs city)
- **CORRECT:** *"The weather in New York is colder than that of Miami."* (or "than in Miami")

> **Pattern:** "that of" (singular) / "those of" (plural) are extremely common correct answers for logical comparisons where a thing is compared to a person or another thing of a different category.

> **Timing:** ~20–25 seconds (parallelism is often the hardest).

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.6
- ✅ Strip each item to its grammatical skeleton: all must match (nouns / gerunds / infinitives / clauses / adjectives).
- ✅ Use "that of" (singular) / "those of" (plural) to compare across categories (novels vs person).
- ✅ Paired conjunctions (not only…but also, either…or, both…and) = mirror form on both sides.
- ✅ Memorize pairs: as…as, more…than, less…than, rather than, between X and Y, different from.
- ❌ "Novels were more studied than William Faulkner" = comparing novels to person (missing "those of").
- ⚡ For each item: is it a noun? gerund? infinitive? clause? Are they ALL the same type?

## 2.7 Adjectives vs. Adverbs

### The complete linking verbs list (memorize)

| Category | Verbs |
|---|---|
| **Being** | *be, am, is, are, was, were, been* |
| **Sensory** | *look, sound, taste, smell, feel* |
| **State change** | *become, grow, turn, get, fall, go* |
| **Continuation** | *remain, stay, keep, continue, prove, appear, seem* |

After ANY of these, an **adjective** describes the subject  -  NOT an adverb.

- ✅ *"The solution seems simple."* (adjective describes solution)
- ❌ *"The solution seems simply."* (adverb  -  WRONG)

### Method 2.7
```
Find what the word describes.
        |
        v
NOUN? --> adjective.
ACTION/VERB? --> adverb.
        |
        v
Is the verb on the linking verbs list?
   YES --> describes the SUBJECT → ADJECTIVE (never adverb)
   NO  --> action verb → ADVERB
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (linking verb trap):**
- **WRONG:** *"The experimental results appear differently than expected."* ("appear" = linking verb → needs adjective)
- **CORRECT:** *"The experimental results appear different from what was expected."* (adjective describes "results")

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (action verb):**
*"The researcher writes [slow/slowly]."*  -  "writes" is action (not on the linking list) → **slowly** (adverb).

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (adjective far from noun):**
- **WRONG:** *"Monkeys, a traditional Mexican symbol, appear frequent in Frida Kahlo's paintings."* ("appear" here is action = "to show up," not linking = "to seem" → needs adverb)
- **CORRECT:** *"…appear frequently in Frida Kahlo's paintings."*

**Worked example 4 [Medium] (linking vs action  -  same verb, different meaning):**
- *"The biologist looked **carefully** at the specimen."* (action: "looked" = directed eyes → adverb)
- *"The specimen looked **strange** under the microscope."* (linking: "looked" = appeared → adjective)

> **The distinction matters because "appear," "grow," "look," "feel," and "prove" all have both linking and action uses. The SAT tests this.**

**Worked example 5 [Medium] (good vs well - the classic pair):**
- *"She performed [good/well] on the exam."* → "performed" is an action verb → **well** (adverb).
- *"The results were [good/well]."* → "were" is a linking verb → **good** (adjective).
- *"After the treatment, the patient felt [good/well]."* → "felt" is a linking verb → **good** (adjective; "well" is also legal when it specifically means "healthy").
- **WRONG:** *"The experiment went good."* → **CORRECT:** *"The experiment went well."*

> **Common Wrong Thinking:** *"Any word ending in -ly is the right choice."* No  -  after a linking verb you need an **adjective**, and many adjectives have no -ly (e.g., *slow, fast, hard, well* [adj: "I am well"] vs *well* [adv: "She writes well"]).

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.7
- ✅ Describes a noun? → adjective. Describes an action? → adverb.
- ✅ After linking verbs (be, seem, look, appear, feel, taste, smell, sound, become, grow, remain, stay, keep, prove): use ADJECTIVE describing the subject.
- ✅ Same verb can switch: "looked carefully" (action) vs "looked strange" (linking).
- ❌ "-ly = always right"  -  wrong after linking verbs (need adjective). Also: early, fast, hard, late are adjectives AND adverbs.
- ⚡ Is the verb before the blank on the linking list? Yes → adjective. No → adverb.

## 2.8 Comparatives and Superlatives

### Method 2.8
```
Count the things compared: exactly 2, or 3+?
        |
        v
2 things --> COMPARATIVE (-er / more)      NEVER both
3+ things -> SUPERLATIVE (-est / most)     NEVER both
        |
        v
Scan for double-marking: "more stronger", "most fastest" = ALWAYS WRONG
        |
        v
Check the comparison STRUCTURE: is it using the correct word pair?
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy] (double-marking):**
- **WRONG:** *"more stronger"* → **CORRECT:** *"stronger"* (short word, suffix alone)

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (superlative double-marking):**
- **WRONG:** *"the most tastiest"* → **CORRECT:** *"the tastiest"*

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (count the things):**
*"Between the two methodologies, the second was [more/most] reliable."*  -  "Between…and" = exactly 2 → **more** (comparative), NOT "most."

**Worked example 4 [Hard] (adverb comparatives):**
- **WRONG:** *"The treatment worked quicker than the placebo."* → **CORRECT:** *"The treatment worked more quickly than the placebo."* (-ly adverbs always use more/most)

### Comparison structures the SAT tests

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| **as…as** (equal) | *"The second trial was **as** conclusive **as** the first."* |
| **more…than** (greater) | *"The experimental group showed **more** improvement **than** the control group."* |
| **less…than** (lesser) | *"The new method was **less** effective **than** the traditional approach."* |
| **rather than** (preference) | *"The committee chose to extend the study **rather than** terminate it."* |
| **between X and Y** (two items) | *"The study distinguished **between correlation and causation.**"* |
| **compared with / compared to** | *"Compared with the baseline, the results showed a 30% improvement."* |
| **different from** (not "different than") | *"The experimental conditions were **different from** those of the prior study."* |
| **the same as** | *"The outcome was **the same as** we predicted."* |

**Worked example 5 [Medium] (as…as error):**
- **WRONG:** *"The results were not as significant than the previous study."* (mixes "as…as" with "than")
- **CORRECT:** *"The results were not as significant as those of the previous study."*

**Worked example 6 [Medium] (between X and Y):**
- **WRONG:** *"The study distinguished between correlation versus causation."* (wrong connector)
- **CORRECT:** *"The study distinguished between correlation and causation."*

**Worked example 7 [Medium] (full question format):**
*"Of the three methods tested, the second proved the [BLANK] reliable under field conditions."*
Choices: (A) most (B) more (C) most reliably (D) more reliable
- "Of the three" → 3+ items → superlative.
- (B) "more" → **WRONG - comparative is for two items**. (C) "most reliably" → **WRONG - adverb, but the blank describes the noun "method" ("proved" is linking here)**. (D) "more reliable" → **WRONG - comparative + wrong item count**.
- **(A) "most"** → **CORRECT**: *"...proved the most reliable under field conditions."*

> **The SAT tests both the form (comparative vs superlative) AND the structure (correct word pairs). A comparative that uses the wrong pair (e.g., "as…than") is as wrong as a double-marked one.**

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.8
- ✅ 2 items → comparative (-er / more). 3+ → superlative (-est / most).
- ✅ Never double-mark: "more stronger" or "most fastest" = always wrong.
- ✅ -ly adverbs → always more/most, never -er/-est.
- ✅ Memorize pairs: as…as, more…than, less…than, between X and Y, different from, the same as, rather than.
- ❌ as…than = mixes two structures → always wrong. "between X versus Y" → wrong connector.
- ⚡ Count: "between the two" = 2 → comparative. "of the three" = 3+ → superlative.

## 2.9 Subject vs. Object Pronoun Case

### Method 2.9
```
Identify the pronoun (often in a compound).
        |
        v
Remove the other person; test the pronoun ALONE in its position.
        |
        v
Which form sounds right alone? Use that.
```

**Worked example 1 [Easy]:**
*"[Dr. Chen and I / Dr. Chen and me] submitted the grant proposal."*  -  "Me submitted" wrong → "I submitted" right → **Dr. Chen and I**.

**Worked example 2 [Easy] (after preposition):**
*"The fellowship was awarded to [she and I / her and me]."*  -  "to she" wrong, "to her" right; "to I" wrong, "to me" right → **her and me**.

**Worked example 3 [Medium] (who vs whom):**
- *"[Who/Whom] did the committee recommend?"* → "The committee recommended him" → object → **whom**.
- *"[Who/Whom] authored the study?"* → "He authored the study" → subject → **who**.

**Worked example 4 [Medium] (whoever vs whomever):**
- *"The award goes to [whoever/whomever] submits the strongest proposal."* → the pronoun is the SUBJECT of its own clause ("whoever submits") → **whoever**.
- *"The committee will consult [whoever/whomever] it selects."* → the pronoun is the OBJECT of "selects" within its clause → **whomever**.
- **Trick:** look INSIDE the clause containing the blank, not at the word before it. In "to whoever submits...", the preposition "to" does not decide; "whoever" is the subject of "submits."

**Worked example 5 [Hard] (comparison with "than" - the hidden case test):**
- *"She is taller than [I/me]."* → the comparison implies a clause: "than I [am tall]" → **I** (subject case). In formal written English (and on the SAT), "than I" is correct.
- *"The scholarship surprised [she and I / her and me]."* → object position → **her and me**.
- **WRONG - hypercorrection:** *"The committee thanked Dr. Chen and I."* → "thanked I" is ungrammatical → **Dr. Chen and me**.

**Worked example 6 [Hard] (full question format):**
*"The fellowship was awarded to [BLANK]."*
Choices: (A) Dr. Chen and me (B) Dr. Chen and I (C) I and Dr. Chen (D) Dr. Chen and myself
- After the preposition "to" → object case.
- (B) → **WRONG - subject case after a preposition (hypercorrection)**. (C) → **WRONG - same case error plus naming yourself first**. (D) → **WRONG - reflexive "myself" cannot replace an object pronoun here**.
- **(A) "Dr. Chen and me"** → **CORRECT**.

> **Trick:** answer the implied question with "him" → whom; with "he" → who.

> **Timing:** ~15 seconds.

### Key Takeaways  -  §2.9
- ✅ In a compound ("X and me"), remove X and test the pronoun alone: "Me submitted" vs "I submitted."
- ✅ After a preposition (to, for, with, between) → object case: me, him, her, us, them, whom.
- ❌ "Dr. Chen and I" as object ("awarded to Dr. Chen and I") = hypercorrection → should be "Dr. Chen and me."
- ⚡ Answer with "him" → whom. Answer with "he" → who.

## 2.10 Coordination, Subordination, and Logical Fit

Not a named official point, but it appears in practice questions and overlaps with the Transitions domain (A3.2): the connector must match the REAL logical relationship between the two ideas. The punctuation can be perfect and the sentence still wrong if "because" sits where "although" belongs.

### The relationship menu (memorize which word = which logic)

| Relationship | Coordinating (FANBOYS) | Subordinating (DC opener) | Conjunctive adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cause/effect | so, for | because, since, as | therefore, consequently, thus |
| Contrast | but, yet | although, though, while, whereas | however, nevertheless |
| Addition | and, nor | (none common) | moreover, furthermore, also |
| Choice/alternative | or | whether...or | otherwise, alternatively |
| Time/sequence | (and) | when, while, after, before, once, until | meanwhile, subsequently |

### Method 2.10

```
Identify the two ideas.
        |
        v
Name the relationship in one word: cause? contrast? addition? choice? time?
        |
        v
Does the connector in each choice match that relationship?
   YES --> keep
   NO  --> WRONG (logic mismatch), even if the punctuation is flawless
```

**Worked example 1 [Medium] (cause vs concession - the most common trap):**
- **WRONG - logic mismatch:** *"Because the study had a small sample size, its findings were widely cited."* (a small sample should HURT citations; the real relationship is surprise/contrast)
- **CORRECT:** *"Although the study had a small sample size, its findings were widely cited."*

**Worked example 2 [Medium] (time vs cause - the double-duty word "since"):**
- *"Since the funding was approved, the lab has expanded."* ("since" = time: after the approval) - legal.
- *"Since the funding was insufficient, the lab scaled back."* ("since" = because) - legal.
- **WRONG:** *"Since the results were inconsistent, the team celebrated."* (neither a temporal nor a causal link connects "inconsistent results" to "celebrating" - "since" forces one)

**Worked example 3 [Hard] ("while" - time vs concession):**
- *"While the data were being collected, the team reviewed the literature."* (time: during)
- *"While the results were impressive, they fell short of expectations."* (concession: although)
- Both are legal. The error is using "while" where neither meaning fits, or using "whereas" for a time relationship (whereas = contrast ONLY).

**Worked example 4 [Hard] (full question format):**
*"The painting's colors have faded over the centuries [BLANK] its composition remains remarkably intact."*
Choices: (A) but (B) because (C) so (D) therefore
- The two facts contrast: faded colors vs intact composition.
- (B) "because" → **WRONG - implies fading caused intactness**. (C) "so" → **WRONG - implies fading caused intactness (result)**. (D) "therefore" → **WRONG - same causal misfit, and a conjunctive adverb would need a semicolon/period before it**.
- **(A) "but"** → **CORRECT**.

> **Why CB tests this:** it checks whether you read for *meaning*, not just mechanics - the punctuation may be perfect but the logic word wrong.

> **Timing:** ~20 seconds.

### Key Takeaways - §2.10
- ✅ Name the relationship first: cause, contrast, addition, choice, time.
- ✅ because/since/as = cause; although/though/while/whereas = concession; but/yet/however = contrast; so/therefore = result.
- ✅ "Since" and "while" are double-duty words (time AND cause/concession) - check which meaning the sentence supports.
- ❌ "Because the study was small, it was widely cited" = logic mismatch (needs concession, not cause).
- ⚡ Ask: does this connector make the two ideas relate the way the passage intends? Perfect punctuation + wrong logic word = still wrong.

## 2.11 Bonus Insurance: Quantity Words, Irregular Verb Pairs, Double Negatives

These are NOT named in the official taxonomy, and they are rare on the digital SAT. They are included because older practice materials and prep books still feature them, and a confident elimination on a rare item is cheap insurance. Learn them once and they cost you nothing on test day.

**Quantity words (fewer/less, amount/number):**
- *fewer* + countable nouns ("fewer participants"); *less* + uncountable ("less time").
- *number* + countable ("a number of studies"); *amount* + uncountable ("a large amount of data").
- **WRONG:** *"Less participants attended this year."* → **CORRECT:** *"Fewer participants attended this year."*

**Irregular verb pairs (lie/lay, rise/raise, sit/set):**
- *lie* (recline) / *lay* (place): "The documents lie on the desk" but "She laid the documents on the desk." Past of "lie" = "lay"; past of "lay" = "laid."
- *rise* (go up on its own) / *raise* (lift something): "Temperatures rise" but "The committee raised the limit."
- *sit* / *set*: "She sits by the window" but "She set the vase on the table."
- These show up as wrong answers in verb-form questions more often than as the tested point itself - knowing them lets you eliminate confidently.

**Double negatives:**
- **WRONG:** *"The team didn't find no evidence of contamination."* → **CORRECT:** *"The team didn't find any evidence of contamination."*
- **WRONG:** *"I couldn't hardly hear the speaker."* → **CORRECT:** *"I could hardly hear the speaker."* (or "couldn't hear")
- Two negatives in one clause = always wrong in Standard Written English.

### Key Takeaways - §2.11
- ✅ fewer/number = countable; less/amount = uncountable.
- ✅ lie/lay, rise/raise, sit/set: memorize the transitive (takes an object) member of each pair.
- ✅ One negative per clause.
- ⚡ Low-frequency items: master the named points first, use this section to eliminate.

## Part 2: FSS at a Glance

| Category | Test | ❌ Trap | ⚡ Quick Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Subject-Verb** (§2.1) | Strip insertions → match true subject | Nearest-noun trap | "Who/what does this verb?" → cross out prep phrases |
| **Verb Tense** (§2.2a) | Check time logic across all verbs | All-past-events-use-simple-past | Two past events? → earlier = past perfect |
| **Verb Aspect** (§2.2a) | In progress? completed? ongoing-then-completed? | has done vs was doing vs had been doing | Progressive = in progress; perfect = completed; perfect progressive = both |
| **Pronoun Person** (§2.3) | Pronoun shifts (you → one → we) mid-sentence | "students...you" | Match person AND number |
| **Verb Finiteness** (§2.2b) | Does subject have a finite verb? No→finite Yes→verbal | Participle alone = fragment | Find subject → is there already a conjugated verb? |
| **Pronoun-Antecedent** (§2.3) | Match number + check ambiguity + distance + dummy | Two nouns nearby = automatic ambiguity | Could pronoun point to >1 compatible noun? |
| **Possessives** (§2.4) | Expand contraction; test sense | it's=belonging, its=it is | "it is" → it's; *else* → its |
| **Modifiers** (§2.5) | Find described noun → check presence, position, distance | -ing always needs comma | Next noun after comma modifier? Does it match? |
| **Parallelism** (§2.6) | Strip items to their grammatical skeleton | All lists need commas; -ing vs infinitive mismatch | Each item: same verb form? same clause type? |
| **Adj vs Adv** (§2.7) | Linking verb? → adj (describes subject) | -ly is always right | Is verb on linking list? yes→adj, no→adv |
| **Comp/Superl** (§2.8) | Count items: 2→comp, 3+→super | more less = correct as...as, never as...than | "between" = 2; "of the three" = 3+ |
| **Pronoun Case** (§2.9) | Isolate pronoun in compound; test alone | Me and him = subject | Remove other person → test alone |
| **Coordination** (§2.10) | Match transition to logical relationship | because = always cause | Is the actual relationship cause, contrast, or concession? |

> **Master pattern:** Every FSS error comes down to a mismatch between two elements that must agree. Identify those two elements first, then run the sub-rule.

## Fully Solved Example Set (clean  -  one correct, three wrong, no repairs)

### Worked Question 1 [Medium] (Boundaries  -  comma splice)
*"The bridge began to sway violently[BLANK]engineers closed it within hours."*
Choices: (A) violently, engineers (B) violently; engineers (C) violently. Engineers (D) violently, and engineers
- **Step 3 (Cover Test):** Left = *"The bridge began to sway violently"* → **[IC]**. Right = *"engineers closed it within hours"* → **[IC]**.
- (A) comma alone joining two **[IC]**s → **WRONG  -  comma splice**.
- (B) semicolon joining two **[IC]**s → legal.
- (C) period joining two **[IC]**s → legal.
- (D) comma + "and" (FANBOYS) joining two **[IC]**s → legal.
- Period (C) and semicolon (B) are NOT identical-else (they differ in capitalization of "Engineers"); the choices here are otherwise distinct, so no auto-elimination. The sentence shows a sequence of events with a causal link → the best fit among the legal options is the connector that signals addition/causation. (D) ", and" is the cleanest, but note (B)/(C) are also grammatically acceptable in isolation. **On the real SAT, exactly one of these would be presented as the intended answer by the surrounding context**; the key skill is recognizing (A) is the only clearly *illegal* choice.
- **Correct answer (the clearly-defensible unique choice when context implies causation): (D)**  -  *"The bridge began to sway violently, and engineers closed it within hours."*

> **Lesson:** a bare FANBOYS with no comma joining two **[IC]**s is a run-on; the SAT presents it correctly bundled as ", and."

### Worked Question 2 [Easy] (Boundaries  -  dependent clause, no semicolon)
*"Although the results were promising[BLANK]the team decided to run additional trials."*
Choices: (A) promising; the team (B) promising, the team (C) promising. The team (D) promising the team
- Left = *"Although the results were promising"* → **[DC]** (subordinator "although"). Right → **[IC]**.
- (A) semicolon → **WRONG  -  semicolon before a [DC]**. (C) period → **WRONG  -  period before a [DC] (fragment)**. (D) no punctuation → **WRONG  -  run-on ([DC]-first needs comma)**.
- **Correct answer: (B)**  -  comma. *"Although the results were promising, the team decided to run additional trials."*

### Worked Question 3 [Medium] (Boundaries  -  nonrestrictive appositive, matching pair)
*"The bridge, [BLANK], has become a popular tourist attraction."*
Choices: (A) built in 1932 (B) built in 1932, (C)  -  built in 1932 (D) built in 1932  - 
- Sentence already opens with a comma ("The bridge, …"). The supplement must **close with the same mark**.
- (A) no closing → **WRONG  -  mismatched pair**. (C) opens with dash → **WRONG  -  mismatched with existing comma**. (D) closes with dash → **WRONG  -  mismatched**.
- **Correct answer: (B)**  -  comma to match. *"The bridge, built in 1932, has become a popular tourist attraction."*

### Worked Question 4 [Medium] (FSS  -  subject-verb with inserted phrase)
*"The results of the experiment, though initially met with skepticism, [BLANK] now widely accepted."*
Choices: (A) is (B) are (C) was (D) has been
- True subject: "The results" (plural) after crossing out "of the experiment" and the non-essential clause.
- (A) "is" → **WRONG  -  singular mismatch**. (C) "was" → **WRONG  -  singular + wrong tense**. (D) "has been" → **WRONG  -  singular auxiliary**.
- **Correct answer: (B)**  -  "are."

### Worked Question 5 [Hard] (FSS  -  modifier placement)
*"[BLANK], the museum's new wing was designed with sustainability in mind."*
Choices: (A) Committed to environmental responsibility (B) Environmental responsibility, being a top priority (C) With environmental responsibility as a commitment (D) As the architects were committed to environmental responsibility
- The modifier must describe the next noun: "the museum's new wing."
- (A)/(B)/(C) all imply the *wing* is "committed"/"responsible" → **WRONG  -  dangling modifier** (a wing can't hold commitments).
- (D) is a full **[DC]** with its own subject ("the architects") → no dangling.
- **Correct answer: (D).**

### Worked Question 6 [Easy] (FSS  -  comparative vs superlative)
*"Of the two proposals submitted, the second was considerably [BLANK]."*
Choices: (A) more efficient (B) most efficient (C) efficient (D) more efficiently
- "Of the two" → exactly 2 → comparative.
- (B) "most efficient" → **WRONG  -  superlative for 2 items**. (C) "efficient" → **WRONG  -  no comparison marker**. (D) "more efficiently" → **WRONG  -  adverb, but describes noun "proposal" (needs adjective)**.
- **Correct answer: (A)**  -  "more efficient."

### Worked Question 7 [Medium] (FSS  -  pronoun ambiguity)
*"When the professor reviewed the student's thesis, [BLANK] made several revisions."*
Choices: (A) she (B) they (C) the student (D) it
- Two compatible people present → "she"/"they" ambiguous; "it" impossible.
- (A) → **WRONG  -  ambiguous referent**. (B) → **WRONG  -  still ambiguous (which person?)**. (D) → **WRONG  -  neither is "it."**
- **Correct answer: (C)**  -  specific noun eliminates ambiguity.

### Worked Question 8 [Medium] (Boundaries  -  colon after complete lead-in)
*"The lab reported a single finding[BLANK] the treatment showed no measurable effect."*
Choices: (A) : (B) ; (C) , (D) .
- Left = *"The lab reported a single finding"* → **[IC]** ✓. Right directly explains WHAT the finding was → colon relationship.
- (C) comma alone → **WRONG  -  comma splice**. (D) period → legal but severs the explanatory link the sentence wants.
- Between (A) colon and (B) semicolon: the right side **explains** the left, so colon is the better structural fit. (Colon and dash are stylistically distinct here; only one is offered, so no equivalence shortcut applies.)
- **Correct answer: (A)**  -  colon. *"The lab reported a single finding: the treatment showed no measurable effect."*

# PART 3  -  MIXED PRACTICE (Full procedure)

**1 [Medium]** *"The data, which were collected over a year, [suggest/suggests] a significant trend."* → "which were collected…" non-essential (cross out) → true subject "data" (plural on the SAT  -  note: collective noun treated as plural in formal/academic contexts) → **suggest**. "suggests" = **agreement mismatch**.

**2 [Hard]** *"Not only did the new regulation reduce emissions, but it also lowered operational costs."* vs *"Not only the new regulation reduced emissions, but also it lowered operational costs."* → second is **WRONG  -  parallel mismatch** (auxiliary inversion vs plain). Correct first.

**3 [Medium]** *"The experimental results appear [different/differently] from what was predicted."* → "appear" is linking verb here (="seem") → adjective → **different**. "differently" = **WRONG  -  adverb after linking verb**.

**4 [Easy]** *"The [researchers/researcher's/researchers'] conclusions were published in a peer-reviewed journal."* → multiple researchers (no ownership indicated) → **researchers** (plural alone). "researcher's" / "researchers'" = **WRONG  -  unnecessary apostrophe**.

**5 [Very Hard]** *"The initial results were promising, however, the team decided to run additional trials."* → **WRONG  -  comma splice** ("however" after bare comma joining two [IC]s). Fix: *"…promising; however, the team…"*

# MASTER CHEAT SHEET (final consolidated reference)

## Boundaries  -  Quick Decision Tree
```
Both sides [IC]?
   |- period / semicolon / comma+FANBOYS all LEGAL
   |     (period ~ semicolon ONLY if choices are otherwise identical)
   |- pick the one that fits logic & isn't duplicated
One side [DC]?
   |- [DC] first → comma
   |- [DC] second → comma or nothing (essential)
Right explains/list left, left is [IC]? → colon or single dash (stylistically distinct)
Mid-sentence aside? → matching pair ,…, /  - … -  / (…), never mixed
Essential info? → NO punctuation
Participial phrase (-ing/-ed, no subject)? → comma only
List with internal commas? → semicolons between main items
Subject-verb split? → NO punctuation (never separate subject from its verb)
Compound predicate (one subject, two verbs)? → NO punctuation between them
```

## FANBOYS vs Conjunctive Adverbs
- **FANBOYS** (comma before, joins two [IC]s): for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so
- **Conjunctive adverbs** (semicolon/period before, comma after): however, therefore, moreover, thus, consequently, nevertheless, furthermore

## FSS  -  Quick Decision Tree
```
Verb differs? → TRUE subject (strip insertions) → match number
Pronoun differs? → antecedent → number + ambiguity + distance + dummy?
Possessive/contraction? → expand contraction, test sense; their/they're/there?
Noun vs action after blank? → adjective (noun) / adverb (action); linking verb → adjective
Verb FINITENESS: does subject have a finite verb already? YES→verbal NO→finite verb
List/comparison? → match grammatical skeletons; use "that of"/"those of"
Opens with modifier? → next noun must fit; check dangling/misplaced/squinting/ambiguous
"of the two"/"than" → comparative; "of all"/"of the three+" → superlative; never double-mark
Pronoun in compound/after preposition? → isolate, test case
Comparison structures: as…as, more…than, less…than, between X and Y, different from
```

## Traps Consolidated
1. Comma splice: comma alone joining two [IC]s  -  always wrong.
2. Conj. adverb after bare comma ("…however, …")  -  needs semicolon.
3. Semicolon/period before a [DC]  -  illegal.
4. Colon after incomplete left side  -  illegal.
5. Mismatched supplement punctuation  -  always wrong.
6. Single comma before an essential name  -  red flag.
7. Nonrestrictive appositive's internal plurals tricking agreement  -  cross out first.
8. "Which" with no commas  -  likely error (restrictive wants "that").
9. Ambiguous pronoun with two compatible antecedents.
10. Pronoun far from its antecedent (distance error).
11. Linking verb + adverb (should be adjective): "appears differently" → "appears different."
12. Double comparative/superlative marking  -  always wrong.
13. Comparing thing to person without "that of"/"those of."
14. Dangling modifier  -  check exact next noun.
15. Squinting modifier  -  could modify either direction.
16. Subject-verb split by comma  -  always wrong regardless of subject length.
17. Compound predicate with a comma between the two verbs  -  always wrong.
18. Fragment: non-finite verb (gerund/participle/infinitive) alone with no finite verb.
19. Dropping "-ly" from adverbs in comparatives: "quicker" (adj) vs "more quickly" (adv).
20. Wrong comparison pair: "as…than" instead of "as…as."
21. Hypercorrection: "to Dr. Chen and I" instead of "to Dr. Chen and me" (object position).
22. Who/whom inversion: use the "him" test  -  "The committee recommended him" → whom; "He wrote the study" → who.

## The Single Highest-Value Habit
Before ANY rule: **classify the question** (Boundaries = punctuation; FSS = words/forms), then **strip every inserted phrase** between the two elements that must agree or connect. These two habits resolve the majority of A4 questions before you recall a named rule.
