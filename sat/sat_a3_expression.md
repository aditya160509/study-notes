# Digital SAT Reading & Writing - Expression of Ideas (Domain A3)

## Scope

~20% of the Reading & Writing section (8-12 of 54 operational questions). Two testing points: **Rhetorical Synthesis** and **Transitions**.

**Domain cross-references:** A1 (Information & Ideas) for rhetorical synthesis passage comprehension | A2 (Craft & Structure) for text purpose and structure analysis in transition questions | A4 (Conventions) for grammatical correctness in concision and style choices

The claim (from the Assessment Framework v3.01, Table 13): *"Students will use revision skills and knowledge to improve the effectiveness of written expression in accordance with specified rhetorical goals."*

Two skill/knowledge testing points are addressed in the domain:
- **Rhetorical Synthesis** - *"Students will strategically integrate provided information and ideas on a topic to form an effective sentence achieving a specified rhetorical aim."*
- **Transitions** - *"Students will determine the most effective transition word or phrase to logically connect information and ideas in a text."*

Questions in this domain appear **last in each module**, after Craft & Structure, Information & Ideas, and Standard English Conventions. Within the domain, Rhetorical Synthesis questions precede Transitions questions. Both are ordered easiest to hardest.

The good news about this domain: it is the most mechanical and trainable part of the entire Reading & Writing section. There is no passage comprehension marathon here. Every question follows a fixed template, and once you learn the template, your accuracy goes up faster than in any other domain. The bad news: students lose points here not because the material is hard, but because they read the notes first instead of the goal, or they pick the answer that "sounds good" instead of the answer that does the exact job the question demands.

---

## Most Common Question Types on the Actual Exam

These are the formats the real Digital SAT repeatedly uses for Expression of Ideas. Based on analysis of official College Board practice tests (Bluebook), the Assessment Framework v3.01, the official SAT Educator Question Bank, Khan Academy's SAT course, and widely reported test-day breakdowns. If you master this list, you cover most of the 8 to 12 questions you will see.

1. **Rhetorical synthesis with a stated goal** (2 to 3 per module, typically 4 to 6 per full test; always the last question or two in each module). Stem: "Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?" You get 4 to 6 bullet points of research notes plus a specific job ("emphasize a similarity," "introduce a scientist and her field," "support a claim"). The correct answer combines the goal-relevant facts into one natural sentence. All four choices are usually factually accurate - the goal is what separates them. This is the single most-tested NEW question format on the digital SAT (it did not exist on the paper test), so expect it on every exam.

2. **Transition selection** (2 to 3 per module, typically 4 to 6 per full test). Stem: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?" A short passage (2 to 4 sentences) has a blank between two ideas; four transition words or phrases are offered. The correct answer matches the logical relationship between the two ideas (contrast, cause-effect, addition, concession, sequence, emphasis, example, result/exception). Grammar is not the issue - every choice is grammatical. Logic is the issue.

3. **Concision** (1 to 2 per test, folded into the domain). Stem: "Which choice most effectively combines the sentences?" or "Which choice best revises the underlined portion to improve clarity and concision?" or simply "Which choice is most effective?" Multiple options are grammatical; the correct one expresses the same meaning in the fewest words without losing precision or tone. Redundancy traps ("consensus agreement," "the reason is because") are the classic setup.

4. **Precision / word choice** (0 to 2 per test, overlaps with A2 Words in Context). Stem: "Which choice most effectively..." or "Which choice best maintains the style and tone of the passage?" The correct word is the one that is precise (exact meaning), appropriate in register (formal passage = formal word), and consistent with the passage's tone. Distractors are informal, vague, overstated, or slightly off in connotation.

**Distribution tip:** Rhetorical Synthesis and Transitions split the domain roughly evenly (each about 4 to 6 questions on a full test). Concision and precision questions are fewer but appear regularly. The two official sample questions College Board publishes for this domain (one rhetorical synthesis, one transitions - both reproduced in this file below) are the best possible introduction to the format because they come with College Board's own explanation of what makes the right answer right.

## A3.1 Rhetorical Synthesis

### Definition in Simplest Terms

You are given a stack of research notes in bullet form (3 to 6 facts about one topic). Then you are told a specific job, like "emphasize a similarity between two projects" or "introduce a scientist and her field of study." Your task: pick the ONE sentence that does exactly that job using only facts from the notes.

Think of it as cooking from a recipe. The bullets are your ingredients. The goal is the recipe. Only one of the four dishes matches the recipe; the other three are perfectly edible food that you did not order. A choice can be 100% true and still be wrong, because the question is not "which sentence is true?" - it is "which sentence does THIS job?"

This question type is unique to the digital SAT. It did not exist on the paper-and-pencil SAT. It shows up on every single test, so it is worth mastering completely. The good news: the skill is entirely mechanical. Read the goal first, filter the notes, apply two elimination passes, verify. There is no way to be "surprised" by a rhetorical synthesis question once you have internalized the method.

### Format

A student's bullet-point notes on a research topic (3-6 facts), followed by a stated rhetorical goal and four choices. The question asks: *"Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish the goal?"*

Every question has four components:

1. **Introduction** - "While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:"
2. **Bullet points** - factual notes (3-6 items) covering a single topic
3. **Goal statement** - specifies the rhetorical purpose (e.g., "The student wants to explain the significance of...")
4. **Four choices** - sentences that combine facts from the notes

### Why the Goal Comes First (The Core Insight)

The single most important habit in this entire domain: **read the goal before you read the notes.** Here is why. The notes contain 3 to 6 facts, but the goal only cares about 2 or 3 of them. If you read the notes first, your brain latches onto whichever fact is most interesting or most detailed - and then you walk into the answer choices already convinced that fact matters. The test writers put "bait" facts in the notes precisely to catch students who read the notes first.

Example: the notes say a scientist led 100 expeditions, spent 7,000 hours underwater, set a diving record, and started an online education project. The goal says "emphasize the innovative nature shared by two of her projects." If you read the notes first, you will fixate on the 7,000 hours (impressive!) and pick a choice about her career. If you read the goal first, you know you are looking for exactly two projects and the word "innovation," and you filter out everything else in seconds.

The goal also tells you the SHAPE the correct answer must take, before you see a single choice. A contrast goal needs both subjects in one sentence with a "while" or "whereas" structure. An introduce goal needs the subject named and defined. A support-a-claim goal needs evidence plus a claim. When you know the shape, wrong answers announce themselves.

### Method (Fixed Order)

**Step 1: Read the goal first.** Identify the rhetorical purpose before looking at the notes. Underline the operative word: *emphasize, compare, contrast, summarize, explain, introduce, support, challenge.* Also note whether the goal names an audience ("for an audience unfamiliar with the topic" is a common add-on that changes everything).

**Step 2: Simplify the goal in your own words.** "They want me to show how X is different from Y." "They want me to name the scientist and say what she studies." This prevents the notes from pulling you off track. If the goal contains "and" (e.g., "introduce the scientist AND her field of study"), both halves are mandatory - a choice that does one half only is wrong.

**Step 3: Scan notes for goal-relevant facts.** Ignore details that don't serve the goal. You may need facts from 1 bullet point or across 4. Treat irrelevant bullets as distractor material. Mentally mark the 2 or 3 bullets the goal actually needs.

**Step 4: Evaluate each choice by asking two questions:**

| Question | If No: |
|---|---|
| Does this accomplish the stated goal? | Eliminate |
| Does this accurately reflect the notes? | Eliminate |

This is the two-pass elimination method (the same structure Khan Academy teaches for this question type):
- **Pass 1 (goal filter):** eliminate every choice that does not do the exact job. Be strict. If the goal is "introduce the scientist AND her field," a choice that names the scientist but not her field fails Pass 1 even though it is accurate.
- **Pass 2 (accuracy filter):** among the survivors, eliminate any choice that misstates, exaggerates, or adds information not in the notes. Watch for words like "exclusively," "only," "all," "proved," "always" - the notes rarely support absolutes.

**Step 5: Re-read the goal just before selecting.** Confirm your choice serves the exact stated purpose, not a different one that happens to be accurate. Then do a final sanity check: read your chosen sentence aloud in your head. Does it read naturally as a real English sentence? The correct answer always does; some distractors are grammatically awkward in ways that signal they were assembled to hit facts rather than to sound like writing.

### Goal Types

| Goal Type | What the Correct Answer Must Do | Keywords in Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Summarize** | Capture the breadth of the notes; cover multiple main points | summarize, provide an overview, describe the research on |
| **Emphasize / Highlight** | Foreground one specific aspect or finding | emphasize, highlight, stress, underscore |
| **Emphasize a similarity** | Name BOTH subjects and the trait they share, in one sentence | emphasize a similarity, both, in common |
| **Emphasize a difference / contrast** | Name BOTH subjects and what distinguishes them, with contrast structure | emphasize a difference, contrast, whereas, unlike |
| **Compare / Contrast** | Address two subjects explicitly using comparative/contrastive structure | compare, contrast, distinguish between, differentiate, similarities, differences |
| **Explain / Describe** | Clarify a process, cause, or mechanism | explain, describe, illustrate, show how |
| **Explain a concept + give an example** | Define the concept AND give a concrete instance from the notes | explain what X is and provide an example |
| **Present a study and its findings** | Identify the research AND report what it discovered | present a study and its findings |
| **Introduce** | Present the topic broadly to an audience (may specify "unfamiliar audience") | introduce, present to an audience unfamiliar with |
| **Support a claim** | Present evidence that backs a stated position | support, provide evidence for, corroborate |
| **Challenge a claim** | Present evidence that undermines a stated position | challenge, undermine, weaken, call into question |
| **List all members of a category** | Include EVERY item the goal names - none may be dropped | list, mention all, each of the |
| **Identify a trend** | Show a pattern across data or time | identify a trend, show how X has changed over time |
| **Describe a process in order** | Present the steps in the correct sequence | describe the process, in order, steps |

### The Goal Verb Predicts the Winner's Shape

This table (adapted from StudyHall's SAT course) is the fastest way to pre-filter choices. The goal's verb tells you what the winning sentence must look like before you read any choice:

| Goal says | The winner must | Instant eliminations |
|---|---|---|
| Emphasize a difference | Contrast both things in one sentence | Anything naming only one of them |
| Emphasize a similarity | Name both things and the shared trait | Contrast constructions ("while," "unlike") |
| Introduce X to an unfamiliar audience | Name X and say what it is, plainly | Choices assuming the reader knows X |
| Present a conclusion | State the takeaway the notes point to | Raw facts with no claim attached |
| Explain a process | Order the steps (first, then, after) | Single facts with no ordering |
| Support a claim | Pair evidence with the claim | Evidence with no claim, or claim with no evidence |
| List all members | Include every named member | Any choice dropping one member |

### Trap Analysis

**Trap 1: Factually correct, rhetorically wrong.**
The choice accurately reflects the notes but does not serve the stated goal. The most common trap on the entire domain. Every wrong answer on a rhetorical synthesis question is usually factually accurate - the goal is the only thing that separates the right answer from the rest.

**Trap 2: Goal achieved, notes contradicted.**
The choice accomplishes the rhetorical purpose but introduces information not in the notes, contradicts a note, or makes a claim the notes do not support. Watch for overreach words: "exclusively," "only," "all," "proved," "always." Example: notes say a scientist's work "has been shown in the United States, Canada, Japan, and Germany" - a choice saying her work is shown "exclusively in North America and Asia" contradicts the notes (Germany is in Europe) and adds the word "exclusively" that nothing supports.

**Trap 3: Wrong scope - too narrow for summarize, too broad for emphasize.**
A summary goal requires breadth (coverage across multiple bullets). An emphasize goal requires focus (drill into one specific bullet). Swapping these produces a wrong answer.

**Trap 4: Logical distortion.**
The choice reverses cause and effect, treats correlation as causation, or overgeneralizes from a single example in the notes.

**Trap 5: Correct information, wrong audience.**
The prompt may specify "an audience unfamiliar with the topic." Choices that assume prior knowledge or use unexplained jargon fail this test. An introduce question rewards the choice that defines and names; a choice that jumps straight to comparisons between un-named things fails the unfamiliar reader.

**Trap 6: Both subjects mentioned but wrong relationship.**
For compare/contrast goals, the choice may mention both subjects but describe them as similar when the notes show difference, or vice versa. A "similarity" goal is killed by "whereas" and "unlike"; a "difference" goal is killed by "both" and "in common."

**Trap 7: The "and" goal - only half done.**
When the goal lists two required elements ("introduce the scientist AND her field," "emphasize the duration AND purpose"), a choice that delivers only one is a very common wrong answer. It is accurate, it is relevant, it is just incomplete. Be strict: both halves must appear.

**Trap 8: Same facts, wrong perspective.**
Two choices can draw on the exact same bullets but frame them differently (one highlights similarity, the other highlights difference, one states raw facts, the other connects them to a claim). You cannot choose by checking facts alone - you must check the angle. This is why Step 1 (read the goal first) and Step 5 (re-read the goal last) bookend the whole process.

**Trap 9: The impressive-fact lure.**
The most detailed or striking bullet (the 7,000 hours underwater, the record-breaking depth) is usually the bait. If the goal does not ask about it, a choice built around it is wrong no matter how impressive it sounds.

**Trap 10: Process order scrambled.**
For "describe a process in order" goals, the correct answer must present the steps in the actual sequence from the notes. Distractors take real steps and scramble them (putting a later step first), or drop steps to shorten the sentence. A process description is a chain; breaking the chain anywhere makes the choice wrong, even if every step mentioned is accurate.

### Official College Board Sample Question

This is one of the two official sample questions College Board publishes for the Expression of Ideas domain (Assessment Framework v3.01, section 3.5.3.1). It is worth studying closely because the answer explanations are College Board's own.

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
- Maika'i Tubbs is a Native Hawaiian sculptor and installation artist.
- His work has been shown in the United States, Canada, Japan, and Germany, among other places.
- Many of his sculptures feature discarded objects.
- His work Erasure (2008) includes discarded audiocassette tapes and magnets.
- His work Home Grown (2009) includes discarded pushpins, plastic plates and forks, and wood.

The student wants to emphasize a similarity between the two works. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) Erasure (2008) uses discarded objects such as audiocassette tapes and magnets; Home Grown (2009), however, includes pushpins, plastic plates and forks, and wood.
B) Like many of Tubbs's sculptures, both Erasure and Home Grown include discarded objects: Erasure uses audiocassette tapes, and Home Grown uses plastic forks.
C) Tubbs's work, which often features discarded objects, has been shown both within the United States and abroad.
D) Tubbs completed Erasure in 2008 and Home Grown in 2009.

**Correct answer: B** (College Board's key)

**Why:** The goal is to emphasize a SIMILARITY between the two works. Choice B uses "like many of Tubbs's sculptures" and "both" to emphasize that the two works share the use of discarded objects, even though the specific objects differ. It names both works, names the shared trait, and uses additive language ("both," "and") rather than contrast language.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 6 - Wrong relationship.** The use of "however" emphasizes a CONTRAST between the works (different discarded objects), but the goal asks for a similarity. The facts are accurate; the angle is backwards.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Wrong scope.** It discusses Tubbs's work in general and does not mention the two specific works at all. The goal is about a similarity between Erasure and Home Grown specifically.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** It conveys completion dates for both works but establishes no logical relationship between them - no similarity, no shared trait. It does not do the job the goal demands.

### Examples

#### [Easy] Summary Goal

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- The Amazon rainforest spans approximately 5.5 million square kilometers across nine South American countries.
- It produces about 20% of the world's oxygen and is often called the "lungs of the Earth."
- Deforestation has reduced the forest by nearly 20% in the last fifty years.
- The Amazon is home to an estimated 10% of all known species on Earth.

The student wants to summarize key facts about the Amazon rainforest. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) The Amazon rainforest, which produces 20% of the world's oxygen, is home to 10% of all known species and spans 5.5 million square kilometers across nine countries, though deforestation has reduced it by nearly 20%.

B) The Amazon rainforest produces about 20% of the world's oxygen and is often called the "lungs of the Earth."

C) Deforestation has reduced the Amazon rainforest by nearly 20% in the last fifty years, threatening its biodiversity.

D) The Amazon rainforest spans approximately 5.5 million square kilometers, making it the largest tropical rainforest in the world.

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** Choice A covers four major facts from the notes (size, oxygen production, biodiversity, deforestation), providing breadth appropriate for a summary.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal (too narrow).** Only covers oxygen production, ignoring size, biodiversity, and deforestation. A summary must be comprehensive.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Wrong scope (too narrow for summary).** Focuses exclusively on deforestation. This might work for an "emphasize a threat" goal, but not for a general summary.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal (too narrow).** Only covers size. Additionally, introduces "largest in the world" which is not stated in the notes - also **Trap 2 (contradicts notes)**.

#### [Easy] Emphasize a Similarity

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- The novel David Copperfield by Charles Dickens follows the adventures of its title character, David Copperfield.
- David Copperfield is considered a bildungsroman, a type of novel in which the main character grows, learns, and changes through experience.
- The novel Tom Jones by Henry Fielding follows the adventures of its title character, Tom Jones.
- Tom Jones is considered a picaresque novel, a type of novel in which the main character has many adventures but stays fundamentally the same.

The student wants to emphasize a similarity between the two novels. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) David Copperfield and Tom Jones are both considered picaresque novels.
B) Both David Copperfield and Tom Jones follow the adventures of their title characters.
C) David Copperfield was written by Charles Dickens; Tom Jones was written by Henry Fielding.
D) In David Copperfield, unlike in Tom Jones, the main character grows, learns, and changes through experience.

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** The goal demands a similarity between the two novels. Bullets 1 and 3 give the shared trait: both follow the adventures of their title characters. Choice B names both novels, names the shared trait, and uses "both" - the exact shape the goal verb predicts.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Contradicts the notes.** The notes say David Copperfield is a bildungsroman (character changes) and Tom Jones is a picaresque novel (character stays the same). Calling both "picaresque" contradicts bullet 2. It does state a similarity, but a false one - the accuracy filter kills it.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 6 - Wrong relationship.** It names both novels but highlights a DIFFERENCE (different authors), not a similarity. Accurate, but the goal asks for what the novels share.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 6 - Wrong relationship.** "Unlike in Tom Jones" is contrast structure. It emphasizes a difference (how the characters change), which is the opposite of the goal. This is the classic "same facts, wrong perspective" trap (Trap 8) - the facts about character development are real, but they are framed as a difference.

#### [Easy] Introduce a Scientist and Her Field

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Dr. Aisha Bello is a marine biologist who studies coral reef ecosystems.
- She has published over 60 peer-reviewed papers on reef resilience.
- Her research has documented the recovery of coral reefs in the Gulf of Guinea after bleaching events.
- She directs a field station in Ghana where students learn reef monitoring techniques.

The student wants to introduce Dr. Bello and her field of study to an audience unfamiliar with the topic. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) Dr. Aisha Bello, a marine biologist who studies coral reef ecosystems, has published over 60 peer-reviewed papers and directs a field station in Ghana.
B) Dr. Aisha Bello has documented the recovery of coral reefs in the Gulf of Guinea after bleaching events.
C) Coral reef ecosystems are among the most biodiverse habitats on Earth, and Dr. Aisha Bello studies them.
D) Dr. Aisha Bello directs a field station in Ghana where students learn reef monitoring techniques.

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** The goal is an "AND" goal: introduce the scientist AND her field. Choice A does both halves - it names her, identifies her as a marine biologist studying coral reefs (the field), and adds supporting facts about her work. It is also self-contained for an unfamiliar audience: nothing is assumed.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 7 - The "and" goal, only half done.** It introduces the scientist but never names her field (marine biology, coral reefs are implied by the content but the field itself is not stated). An unfamiliar audience learns what she did, not what field she works in.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Wrong scope (too broad for introduce).** It spends most of the sentence on a general fact about coral reefs ("among the most biodiverse habitats on Earth") that is NOT in the notes - **Trap 2** - and introduces Dr. Bello only as an afterthought.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 7 - Half done + narrow.** It names the scientist and one activity (directing a field station) but never says what field she is in. For an unfamiliar audience, this fails the "introduce the field" half of the goal.

#### [Easy] Compare/Contrast Goal

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Venus has a surface temperature of about 462 degrees Celsius due to a runaway greenhouse effect.
- Mars has an average surface temperature of about -60 degrees Celsius.
- Venus's atmosphere is 96% carbon dioxide, creating extreme pressure at the surface.
- Mars's atmosphere is 95% carbon dioxide but is much thinner, with less than 1% of Earth's atmospheric pressure.

The student wants to contrast the surface conditions on Venus and Mars. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) Venus and Mars both have atmospheres composed primarily of carbon dioxide.

B) Venus has a surface temperature of 462 degrees Celsius and an atmosphere that is 96% carbon dioxide.

C) While both planets have carbon dioxide-dominated atmospheres, Venus has an extreme surface temperature of 462 degrees Celsius and crushing atmospheric pressure, whereas Mars is bitterly cold with an average of -60 degrees Celsius and a much thinner atmosphere.

D) Mars has an average surface temperature of about -60 degrees Celsius, making it inhospitable to human life.

---

**Correct answer: C**

**Why:** Choice C uses contrastive structure ("while both... whereas...") and pairs corresponding facts (temperature vs. temperature, pressure vs. thin atmosphere) to highlight differences, directly serving the contrast goal.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 6 - Mentions both subjects but emphasizes similarity (wrong relationship).** The goal is contrast; this choice describes what the two planets have in common.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** Only discusses Venus. A contrast requires both subjects with explicit comparison.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** Only discusses Mars. Also introduces "inhospitable to human life" which is not in the notes - **Trap 2.**

#### [Easy] List All Members of a Category

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- The Great Barrier Reef is the world's largest coral reef system, stretching over 2,300 kilometers off Australia's northeast coast.
- The Belize Barrier Reef is the largest reef system in the Northern Hemisphere, spanning about 300 kilometers.
- The New Caledonia Barrier Reef surrounds the island of New Caledonia in the Pacific and is the second longest in the world.
- All three reefs are UNESCO World Heritage sites.

The student wants to list all three of the world's major barrier reefs named in the notes and note their shared status. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) The Great Barrier Reef, the Belize Barrier Reef, and the New Caledonia Barrier Reef are all UNESCO World Heritage sites.
B) The Great Barrier Reef stretches over 2,300 kilometers, making it the world's largest coral reef system.
C) The Belize Barrier Reef and the New Caledonia Barrier Reef are both UNESCO World Heritage sites.
D) The Great Barrier Reef is the world's largest coral reef system, and the Belize Barrier Reef is the largest in the Northern Hemisphere.

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** The goal is a "list all" goal: all three reefs must be named, plus their shared status. Choice A names all three and attaches the shared fact (UNESCO World Heritage status). Nothing is dropped, nothing is added.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Too narrow.** Only one reef is named. A list goal requires every member.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 7 - The list, only half done.** Two reefs are named but the Great Barrier Reef is dropped. The notes name three major barrier reefs; a "list all three" goal fails if any is missing.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 + Trap 7.** It names only two reefs, drops New Caledonia, and omits the shared UNESCO status entirely. It is factually accurate but does not do the listing job.

#### [Medium] Emphasize/Highlight Goal

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- The Great Pacific Garbage Patch (GPGP) is a massive collection of floating debris in the North Pacific Ocean, estimated at 1.6 million square kilometers.
- Approximately 80% of the debris comes from land-based sources, primarily from rivers in Asia.
- Marine organisms often mistake microplastics for food, introducing toxins into the food chain.
- Unlike organic waste, plastics in the GPGP do not biodegrade; they break into smaller fragments called microplastics that persist for centuries.

The student wants to emphasize what makes the GPGP's plastic waste a distinctive environmental threat. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) The Great Pacific Garbage Patch covers an estimated 1.6 million square kilometers, with about 80% of its debris coming from land-based sources in Asia.

B) Unlike ordinary organic waste, the plastics in the GPGP do not biodegrade but instead fragment into microplastics that persist for centuries, introducing toxins into the food chain when marine organisms ingest them.

C) The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a massive collection of floating debris in the North Pacific Ocean, and marine organisms often mistake microplastics for food.

D) Approximately 80% of the debris in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch comes from rivers in Asia, while the remaining 20% comes from ocean-based sources such as fishing nets.

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** The goal asks what makes the GPGP's plastic waste *distinctive*. Choice B emphasizes the key distinctive feature - plastic's persistence and non-biodegradability compared to organic waste - and connects it to the ecological consequence (toxins entering the food chain).

**A is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** Size and source are factual but do not address what makes the threat *distinctive*.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** States facts about the patch and microplastic ingestion but does not highlight the *distinctive* quality (persistence/non-biodegradability).

**D is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** Source breakdown is irrelevant to the goal of highlighting the distinctive threat. Also, "the remaining 20% comes from ocean-based sources such as fishing nets" adds a detail not in the notes - **Trap 2.**

#### [Medium] Present a Study and Its Findings

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Researchers at the University of Oslo conducted a 10-year study of honeybee foraging behavior.
- The study tracked 40,000 individual bees using tiny radio-frequency identification tags.
- The researchers found that bees visited an average of 50 flowers per foraging trip.
- They also found that bees foraging in pesticide-treated fields returned to the hive with significantly fewer pollen loads.

The student wants to present the study and its findings. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) Using tiny radio-frequency identification tags, researchers tracked 40,000 individual bees, which is one of the largest samples ever used in honeybee research.
B) A 10-year University of Oslo study that tracked 40,000 bees found that the bees visited an average of 50 flowers per trip and returned from pesticide-treated fields with significantly fewer pollen loads.
C) Bees visit an average of 50 flowers per foraging trip, and bees in pesticide-treated fields bring back less pollen.
D) The University of Oslo researchers tracked 40,000 individual bees using radio-frequency identification tags over a period of 10 years.

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** The goal is a "present a study and its findings" goal. Choice B identifies the study (10-year University of Oslo study, 40,000 bees tracked) AND reports both findings (50 flowers per trip; reduced pollen from pesticide-treated fields). Study + findings, both halves complete.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 7 - Half done.** It describes the study's method but reports NO findings. Also "one of the largest samples ever used" is not in the notes - **Trap 2.**

**C is wrong:** **Trap 7 - Half done (findings without the study).** It reports the findings but never identifies the study (who did it, when, how many bees). The goal asks to present "the study and its findings" - both are required.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** It describes the study but omits both findings entirely. This would serve a "describe the methodology" goal, not a "present the study and its findings" goal.

#### [Medium] Explain a Process

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing technology derived from a bacterial immune system.
- The Cas9 enzyme acts as "molecular scissors" that cut DNA at a specific location.
- A guide RNA molecule is designed to match the target DNA sequence and directs Cas9 to the correct spot.
- Once the DNA is cut, the cell's natural repair mechanisms can be used to disable a gene or insert a new sequence.

The student wants to explain how CRISPR-Cas9 edits genes. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing technology that has revolutionized genetic research and holds promise for treating genetic disorders.

B) CRISPR-Cas9 uses a guide RNA to direct the Cas9 enzyme to a specific DNA location, where Cas9 cuts the DNA; the cell's repair mechanisms can then disable the gene or insert a new sequence.

C) The Cas9 enzyme acts as "molecular scissors" that cut DNA at a specific location, and the technology is derived from a bacterial immune system.

D) Derived from a bacterial immune system, CRISPR-Cas9 is a powerful tool for editing genes by cutting DNA and allowing cells to repair themselves.

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** Choice B presents the process in chronological order: guide RNA directs Cas9, Cas9 cuts, cell repair mechanisms act. This is the minimum set of steps needed to explain the mechanism.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** Discusses the significance of CRISPR rather than the mechanism. Contains no process steps.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Wrong scope (too narrow).** Only covers the cutting step but omits the guide RNA and repair mechanism, which are essential to explaining how gene editing works.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Goal partially achieved but vague.** Mentions cutting and repair but omits the crucial guide RNA step that makes the process specific. Also uses vague language ("powerful tool") that does not explain the mechanism.

#### [Hard] Explain a Cause-Effect Sequence

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Beginning in the 1970s, the population of the North Atlantic right whale declined sharply.
- Ship strikes killed an estimated 20 whales per year along the eastern US coast.
- New shipping lanes were rerouted away from whale feeding grounds starting in 2008.
- Since the rerouting, right whale deaths from ship strikes have fallen by roughly 80%.
- Despite this progress, the population remains endangered due to entanglement in fishing gear.

The student wants to explain how the rerouting of shipping lanes led to a decline in whale deaths. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) Ship strikes killed an estimated 20 whales per year until shipping lanes were rerouted away from whale feeding grounds in 2008, after which deaths from ship strikes fell by roughly 80%.
B) The North Atlantic right whale population declined sharply beginning in the 1970s, and the species remains endangered today.
C) New shipping lanes were rerouted away from whale feeding grounds starting in 2008, which shows that conservation measures can be effective.
D) Right whale deaths from ship strikes have fallen by roughly 80% since 2008, and the population remains endangered due to entanglement in fishing gear.

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** The goal asks for a cause-effect sequence: rerouting lanes -> fewer deaths. Choice A delivers the chain in order (strikes were killing 20 per year, lanes were rerouted in 2008, deaths fell 80% after) with causal language ("until," "after which"). It uses exactly the two bullets that explain the mechanism.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** It describes the decline and current status but never mentions the rerouting or the causal chain. No cause-effect explanation.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal (too vague).** It mentions the rerouting but does not connect it to the drop in deaths - no before/after comparison, no mechanism. "Shows that conservation measures can be effective" is an unsupported editorial comment, not an explanation - **Trap 2** (adds a claim the notes do not make).

**D is wrong:** **Trap 8 - Same facts, wrong angle.** It states the drop in deaths and the entanglement problem but omits the cause (rerouting) entirely. It would serve a "summarize the current status" goal, not an "explain how rerouting led to fewer deaths" goal.

#### [Hard] Describe a Process in Order

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- To make paper, wood chips are first cooked with chemicals to break them into fibers, producing a pulp.
- The pulp is then washed and bleached to remove impurities and brighten the fibers.
- The cleaned pulp is spread onto a moving wire mesh screen, where water drains away and the fibers mat together.
- Finally, the matted sheet is pressed and dried, then rolled into large spools of finished paper.

The student wants to describe the process of making paper in the correct order. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) Wood chips are cooked with chemicals to produce pulp, which is washed and bleached; the cleaned pulp is spread on a wire mesh where water drains, and the resulting mat is pressed and dried into finished paper.
B) Wood chips are cooked with chemicals to produce a pulp, and the finished paper is pressed, dried, and rolled into large spools.

**Why:** The goal is a process-in-order goal. Choice A preserves the full sequence: cook -> wash/bleach -> spread on mesh -> drain -> press/dry. Every step appears, and the order matches the notes exactly.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 10 - Right steps, wrong order / dropped steps.** It jumps from cooking the chips straight to pressing and drying the finished paper, dropping the washing, bleaching, and mesh-spreading steps. The chain is broken.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 10 - Order reversed.** It starts with spreading the pulp on the mesh and THEN cooks the wood chips. The steps are real but scrambled - a process description must follow the actual order.

#### [Very Hard] Emphasize an Extreme (The "Longest River" Goal)

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- The Nile River flows approximately 6,650 kilometers, making it the longest river in the world.
- The Amazon River flows approximately 6,400 kilometers.
- The Yangtze River flows approximately 6,300 kilometers.
- The Nile passes through eleven countries, while the Amazon passes through four and the Yangtze through one.

The student wants to emphasize that the Nile is the longest river. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A) The Nile, Amazon, and Yangtze are among the longest rivers on Earth, together flowing through sixteen countries.
B) At approximately 6,650 kilometers, the Nile is longer than both the Amazon (about 6,400 kilometers) and the Yangtze (about 6,300 kilometers), making it the longest river in the world.
C) The Nile flows through eleven countries, more than the Amazon and Yangtze combined.
D) The Nile is the longest river in the world, and the Amazon and Yangtze are also extremely long rivers.

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** The goal is to emphasize the Nile's status as the LONGEST river - a comparison goal built around an extreme. Choice B does the comparison explicitly: it states the Nile's length and positions it against the other two rivers with numbers, then states the conclusion ("making it the longest river in the world"). The comparison structure is what makes it work; simply asserting "longest" without the contrast would not emphasize the extreme.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** It groups the three rivers as "among the longest" without singling out the Nile or comparing lengths. It actually de-emphasizes the Nile's #1 status. Also "together flowing through sixteen countries" (11 + 4 + 1 = 16) is accurate but irrelevant to the length goal - **Trap 9** (irrelevant fact).

**C is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal.** It emphasizes a different extreme (most countries), not longest river. The length data - the facts the goal demands - are absent. This is the "wrong extreme" trap: the notes contain several superlatives, and you must serve the one the goal names.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Correct information, wrong goal (assertion without comparison).** It states the Nile is longest but provides no comparative evidence from the notes. "Are also extremely long" adds no data and does not emphasize the Nile's position relative to the others. An emphasis goal needs the supporting comparison, not just the claim.

## A3.2 Transitions

### Definition in Simplest Terms

A short passage has a blank. On either side of the blank are two ideas. You must pick the transition word or phrase that best shows how those two ideas are related - whether the second idea opposes the first, follows from it, adds to it, illustrates it, concedes to it, or comes after it in time.

Every transition word makes a small logical promise. "Therefore" promises the next idea follows as a result. "For example" promises a specific instance of a broader point. "Nevertheless" acknowledges the previous point and says the next claim still holds. Your job is to figure out which promise the passage actually needs, then pick the word that makes exactly that promise.

Crucial fact: every answer choice is grammatically correct. You are never choosing between "correct grammar" and "wrong grammar." You are choosing between four words that all fit the sentence grammatically - only one fits the LOGIC. This is why you cannot answer by ear alone ("it sounds right") and why you must name the relationship before you look at the choices.

### Format

A short passage (2-4 sentences) with a blank. Four transition words or phrases as choices. The task: select the transition that most logically connects the ideas. Stem: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?"

Transitions questions are one of the two testing points that make up the Expression of Ideas domain (the other being Rhetorical Synthesis). Expect roughly 2 to 3 per module, 4 to 6 per full test.

### The Method

**Step 1: Cover the answer choices.** Reading them first creates confirmation bias. If you see "however" in the choices, your brain starts hunting for a contrast even when the passage is additive. Cover them.

**Step 2: Read the sentence before the blank and the sentence after. Paraphrase each in your head.** Translate each into a simple bullet point. "Sentence 1 says the project cost more than planned. Sentence 2 says it cut commute times." If the passage is hard to parse, simplify each sentence until you can state its claim in a few words.

**Step 3: Identify the precise logical relationship.** Do not use vague labels like "it connects them." Ask:

| Question | Likely Relationship |
|---|---|
| Does the second sentence follow from the first? | Cause-Effect |
| Does the second sentence oppose or reverse the first? | Contrast |
| Does the second sentence acknowledge the first but pivot? | Concession |
| Does the second sentence add a similar point? | Addition |
| Does the second sentence illustrate the first? | Example |
| Does the second sentence happen after the first? | Sequence |
| Does the second sentence emphasize or clarify the first? | Emphasis |
| Does the second sentence present an alternative? | Result/Exception |

**Step 4: Predict your own transition word.** Use a simple, concrete word that matches the relationship. *"This is contrast, so I need something like 'however'."* Say it in your own words first: "the second sentence pushes back against the first." THEN match to a category.

**Step 5: Uncover the choices and match.** Do not read every word - scan for the category you predicted first. Only compare within-category if multiple choices match your relationship label.

**Step 6: Substitute and sanity-check.** Plug your pick into the blank and read the full sentence in your head. Does the logic flow? If the passage reads like a real paragraph, you are done.

Two extra tips from Khan Academy's SAT course:

- **Be flexible.** The exact word you predicted ("therefore") may not be among the choices. Look for a word that does the same job. A cause-effect relationship can be served by "therefore," "as a result," "consequently," "accordingly," or "thus." If your predicted word is absent, look for a synonym in the same family, not a word from a different family.
- **Eliminate copycats.** If two answer choices have essentially the same meaning (e.g., "therefore" and "as a result," or "however" and "nevertheless" in a pure-contrast passage), they cannot both be right - and on the SAT, usually NEITHER is. When you see two near-synonyms, both are probably traps, because the test only allows one correct answer. Eliminate the pair and choose from what remains.

Also remember PrepMaven's warning: **if two answers from the same category both seem right, go back to the passage.** There is never more than one correct answer, so if your relationship label admits two choices, your label is probably too vague. Tighten it: it is not just "contrast" - it is "contrast that survives a conceded obstacle" (nevertheless) or "direct opposition" (however) or "inverted relationship" (conversely).

### Full Transition Taxonomy

#### Sequence

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| then | Next step in time or logic | "First, the solution is heated. Then, the catalyst is added." |
| subsequently | After something else | "The bill passed the House. Subsequently, it moved to the Senate." |
| first / second / third | Ordered enumeration | "First, identify the variable. Second, control for confounds." |
| next | Immediate following step | "Next, the mixture is cooled to room temperature." |
| afterward | Following in time | "The eruption occurred. Afterward, ash covered the region." |
| later | After some time has passed | "Later, researchers discovered the same pattern in primates." |
| previously | Before the current point | "Previously, scientists believed the species was extinct." |
| finally | Last in a sequence | "Finally, the results are published in a peer-reviewed journal." |
| meanwhile | At the same time; parallel development | "The architects finalized the blueprints. Meanwhile, contractors began site preparation." |
| eventually | Later outcome after time or steps | "The negotiations stalled repeatedly. Eventually, a compromise was reached." |

**Key distinction:** sequence words claim ORDER, not causation. "Subsequently" means "later," not "because of." If B merely follows A in time, that is sequence.

#### Contrast

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| however | Direct opposition | "The hypothesis seemed promising. However, the data did not support it." |
| conversely | Opposite relationship | "In urban areas, population is increasing. Conversely, rural areas are losing residents." |
| in contrast | Highlight difference | "Mars has a thin atmosphere. In contrast, Venus has an extremely dense one." |
| on the other hand | Alternative perspective | "Raising taxes may fund social programs. On the other hand, it may slow economic growth." |
| nevertheless | Despite what preceded | "The experiment had several flaws. Nevertheless, the results were striking." |
| nonetheless | Despite what preceded | "The funding was cut. Nonetheless, the research continued." |
| yet | Unexpected outcome | "She had little formal training, yet her work was groundbreaking." |
| whereas | Comparison showing difference | "Whereas Saturn's rings are visible from Earth, Uranus's rings are extremely faint." |
| by contrast | Explicit two-subject opposition | "The northern region is densely populated. By contrast, the south is sparsely settled." |
| still | Maintains a point despite opposition | "The evidence is incomplete. Still, the pattern is hard to ignore." |
| even so | Point holds despite what precedes | "The treatment is costly. Even so, patients report dramatic improvement." |

**However vs. nevertheless (a distinction the SAT loves):** Both signal contrast, but they are not interchangeable. "However" typically marks a shift in direction - the second point differs from the first (often positive to negative). "Nevertheless" marks a conclusion that SURVIVES an obstacle - the first point was a difficulty or negative, and the second point still holds anyway (often negative to positive). Compare: "I was looking forward to the show; the tickets, however, had cost an arm and a leg" (positive to negative, no obstacle). "I wasn't excited for the show; nevertheless, it would be nice to spend time with my cousin" (negative to positive, the point survives the obstacle). If the passage simply opposes two ideas, prefer "however." If it concedes a difficulty and then asserts that something remains true anyway, "nevertheless" fits.

#### Concession

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| granted | Admit truth before countering | "Granted, the policy has costs. But the benefits far outweigh them." |
| admittedly | Acknowledge a weakness | "Admittedly, the sample size was small. Still, the trend was clear." |
| of course | Concede an obvious point | "Of course, correlation is not causation. Yet the pattern merits investigation." |
| to be sure | Acknowledge validity | "To be sure, the technology is expensive. However, prices are falling rapidly." |
| certainly | Concede plausibility | "Certainly, the opposition raises valid concerns. Nevertheless, action is necessary." |
| despite this | Despite a conceded point | "The drug has side effects. Despite this, it remains the best treatment option." |
| although | Subordinate concession | "Although the study was observational, its findings have influenced policy." |

**Critical distinction - Contrast vs. Concession:** In pure contrast, the second sentence simply opposes the first. In concession, the writer *admits* the first point is true or reasonable *before* pushing back. Concession always has a "yes, but..." structure. If the first idea is not being granted as valid before countering, it is not concession. Test: does the passage say "sure, X is true, but Y"? Then concession. Does it just say "X. Y." where Y contradicts X? Then contrast.

**Concession contains two moves:** yield a point, then preserve or qualify the main claim. The transition word itself (granted, admittedly, to be sure, certainly, of course) does the yielding; the rest of the sentence or the next sentence does the preserving.

#### Cause-Effect

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| therefore | Logical conclusion | "The compound is toxic to insects. Therefore, it is used as a pesticide." |
| consequently | Direct result | "Sea levels are rising. Consequently, coastal cities face increased flooding." |
| as a result | Outcome of stated cause | "Emissions were reduced. As a result, air quality improved significantly." |
| thus | Inferred conclusion | "The evidence is inconclusive. Thus, further research is needed." |
| hence | Logical consequence | "The bridge was structurally unsound; hence, it was closed to traffic." |
| accordingly | Action in response to condition | "Demand exceeded supply. Accordingly, prices rose." |
| because | Reason for stated result | "The flight was delayed because a winter storm grounded all departures." |
| since | Reason (assumed known) | "Since the funding has been approved, construction will begin next month." |
| in turn | A result becomes the cause of the next step | "The storm knocked out power. This, in turn, shut down the water pumps." |

**Cause-Effect vs. Sequence:** Sequence is temporal (A happens, *then* B happens). Cause-Effect is causal (A causes B to happen). If B merely follows A in time without being caused by A, the relationship is sequence, not cause-effect. "Later does not automatically mean because."

**Direction matters:** cause-effect can run either way in the sentence. If sentence 1 states the cause and sentence 2 the effect, use "therefore," "as a result," "consequently." If sentence 1 states the effect and sentence 2 states the cause, use "because," "since," or "this is because." Getting the direction backwards is a favorite trap.

#### Addition

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| furthermore | Extend with strong emphasis | "The policy reduces emissions. Furthermore, it creates jobs in renewable energy." |
| moreover | Add compelling point | "The treatment is effective. Moreover, it has fewer side effects than alternatives." |
| in addition | Add related point | "The museum offers guided tours. In addition, it hosts weekly workshops." |
| additionally | Add supporting detail | "The software is free to download. Additionally, it includes updates for one year." |
| also | Simple addition | "She is a published author. She also works as a documentary filmmaker." |
| besides | Add reinforcing point | "The location is convenient. Besides, the rent is reasonable." |
| indeed | Strengthen preceding claim | "The results were remarkable. Indeed, they exceeded all expectations." |
| similarly / likewise | Add a case that resembles the previous one | "Lentils add plant-based protein to a diet. Similarly, beans and chickpeas serve as protein sources." |

**Addition vs. Example:** if the second sentence is a specific instance of the general claim in the first, it is example ("for instance"). If it is a new but related point that does not illustrate the first, it is addition ("furthermore"). The distinction: does sentence 2 fit INSIDE sentence 1's category, or sit BESIDE it?

#### Emphasis / Clarification

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| indeed | Confirm and strengthen | "This is a historic achievement. Indeed, it is the first of its kind." |
| in fact | Present stronger truth | "The solution appears simple. In fact, it took years to develop." |
| notably | Draw attention to key point | "Notably, the correlation held across all demographic groups." |
| particularly | Specify within category | "The region is rich in biodiversity, particularly in amphibian species." |
| specifically | Precisely identify | "The policy targets several sectors, specifically manufacturing and transportation." |
| above all | Most importantly | "Above all, the study demonstrates the need for early intervention." |
| especially | Highlight one member as unusually relevant | "The drought affected the whole region, especially small farms." |

**Emphasis vs. Addition:** both extend the previous idea, but emphasis STRENGTHENS or refines the exact point just made (often confirming it), while addition brings in a NEW point in the same direction. "In fact" verifies, corrects, or intensifies the claim just made; "furthermore" stacks a separate supporting point.

#### Example / Illustration

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| for example | Introduce a representative instance | "Many ecosystems are threatened. For example, the Great Barrier Reef has lost half its coral." |
| for instance | Same as for example | "Some ancient texts have been lost entirely. For instance, only fragments remain of Sappho's poetry." |
| specifically | Narrow from general to particular | "The practice was common in ancient cultures, specifically in Egyptian and Mesopotamian societies." |
| in particular | Single out as noteworthy | "Several factors contributed to the decline. In particular, overfishing played a major role." |
| namely | Enumerate specifics | "Three countries opposed the measure, namely France, Germany, and Italy." |
| to illustrate | Signals the next case demonstrates the previous point | "The technique works in practice. To illustrate, consider the recent restoration of the bay." |

**The key test for Example:** is sentence 2 a member of the category named in sentence 1, or a concrete instance of the claim? If yes, the relationship is example. "The botanist studies plants that delay blooming until conditions are right. For example, the alpine dryad waits for snowmelt before flowering." The dryad is one plant in the category "plants that delay blooming" - example.

#### Result / Exception / Alternative

| Transition | Function | Example Context |
|---|---|---|
| as a result | Outcome | "Glaciers are melting. As a result, sea levels are rising." |
| therefore | Conclusion | "The test was inconclusive. Therefore, we must repeat the experiment." |
| otherwise | If not, then | "The patient must rest. Otherwise, recovery will be delayed." |
| instead | Replacement of expected action | "The committee did not approve the budget. Instead, it called for a revised proposal." |
| rather | Correction or preference | "This is not a bug. Rather, it is a feature of the system." |
| alternatively | Another option | "The medication can be taken orally. Alternatively, it can be administered by injection." |

**Otherwise** is a negative conditional: if not X, then Y. If the passage does not present a conditional negative consequence, "otherwise" is wrong. **Instead** replaces an expected or rejected option. **Alternatively** presents a second valid option without rejecting the first.

### Official College Board Sample Question

This is the second official sample question College Board publishes for this domain (Assessment Framework v3.01, section 3.5.3.1). The explanation below follows College Board's own key explanation.

Iraqi artist Nazik Al-Malaika, celebrated as the first Arabic poet to write in free verse, didn't reject traditional forms entirely; her poem "Elegy for a Woman of No Importance" consists of two ten-line stanzas and a standard number of syllables. Even in this superficially traditional work, ______ Al-Malaika was breaking new ground by memorializing an anonymous woman rather than a famous man.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A) in fact,
B) though,
C) therefore,
D) moreover,

**Correct answer: B** (College Board's key)

**Why:** The first sentence establishes that although Al-Malaika is famous for free verse, she still used traditional forms in "Elegy." The last sentence qualifies that point: even in this traditional work, she was "breaking new ground" by choosing an anonymous woman as her subject. The two ideas disagree - traditional form vs. new ground. "Though" (along with "even" earlier in the sentence) signals this contrast/qualification: she subverted tradition even when using traditional forms. The relationship is contrast - a qualification of the point just made.

**A is wrong:** "In fact" would signal that the last sentence STRENGTHENS or amplifies the truth of the first. But the last sentence qualifies and partially opposes it, not amplifies it. **Trap: Emphasis vs. Contrast.**

**C is wrong:** "Therefore" would signal that the last sentence is a CONSEQUENCE of the first. But breaking new ground is not caused by her use of traditional forms - it stands in tension with it. **Trap: Cause-Effect vs. Contrast.**

**D is wrong:** "Moreover" would signal that the last sentence merely ADDS more information in the same direction. But it does not add to the point about traditional forms; it qualifies and complicates it. **Trap: Addition vs. Contrast.**

The lesson: all three wrong answers are grammatically perfect. They fail on logic alone. This is why you must name the relationship (contrast/qualification) before looking at the choices.

### Trap Analysis

**Trap 1: Contrast vs. Concession confusion.** Both involve opposing ideas, but concession admits the first point before countering. If the passage says "Granted, X. But Y," that is concession. If it simply says "X. However, Y," that is contrast. The SAT frequently includes one of each as answer choices. Test for the "yes, but..." structure: is the first idea being granted as valid before the pivot? If yes, concession (granted, admittedly, to be sure). If the first idea is simply opposed, contrast (however, in contrast).

**Trap 2: Cause-Effect vs. Sequence confusion.** If B merely follows A in time, use sequence ("then," "subsequently"). If A causes B, use cause-effect ("therefore," "consequently"). The distinction is whether the relationship is temporal or causal. "The comet passed. Subsequently, the museum opened" is sequence (no causation). "The comet passed. Consequently, the museum opened" would be absurd - no causal link exists.

**Trap 3: Addition vs. Example confusion.** If sentence 2 is a specific instance of the general claim in sentence 1, the relationship is example ("for instance," "for example"). If sentence 2 adds a new but related point without illustrating sentence 1, the relationship is addition ("furthermore," "moreover").

**Trap 4: "However" overuse.** "However" is flexible, so students default to it. But it is frequently a wrong answer when the relationship is addition, example, cause-effect, or concession. Do not pick "however" unless you have confirmed a direct contrast relationship.

**Trap 5: Similarly vs. Likewise vs. Conversely.** "Similarly" and "likewise" signal similarity. "Conversely" signals opposition. Students occasionally pick "conversely" when the passage shows similarity, or vice versa.

**Trap 6: Therefore vs. However - same sentence structure.** Two transitions can be grammatically identical in the sentence. The difference is purely logical. If you rely on "sounding right" instead of defining the relationship, you will pick traps.

**Trap 7: Meanwhile vs. However.** "Meanwhile" signals simultaneity (time) or a parallel thread. Students sometimes pick it for contrast because it can appear in narrative contrasts, but it primarily denotes concurrent action, not logical opposition. "Meanwhile" is temporal; "however" is contrastive.

**Trap 8: Otherwise vs. However.** "Otherwise" signals a negative conditional: if not X, then Y. "However" signals contrast. If the passage does not present a conditional negative consequence, "otherwise" is wrong.

**Trap 9: However vs. Nevertheless (the polarity trap).** Both are contrast words, but "however" marks a simple shift in direction while "nevertheless" marks a conclusion that survives a conceded obstacle. When both appear as choices, the passage's exact structure decides: if the first idea is a difficulty and the second asserts something that holds anyway, "nevertheless"; if the second idea simply differs from the first, "however."

**Trap 10: The copycat pair.** Two choices with the same meaning ("therefore" and "as a result"; "for example" and "for instance") - both are usually wrong, because the test never has two correct answers. Eliminate the pair, then pick from the remaining two based on your relationship label.

**Trap 11: Cause-Effect direction reversal.** "Therefore" and "as a result" make sentence 2 the effect of sentence 1. If sentence 2 actually states the CAUSE of sentence 1's effect, you need "because," "since," or "this is because." Reading the direction backwards is a top-tier trap on hard transitions.

**Trap 12: The flexible prediction trap.** You predict "therefore," it is absent, and you panic-pick a contrast word. Instead, scan for the same FAMILY: consequently, as a result, thus, accordingly, hence all do the same job. A missing exact word is not a signal to change your relationship label.

Urban green spaces provide recreational areas for residents. ________, they help reduce the urban heat island effect by cooling surrounding neighborhoods.

A) However
B) For example
C) In addition
D) Consequently

---

**Correct answer: C**

**Why:** Sentence 2 adds another benefit of urban green spaces. The relationship is addition.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 4 - "However" overused.** There is no opposition between the two benefits.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Addition vs. Example confusion.** Sentence 2 is not a specific instance of sentence 1; it is a separate benefit.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect vs. Sequence confusion.** While green spaces do cool neighborhoods, sentence 2 is not presented as a consequence of sentence 1's claim about recreation. The relationship is additive, not causal.

#### [Easy] Contrast

The ancient Greek city-states Athens and Sparta were both powerful in the fifth century BCE. ________, their societies were organized very differently: Athens valued democracy and intellectual pursuits, while Sparta emphasized military discipline and oligarchic rule.

A) Furthermore
B) Similarly
C) However
D) For instance

---

**Correct answer: C**

**Why:** Sentence 1 establishes a similarity (both powerful). Sentence 2 pivots to a difference. This is contrast.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Addition vs. Example confusion.** "Furthermore" adds a point in the same direction. Here, the direction reverses.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 5 - Similarly vs. Conversely error.** "Similarly" signals similarity; the passage emphasizes difference.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Contrast confusion.** Sentence 2 is not an example of sentence 1; it contrasts with it.

#### [Easy] Example / Illustration

Some plant species delay blooming until environmental conditions are favorable. ________, the alpine dryad, a small Arctic flower, waits for the snow to melt before producing its blossoms.

A) For instance,
B) However,
C) Therefore,
D) Moreover,

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** Sentence 1 makes a general claim about a category (plants that delay blooming). Sentence 2 names one specific member of that category (the alpine dryad). That is the definition of exemplification: a concrete instance of the broader statement.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 4 - "However" overused.** There is no opposition. The dryad is a case that CONFIRMS the general claim, not one that contradicts it.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect vs. Example confusion.** The dryad's behavior is not caused by sentence 1's claim; it is an illustration of it.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Addition vs. Example confusion.** Sentence 2 is not a separate new point; it is a specific example of the point just made. When the second sentence fits INSIDE the first sentence's category, the relationship is example, not addition.

#### [Medium] Cause-Effect

The volcanic eruption of Mount Tambora in 1815 released vast amounts of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere. ________, global temperatures dropped by as much as 0.7 degrees Celsius the following year, causing widespread crop failures.

A) Nevertheless
B) For example
C) As a result
D) Meanwhile

---

**Correct answer: C**

**Why:** The eruption (cause) directly produced a temperature drop (effect). This is cause-effect.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Contrast/Concession confusion.** There is no opposition. The temperature drop is an expected consequence, not a counterpoint.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Cause-Effect confusion.** Sentence 2 is not an example of the eruption; it is its consequence.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 7 - Meanwhile vs. However confusion.** "Meanwhile" signals simultaneity, but the temperature drop happened the following year, not at the same time.

#### [Medium] Concession

The new public transit line cost significantly more than originally budgeted. ________, it has already reduced commute times by an average of 15 minutes and increased ridership by 20%.

A) Granted
B) For instance
C) Conversely
D) In addition

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** Sentence 1 presents a negative (cost overrun). Sentence 2 presents positive outcomes. "Granted" concedes the cost problem while setting up the positive counterpoint. The structure is "yes, it cost more, but..."

**B is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Concession confusion.** Sentence 2 is not an example of the cost overrun.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Contrast vs. Concession confusion.** "Conversely" signals an inverted relationship (X is true; conversely, the opposite is true). Here, the relationship is concession (admitting a drawback before giving the upside), not a direct opposite.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Addition vs. Concession confusion.** "In addition" would add a similar point, but sentence 2 is not in the same evaluative direction as sentence 1.

#### [Medium] Emphasis

The proposal appeared to offer only modest benefits. ________, independent analysts calculated that it would save the city $40 million over five years.

A) In fact,
B) However,
C) Consequently,
D) For instance,

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** Sentence 2 verifies and INTENSIFIES the situation set up in sentence 1 - the proposal that "appeared" modest actually saves $40 million. "In fact" is the classic transition for presenting a stronger truth that corrects or sharpens the impression just given.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 4 - "However" overused.** Sentence 2 does not oppose sentence 1; it refines it. The appearance of modesty is corrected by the real number - that is emphasis, not contrast.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect vs. Emphasis confusion.** The $40 million figure is not caused by the appearance of modesty; it is the actual magnitude beneath that appearance.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Emphasis confusion.** The $40 million is not an example of the category "modest benefits"; it contradicts that impression and reveals the true scale.

#### [Hard] Contrast vs. Concession

Many critics argue that social media has weakened genuine interpersonal connections by replacing face-to-face interaction with superficial online engagement. ________, some studies suggest that social media can strengthen existing relationships by facilitating frequent communication across distances.

A) Granted
B) Consequently
C) In fact
D) Nevertheless

---

**Correct answer: D**

**Why:** Sentence 1 presents a critical position. Sentence 2 presents a countervailing finding that contradicts the implication that social media only weakens connections. This is contrast, not concession, because sentence 1 is not being "granted" as valid before pushing back - it is being directly opposed. "Nevertheless" signals "despite what critics say, this other finding holds."

**A is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Concession vs. Contrast confusion.** If the passage began "Granted, social media has drawbacks," then pivoted, that would be concession. But here, sentence 1 presents the critics' position as a claim, and sentence 2 presents a finding that contravenes it. The passage does not first admit the critics are right.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect vs. Contrast confusion.** Sentence 2 is not a consequence of the critics' argument.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Emphasis vs. Contrast confusion.** "In fact" would strengthen or clarify sentence 1, but sentence 2 pushes against sentence 1.

#### [Hard] Cause-Effect with Trap (Sequence Decoy)

Researchers observed that corporate investment in renewable energy increased by 40% between 2018 and 2023. ________, the cost of solar photovoltaic technology fell by nearly 90% during the same period, making it more economically viable.

A) However
B) Therefore
C) Previously
D) Similarly

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** The cost decline (cause) enabled the investment increase (effect). The causal relationship runs from cheaper technology to increased investment, even though cost is mentioned second. "Therefore" captures this causal link.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 4 - "However" overused.** There is no opposition. Both facts point in the same direction (renewable energy growing).

**C is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Sequence vs. Cause-Effect confusion.** "Previously" would mean the cost decline happened before the investment increase, but the passage describes concurrent trends, not a before-and-after sequence. More importantly, the relationship is causal, not temporal.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 5 - Similarly vs. Contrast confusion.** The two facts are not merely similar - one explains the other.

#### [Hard] However vs. Nevertheless

The expedition's radio equipment malfunctioned on the third day, cutting off all communication with base camp. ________, the team completed the crossing and returned with its data intact.

A) However,
B) Nevertheless,
C) Therefore,
D) For instance,

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** Sentence 1 presents an obstacle (communication failure). Sentence 2 asserts that the team succeeded ANYWAY. That is the "nevertheless" structure: a conclusion that survives a difficulty. The negative-to-positive polarity (malfunction -> success despite it) is exactly what "nevertheless" signals.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 9 - However vs. Nevertheless.** "However" would mark a simple shift in direction, but here the second sentence does not merely differ from the first - it triumphs over it. The obstacle in sentence 1 is explicitly overcome in sentence 2, which is the "despite this, it still happened" structure. "However" loses the "despite the obstacle" meaning.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect vs. Concession.** The team's success is not caused by the equipment failure. If anything, the failure made success harder.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Concession.** The crossing is not an example of the malfunction; it is an outcome that stands despite it.

#### [Hard] The Copycat Pair

The "Gordie Howe hat trick," an unofficial statistic in which a hockey player scores a goal, records an assist, and gets in a fight in the same game, is named after hockey great Gordie Howe. ________, Howe only achieved this feat twice in his professional career, far fewer times than many other players.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A) However,
B) Therefore,
C) As a result,
D) Subsequently,

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** Sentence 1 introduces the feat and its namesake. Sentence 2 reveals an ironic twist: the man it is named after rarely achieved it. The relationship is contrast - the fact about Howe cuts against what the naming implies. "However" signals that shift.

**B and C are wrong together:** **Trap 10 - The copycat pair.** "Therefore" and "as a result" mean essentially the same thing (cause-effect). Since the SAT never has two correct answers, and both would fit identically if the relationship were causal, the relationship must NOT be causal - and indeed it is not: Howe's rare achievement is not caused by the statistic being named after him. Eliminate the pair, and the contrast relationship becomes obvious. This is the "eliminate copycats" move from Khan Academy - when you see two near-synonyms, both are usually traps.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Sequence vs. Contrast.** "Subsequently" would place Howe's two achievements after the naming in time, which the passage does not establish. The relationship is logical (contrast), not temporal.

#### [Very Hard] Cause-Effect Direction Reversal

The eastern monarch butterfly population has declined by over 80% in the last two decades. ________, the loss of milkweed plants - the only food source for monarch caterpillars - due to herbicide use has reduced their reproductive success.

A) For instance,
B) However,
C) This decline is largely because
D) As a result,

---

**Correct answer: C**

**Why:** This question reverses the typical cause-effect order. Sentence 1 states the effect (population decline). Sentence 2 states the cause (milkweed loss). "This decline is largely because" correctly signals that the second sentence explains the first.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Explanation confusion.** Sentence 2 is not an example of the decline; it is the reason for it.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 1 - Contrast vs. Explanation confusion.** There is no opposition. Sentence 2 explains sentence 1.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect direction confusion.** "As a result" would make sentence 2 the effect of sentence 1, which reverses the actual causal direction. This is the classic cause-effect direction trap.

#### [Very Hard] Three-Way Contrast: However vs. Conversely vs. On the Other Hand

In countries with strong labor protections, employers tend to hire slowly and invest heavily in training each worker. ________, in countries with weak labor protections, employers hire quickly and frequently, relying on short-term contracts.

A) However,
B) Conversely,
C) On the other hand,
D) Meanwhile,

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** Sentence 1 describes one situation (strong protections -> slow hiring, heavy training). Sentence 2 describes the mirrored situation (weak protections -> quick hiring, short contracts). This is an INVERTED relationship: each element flips to its opposite. "Conversely" is the precise word for a relationship where the direction of a stated relationship reverses.

**A is wrong:** **Trap 4 - "However" overused.** "However" marks a simple opposition, but here the two sentences are not merely opposed - they are structurally MIRRORED, with every element flipping. "However" is not wrong enough to be eliminated for grammar, but it is too weak and imprecise for a mirrored contrast. When the passage sets two parallel situations against each other with corresponding flipped details, "conversely" or "in contrast" is the better fit.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 5 - On the other hand misuse.** "On the other hand" introduces an alternative perspective on the SAME question ("raising taxes may fund programs; on the other hand, it may slow growth"). Here, sentence 2 is not an alternative view of the same issue - it is the mirrored situation in a different country. The two sentences describe parallel cases, not two sides of one case.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 7 - Meanwhile vs. Contrast.** "Meanwhile" signals simultaneity or parallel timing. The passage does not establish that both countries' labor patterns occur at the same time - it establishes an inverted logical relationship. The contrast family, not the time family, is required.

#### [Very Hard] Multi-Clause Concession

The study's findings are impressive: the treatment reduced hospital readmissions by 30% over two years. To be sure, the trial enrolled only 200 patients at a single hospital, and the results have not yet been replicated elsewhere. ________, the magnitude of the effect is large enough that many hospitals have already begun adopting the protocol.

A) Even so,
B) For example,
C) As a result,
D) In addition,

---

**Correct answer: A**

**Why:** This passage performs a full concession cycle. Sentence 1 makes a strong claim (30% reduction). Sentence 2 concedes a limitation ("To be sure, the trial enrolled only 200 patients... not replicated"). Sentence 3 asserts that the main point STILL stands (hospitals are adopting the protocol anyway). "Even so" is the transition that says: despite the limitation just conceded, the claim holds. It completes the "yes, but" structure that "To be sure" opened.

**B is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Example vs. Concession.** The hospital adoptions are not an example of the limitation in sentence 2; they are evidence that the main claim survives it.

**C is wrong:** **Trap 2 - Cause-Effect vs. Concession.** The adoptions are not caused by the limitation (small sample). If anything, the limitation would make adoptions LESS likely - the adoptions happen despite it.

**D is wrong:** **Trap 3 - Addition vs. Concession.** "In addition" would add a point in the same direction as sentence 2, but sentence 3 reverses sentence 2's direction - it pushes back against the limitation. The relationship is concession (limitation -> point survives), not addition.

## Rhetorical Effectiveness (Concision, Style, Precision)

Though not a separate testing point in the official framework, rhetorical effectiveness is tested within the Expression of Ideas domain through questions about **concision**, **style**, **tone**, and **precision**. These appear as answer choices where multiple options are grammatical but differ in efficiency, register, or clarity.

### Definition in Simplest Terms

Sometimes the test gives you four grammatically perfect sentences and asks which one is the BEST writing. "Best" here means three things, usually in combination:

1. **Concision** - the fewest words that say the same thing (no "the reason is because" when "because" works, no "collaborate together" when "collaborate" works).
2. **Style / tone** - the words match the passage's register (no "totally psyched" in an academic passage).
3. **Precision** - the words mean exactly what the passage intends (not "went down" when "plummeted" or "declined" is meant; not "proved" when the evidence only "suggests").

The correct choice is the one that is concise WITHOUT losing meaning, precise WITHOUT overstating, and tonally consistent with the passage. A choice that is shorter but vaguer is not more concise - it is just shorter. "Shortest" and "best" are only equal when meaning and tone are held constant.

### The "Which Choice Is Most Effective" Format

Some questions in this domain are phrased as effectiveness questions rather than pure concision questions: "Which choice most effectively..." or "Which choice best..." These combine two or three of the criteria above. The method:

1. **Read the full sentence with each option mentally.** Do not compare fragments in isolation.
2. **First filter: eliminate anything ungrammatical or awkward.** The SAT rarely makes you choose between two grammatical options and one broken one at the hard level - but at the easy level, one choice is often outright ungrammatical. Kill it first.
3. **Second filter: eliminate anything redundant.** "Consensus agreement" and "the reason is because" die here.
4. **Third filter: eliminate anything off-register.** Slang in a formal passage dies here.
5. **Fourth filter: among survivors, choose the most precise.** If two options are equally concise and grammatical, the one with the more exact meaning wins.
6. **Final check: does the shortest option preserve ALL the meaning?** If trimming cut a necessary detail or changed the claim, the shorter option is wrong.

### Concision

The SAT values direct, non-redundant expression. The shortest grammatically correct option that preserves all necessary meaning is usually correct.

**Redundancy patterns to eliminate:**

| Redundant | Concise |
|---|---|
| collaborate together | collaborate |
| repeat again | repeat |
| advance forward | advance |
| return back | return |
| past history | history |
| end result | result |
| each and every | each (or every) |
| the reason is because | the reason is that (or because) |
| in spite of the fact that | although |
| due to the fact that | because |
| at this point in time | now |
| consensus agreement | consensus |
| free gift | gift |
| new innovation | innovation |
| unexpected surprise | surprise |
| close proximity | proximity |
| future plans | plans |
| basic fundamentals | fundamentals |
| absolutely essential | essential |
| combine together | combine |
| final outcome | outcome |
| general public | public (in most contexts) |
| refer back | refer |
| first began | began |
| added bonus | bonus |
| true facts | facts |
| brief moment | moment |

**Wordy constructions to tighten (from official SAT question styles):**

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| in the event that | if |
| in order to | to |
| at the present time | now |
| for the purpose of | for |
| has the ability to | can |
| is able to | can |
| the majority of | most |
| a large number of | many |
| in spite of the fact that | although |
| with regard to | about / regarding |
| on a daily basis | daily |
| in close proximity to | near |
| there is no doubt that | undoubtedly (or nothing) |
| it is important to note that | (delete - the sentence is important on its own) |

**The deletion test:** if you can delete a word or phrase and the sentence means exactly the same thing, delete it. "It is important to note that the results were mixed" becomes "The results were mixed" with zero meaning lost. That is the core move of concision.

### Style and Tone

The correct choice must match the passage's register. Academic passages require formal language. Narrative or informal passages may allow contractions and everyday language.

| Register | Characteristics | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Formal / Academic | No contractions; precise vocabulary; third person | "The experiment yielded inconclusive results." |
| Neutral / Expository | Clear, straightforward; some abstraction | "Several factors contributed to the outcome." |
| Informal / Narrative | May include contractions; conversational | "It turned out the data didn't show what we expected." |

**How to identify the passage's register fast:** look at the vocabulary already in the passage. Words like "considerable," "initiative," "sustainable," "yielded," "consequently" signal formal. Words like "turned out," "pretty," "a lot," "stuff" signal informal. Match the blank to the neighborhood. Also watch the subject: a passage about a museum exhibition uses formal language; a passage quoting a scientist's memoir may be more personal.

**Tone words to know:** the SAT often tests connotation pairs - "debate" vs. "argue about," "assert" vs. "claim," "question" vs. "doubt." The correct word must match both the register AND the strength of the claim (a passage that says "researchers debate whether..." cannot flip to "researchers prove that...").

### Precision

The correct choice uses words that convey the exact intended meaning without overstatement or understatement.

| Imprecise | Precise |
|---|---|
| very big | massive / substantial / enormous |
| got better | improved / recovered / stabilized |
| a lot of people | many / the majority / a significant number |
| went up | increased / rose / escalated |
| bad | harmful / detrimental / adverse / negative |
| very important | crucial / pivotal / essential |
| a long time | decades / centuries (be specific) |
| shows | suggests / indicates / demonstrates (match strength) |
| says | argues / claims / notes / reports (match the speech act) |

**Precision vs. overstatement:** the SAT loves testing whether your word's strength matches the evidence. If the notes say a study "found" a correlation, a choice saying the study "proved" causation overstates. If the passage says researchers "suspect" a mechanism, a choice saying they "demonstrated" it overstates. Precision cuts both ways: too weak is imprecise, too strong is also imprecise.

### Examples

#### [Easy] Concision

The scientist was able to successfully complete the experiment despite the fact that the equipment was outdated.

A) was able to successfully complete
B) successfully completed
C) was able to successfully complete the experiment despite the fact that
D) completed successfully the experiment

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** "Successfully completed" is the most direct. "Was able to" is wordy. "Despite the fact that" can be shortened, but the shortest entirely correct option is B.

**A is wrong:** **Wordy.** "Was able to" adds unnecessary words.

**C is wrong:** **Redundant and wordy.** "Despite the fact that" should be "although" or "despite."

**D is wrong:** **Awkward word order.** "Completed successfully the experiment" violates standard English syntax (adverb placement).

#### [Easy] Redundancy

The committee reached a consensus agreement on the new policy after several hours of discussion.

A) reached a consensus agreement
B) reached a consensus
C) reached an agreement of consensus
D) reached a complete consensus agreement

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** "Consensus" already means "general agreement." Adding "agreement" repeats the meaning. "Reached a consensus" says everything in three words.

**A is wrong:** **Redundant.** "Consensus agreement" - consensus IS agreement.

**C is wrong:** **Wordy and awkward.** "An agreement of consensus" adds words and distorts the phrase without adding meaning.

**D is wrong:** **Double redundancy.** "Complete consensus agreement" piles "complete" onto an already redundant pair. This is the classic over-stuffed distractor the SAT uses to test whether you will pick the longest "safe" option.

#### [Medium] Style and Tone

The city's initiative to expand bike lanes has been met with considerable enthusiasm from residents, who ________ the program as a step toward sustainable urban living.

A) are totally psyched about
B) regard
C) look upon with a generally favorable attitude toward
D) have expressed a predominantly positive opinion regarding

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** "Regard" matches the formal register of the passage ("considerable enthusiasm," "sustainable urban living"). It is precise and concise.

**A is wrong:** **Tone mismatch.** "Totally psyched about" is too informal for an academic-adjacent passage.

**C is wrong:** **Wordy.** "Look upon with a generally favorable attitude toward" is needlessly verbose.

**D is wrong:** **Wordy and indirect.** "Have expressed a predominantly positive opinion regarding" could be replaced with "regard" or "view."

#### [Hard] Precision

The correlation between screen time and sleep disruption among adolescents is well-established, but researchers ________ whether the relationship is causal or merely associative.

A) dispute
B) argue about
C) debate
D) are not in total agreement as to

---

**Correct answer: C**

**Why:** "Debate" precisely conveys active scholarly disagreement over an unresolved question. It fits the academic register and the specific context of a scientific question.

**A is wrong:** **Imprecise.** "Dispute" suggests outright rejection of a claim, which overstates the position. Researchers do not reject the possibility of a causal link; they are examining it.

**B is wrong:** **Register mismatch and slightly informal.** "Argue about" is less formal and less precise than "debate" in an academic context.

**D is wrong:** **Wordy and indirect.** While grammatically correct, it is far less concise than "debate" without adding clarity.

#### [Hard] "Which Choice Is Most Effective" (Combined Format)

The historian's account of the battle, which was published in 1962 and which drew on interviews with surviving veterans, remains the definitive study of the conflict.

A) The historian's account of the battle, which was published in 1962 and which drew on interviews with surviving veterans, remains
B) The historian's account of the battle, published in 1962 and drawing on interviews with surviving veterans, remains
C) The historian's account of the battle, which was published in 1962 and drew on interviews from veterans who survived the battle, remains
D) Published in 1962, the historian's account of the battle, which drew on interviews with surviving veterans, remains

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** "Published in 1962 and drawing on interviews with surviving veterans" says everything the other choices say in fewer words, with the participial phrases properly attached to "account." No meaning is lost: the publication year, the interview source, and the veterans' survival are all preserved.

**A is wrong:** **Wordy (relative clauses).** "Which was published in 1962 and which drew on interviews" uses two full relative clauses where participial phrases do the same job. "Which was... and which drew" is the classic doubled-relative-clause wordiness.

**C is wrong:** **Wordy and redundant.** "Veterans who survived the battle" adds "who survived the battle" - but "surviving veterans" already says it. Also keeps the first "which was" clause.

**D is wrong:** **Awkward emphasis.** Moving "Published in 1962" to the front changes the sentence's rhythm and emphasis, and the sentence now buries the main clause ("the historian's account... remains") behind two interrupting phrases. It is grammatical but less effective - the clearest version keeps the modifiers attached to the noun they describe.

#### [Very Hard] Precision vs. Overstatement

According to the study's authors, the results ________ that the new drug reduces inflammation, though they caution that larger trials are needed to confirm the effect.

A) prove
B) suggest
C) guarantee
D) establish beyond doubt

---

**Correct answer: B**

**Why:** The sentence itself contains the caution: "larger trials are needed to confirm the effect." That caution means the results do not yet prove anything - they only point toward an effect. "Suggest" matches the evidence's strength exactly.

**A is wrong:** **Overstatement.** "Prove" contradicts the authors' own caution. If the results proved the effect, larger trials would not be needed to "confirm" it. The sentence would become self-contradictory.

**C is wrong:** **Overstatement (stronger).** "Guarantee" is even stronger than "prove" - the drug's effect cannot be guaranteed by one study's results.

**D is wrong:** **Overstatement (wordy).** "Establish beyond doubt" is a wordy way of saying "prove," and it overstates for the same reason. This choice shows how overstatement and wordiness often travel together in the wrong answers.

## A3.4 Cross-Domain Edge Cases

Some questions blur the boundary between Expression of Ideas and Standard English Conventions:

- **Transitions + Boundaries:** The transition word may affect whether a semicolon or period is needed. In Expression of Ideas, the primary test is logical relationship; but the correct choice must also be punctuated correctly. (Example: "therefore" after a semicolon vs. "therefore," starting a new sentence - both can be right depending on the punctuation already present. If two choices are logically identical, check the punctuation.) See the A4 manual for the boundary rules.
- **Rhetorical Synthesis + Concision:** Among choices that all accurately reflect the notes, the difference may be concision - the shortest faithful sentence that serves the goal wins. When two rhetorical synthesis choices both pass the goal test and the accuracy test, the more concise one is usually right (and the wordier one usually has a subtle distortion hidden inside).
- **Style + Word in Context:** A question may test whether a word's connotation fits the passage's tone. This overlaps with the Craft & Structure domain's "Words in Context" skill. The A2 manual covers the vocabulary side; this file covers the register side.
- **Transitions + Rhetorical Synthesis:** Some hard rhetorical synthesis choices use transition words to frame the relationship between facts. A choice that says "Erasure uses tapes; Home Grown, however, uses pushpins" frames the facts as a contrast - which kills a "similarity" goal. The transition word inside the choice IS the signal of the relationship. Read those framing words ("however," "whereas," "both," "unlike," "similarly") as goal-checkers.
- **Concision + Meaning Shift:** The shortest option is only correct if it preserves meaning. A choice that deletes "nearly" from "nearly 80%" changes the fact. On any concision question, verify the numbers and qualifiers survive the trim.

## Complete Transition Taxonomy Table

| Category | Function | Key Transitions |
|---|---|---|
| Sequence | Order in time or steps | then, subsequently, first/second/third, next, afterward, later, previously, finally, meanwhile, eventually |
| Contrast | Direct opposition | however, conversely, in contrast, on the other hand, nevertheless, nonetheless, yet, whereas, by contrast, still, even so |
| Concession | Admit point then counter | granted, admittedly, of course, certainly, to be sure, despite this, although |
| Cause-Effect | One produces the other | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, accordingly, because, since, in turn |
| Addition | Extend or reinforce | furthermore, moreover, in addition, additionally, also, besides, indeed, similarly, likewise |
| Emphasis | Strengthen or clarify | indeed, in fact, notably, particularly, specifically, above all, especially |
| Example | Illustrate general point | for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, namely, to illustrate |
| Result/Exception | Logical outcome or alternative | as a result, therefore, otherwise, instead, rather, alternatively |

**The two families that overlap across categories:** "indeed" appears in both Addition and Emphasis (it adds AND strengthens), and "as a result"/"therefore" appear in both Cause-Effect and Result/Exception. When a word appears in two rows, the passage's exact relationship decides which job it is doing. "Indeed" after a claim that it confirms = emphasis; "indeed" adding a further confirming fact = addition. Both are "same direction" moves, so they rarely create wrong-answer confusion - the important split is same-direction (addition/emphasis/example) vs. direction-change (contrast/concession).

## One-Page Cheat Sheet

### Rhetorical Synthesis
- **Read the goal FIRST.** Not the notes. The goal decides which facts matter and what shape the answer must take.
- **Two-question test:** Does this accomplish the goal? Does it accurately reflect the notes? If either answer is no, eliminate.
- **Be strict with "AND" goals.** If the goal demands two elements ("scientist AND her field," "duration AND outcome"), both must appear. Half-done = wrong.
- **Breadth for summary, depth for emphasis.** Know which goal type you have.
- **The goal verb predicts the shape.** Emphasize a difference = contrast structure with both subjects. Emphasize a similarity = "both" + shared trait. Introduce = name + define. Support = evidence + claim. List all = every member present.
- **Re-read the goal last.** Before selecting, confirm alignment.
- **Check it reads naturally.** The right answer always sounds like a real sentence.

### Transitions
- **Cover the choices.** Define the relationship before you see the options.
- **Paraphrase both sides, then name the relationship in your own words.** "Sentence 2 pushes back against sentence 1" = contrast. "Sentence 2 is caused by sentence 1" = cause-effect.
- **Predict your own word.** "This is contrast, so I need 'however'."
- **Be flexible.** Your exact word may be absent; pick a word from the same family.
- **Eliminate copycats.** Two near-synonym choices ("therefore" / "as a result") are usually both wrong.
- **Contrast vs. Concession:** Concession admits the first point is valid before countering ("yes, but..."). Contrast simply opposes.
- **However vs. Nevertheless:** However = simple shift. Nevertheless = the point survives a conceded obstacle.
- **Cause-Effect vs. Sequence:** Temporal order is sequence. Causal relationship is cause-effect. "Later" does not mean "because."
- **Cause-Effect direction:** Effect stated first + cause second = "because"/"since." Cause first + effect second = "therefore"/"as a result."
- **Addition vs. Example:** Specific instance = example. New related point = addition.
- **Never pick "however" by default.** Verify actual contrast.

### Rhetorical Effectiveness
- **Shortest correct option wins** - unless it changes meaning or tone.
- **Run the deletion test.** If deleting a phrase loses nothing, delete it.
- **Match the passage's register.** Academic passages need formal language.
- **Choose precise words.** "Debate" not "argue about"; "decline" not "went down"; "suggest" not "prove" (unless the evidence proves).
- **Verify numbers and qualifiers survive the trim.** "Nearly 80%" is not "80%."

## Numbered Traps List

1. **Factually correct, rhetorically wrong** - The choice is accurate but serves a different goal. Most common Rhetorical Synthesis trap.
2. **Goal achieved, notes contradicted** - Serves the goal but introduces information not in the notes or contradicts them. Watch for "exclusively," "only," "all," "proved."
3. **Wrong scope** - Too narrow for summarize; too broad for emphasize.
4. **Logical distortion** - Reverses cause-effect, overgeneralizes, assumes correlation = causation.
5. **Wrong audience** - Uses unexplained jargon for an audience specified as unfamiliar with the topic.
6. **Both subjects mentioned, wrong relationship** - For compare/contrast goals, describes similarity when notes show difference or vice versa. "Whereas" kills a similarity goal; "both" kills a difference goal.
7. **The "and" goal, half done** - Goal demands two elements ("scientist AND her field"); the choice delivers only one.
8. **Same facts, wrong perspective** - Two choices draw on identical bullets but frame them differently (similarity vs. difference; raw facts vs. claim). Check the angle, not just the facts.
9. **The impressive-fact lure** - The most striking bullet is usually the bait if the goal does not ask for it.
10. **Contrast vs. Concession** - Both involve opposing ideas, but concession requires "yes, but..." structure. Pure contrast does not.
11. **Cause-Effect vs. Sequence** - Temporal order is not the same as causation. If B follows A in time but A does not cause B, it is sequence, not cause-effect.
12. **Addition vs. Example** - If the second sentence illustrates the first, it is example. If it adds a new point, it is addition.
13. **"However" overuse** - Frequently appears as a wrong answer when the relationship is addition, example, cause-effect, or concession.
14. **Similarly vs. Conversely** - Similarity vs. opposition. These are opposites. Pick the one that matches the relationship.
15. **Cause-Effect direction reversal** - Effect-cause (sentence 2 explains why sentence 1 happened) requires "because," "since," or "this is because," not "therefore" or "as a result."
16. **However vs. Nevertheless** - However = simple shift in direction. Nevertheless = the claim survives a conceded obstacle (negative-to-positive polarity).
17. **The copycat pair** - Two choices with the same meaning ("therefore" / "as a result") - both are usually wrong. Eliminate the pair.
18. **The missing-word panic** - Your predicted transition is absent; that does not change the relationship. Scan the same family (consequently, thus, hence, accordingly) instead of switching families.
19. **Register mismatch** - Informal language in an academic passage is wrong, even if the logic is correct.
20. **Wordiness / Redundancy** - If two choices are both logical, the shorter one is usually correct. But verify meaning survives: "nearly 80%" is not "80%."
21. **Overstatement / precision failure** - A word that is too strong for the evidence ("prove" when the study only "suggests") is wrong, even if it is concise and grammatical.
22. **Double transitions** - Two transitions doing the same job in the same sentence ("But nevertheless," "Therefore, as a result") are always wrong on the SAT.

## How to Practice This Domain

Rhetorical synthesis is the single most-tested NEW format on the digital SAT - it did not exist on the paper test, it appears on every exam, and it is the last question type in each module. Transitions are its constant companion. Together they are roughly 20% of your Reading & Writing score, and they are the most trainable 20% you have. Here is the practice plan:

1. **Learn the templates cold.** Memorize the two-question test (goal? accuracy?) for rhetorical synthesis and the relationship map (the 8 categories) for transitions. Drill the goal-verb-to-shape table until you can predict the winner's structure from the goal alone.

2. **Use official materials first.** The College Board's SAT Educator Question Bank (satsuitequestionbank.collegeboard.org) has hundreds of retired rhetorical synthesis and transition questions with official explanations. Bluebook's full-length practice tests give you the real timing experience. Khan Academy's SAT course has free lessons and practice for both question types and teaches the same two-pass method described here.

3. **Practice goal recognition separately.** Before answering, cover the choices and say the goal out loud in your own words: "They want me to emphasize a similarity between two projects." Then predict the shape. This 10-second habit is the single highest-leverage skill in the domain.

4. **For transitions, name the relationship before you peek.** Cover the choices, paraphrase both sides, and commit to a relationship label and a predicted word. Only then reveal the choices. If you cannot name the relationship, you are guessing.

5. **Track your error patterns.** Most students miss rhetorical synthesis because of Trap 1 (accurate but wrong goal) or Trap 7 (half-done AND goals). Most students miss transitions because of Trap 1 (contrast vs. concession) or Trap 11 (cause-effect direction). Log every miss by trap number and drill the two or three traps that actually hit you.

6. **Time yourself.** Rhetorical synthesis questions should take 45 to 60 seconds each once the method is internalized - goal (10s), notes scan (10s), two passes over choices (20-30s), verify (5s). Transitions should take 30 to 45 seconds. If you are spending longer, you are probably reading the notes first or skipping the relationship-naming step.

7. **The 2-3 per module reality.** You will see roughly 2 to 3 rhetorical synthesis questions per module (4 to 6 per test) and a similar count of transitions. They are always near the end. Because they are the last questions, students who run out of time often guess on them - but these are the EASIEST questions in the section to answer correctly once trained, so budget your time to reach them. Two minutes of saved time earlier in the module is worth two rhetorical synthesis points at the end.
