# Domain A2: Craft and Structure - Digital SAT Reading & Writing

---

## 1. Domain Overview

**Weight:** ~28% of Reading & Writing (13-15 of 54 questions). Largest single R&W domain. That means roughly one in every four questions you see on test day belongs to this domain.

**Three Sub-Domains:**

| Sub-Domain | Approx Frequency | Core Question Type |
|---|---|---|
| A2.1 Words in Context | 5-7 per test | Pick the word that best completes / matches the text |
| A2.2 Text Structure and Purpose | 4-5 per test | What is the function / main purpose / structure? |
| A2.3 Cross-Text Connections | 3-4 per test | How would Text 2's author respond to Text 1? / What do both agree on? |

---

## 3. A2.1 Words in Context

---

### 3.1 What It Tests

Two question formats:
- **Find-the-meaning:** "As used in the text, what does 'x' most nearly mean?" (You identify the meaning of a highlighted word.)
- **Complete-the-text:** "Which choice most logically and precisely completes the text?" (You supply the word for a blank.)

Both formats test the SAME skill in opposite directions. Both reward the same habit: read the context carefully, predict your own word, then match. The College Board distinguishes these as "determine the meaning" and "use such vocabulary in a precise, contextually appropriate way" - two directions of the same skill.

---

### 3.2 Core Method: Predict Then Match

**Step 1: Cover the choices.** Read the passage. Identify context clues - transition words, punctuation, restatements, contrasts. Ignore the answer choices entirely at this stage. This is non-negotiable: once you read the choices, your brain starts rationalizing wrong ones.

**Step 2: Predict your own word.** Use simple language. The blank needs something like "harmful" or "supporting" or "changing." If you can name the idea in plain words, you have already solved most of the question. Your prediction does not need to be fancy - it needs to be accurate.

**Step 3: Uncover choices. Match definition + tone.** The right answer must fit both the denotation (literal meaning) and connotation (emotional register). A perfect synonym with the wrong attitude is a wrong answer.

**Step 4: Test the word IN the sentence.** The final check: plug your chosen answer into the blank (or replace the highlighted word with it) and read the sentence aloud in your head. If it sounds logically and tonally right, keep it.

**Step 5: Eliminate.** Wrong answers usually fail on tone, intensity, or precision - not on being completely unrelated. Name the failure mode as you eliminate: "too positive," "too weak," "right idea, wrong degree."

---

### 3.3 Context Clue Types

| Clue Type | Signal | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | "is," "means," "refers to," commas setting off an appositive | "The researchers noted an anomaly, an unexpected deviation from the predicted pattern." |
| Restatement | "in other words," "that is," "or," dashes | "The results were equivocal - open to more than one interpretation." |
| Contrast | "but," "however," "although," "despite," "yet," "while," "unlike" | "Although the senator was initially critical, her later statements were surprisingly conciliatory." |
| Comparison | "similarly," "likewise," "also," "just as" | "The first experiment yielded promising data; the replication produced equally compelling results." |
| Cause-Effect | "because," "since," "therefore," "thus," "consequently" | "Because the policy was unpopular, the administration moved to amend it." |
| Example | "for example," "such as," "including," "for instance" | "The artist's work was eclectic, ranging from classical landscapes to abstract expressionist pieces." |

---

### 3.4 The Connotation Trap

The SAT frequently tests connotation - not just whether a word fits logically, but whether its emotional register matches the passage's tone. Khan Academy's official guidance uses this pair: "promising" and "ominous" both mean "predictive of the future," but one is strongly positive and the other strongly negative, so they cannot be used in the same context. The test expects you to feel that difference.

**Connotation spectrum for "thin":**

| Word | Connotation |
|---|---|
| slender | positive (graceful, attractive) |
| thin | neutral (factual description) |
| skinny | negative (unattractive, unhealthy) |
| gaunt | strongly negative (emaciated, haggard) |

**Rule:** A word can be a perfect synonym in denotation and still be wrong because its connotation is too positive, too negative, or too extreme for the passage's tone. Match the attitude, not just the dictionary definition.

---

### 3.5 Negation Traps

Words like "although," "however," "despite," "nevertheless," "while," "whereas," "though," "yet," "even as," and "but" signal a contrast. The blank must produce the OPPOSITE of whatever the contrasting clause says. This is the single most reliable mechanical pattern in the entire domain - and the one students miss most often under time pressure.

**Example:** "Although the proposal was greeted with widespread ___ by activists, the general public remained largely indifferent."

If activists had a strong reaction and the public was indifferent, the blank must mean "strong interest" - but which direction? "Although" signals the activists' reaction contrasts with the public's indifference, so the blank could be "enthusiasm" (positive) or "outrage" (negative). The rest of the passage would decide the tone.

**Trap:** Students pick "indifference" because they associate the topic with apathy, ignoring the "although" signal. The structure demands the OPPOSITE of indifference.

**Trap, harder version:** Students pick a word that is merely different rather than OPPOSITE. "Although the results seemed promising, the researchers remained ___" needs "skeptical" or "cautious" (the opposite of convinced), not merely "interested" (which is compatible with the promising results). The contrast word demands opposition, not just difference.

---

### 3.6 Secondary Meaning Trap

The SAT frequently tests less common meanings of common words. The Assessment Framework notes that "common words can have specialized meanings in different contexts" - the classic example is "selection," which means something different in a biology context than in everyday speech. Official questions love this: a very common word like "quality" is highlighted, and the correct answer is the academic meaning ("characteristic") rather than the everyday meaning ("excellence").

| Word | Common Meaning | SAT-Tested Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| qualify | meet a requirement | limit or modify a statement |
| champion | a winner (noun) | support or defend (verb) |
| novel | a book (noun) | new, original (adj) |
| appropriate | suitable (adj) | take for oneself (verb) |
| check | verify / payment method | restrain or stop |
| discipline | field of study | punish / self-control |
| informed | having information | shaped by knowledge (adj) |
| minute | 60 seconds | extremely small |
| appreciate | feel grateful | increase in value / recognize worth |
| temper | mood (noun) | moderate or soften (verb) |
| severe | strict | very intense or extreme |
| sophisticated | culturally refined | technically complex |

**Example [Medium]:**

The historian argued that the evidence was insufficient to conclusively support the theory, though she was careful to note that this assessment should not be read as an ___ of the researcher's methodology.

A) endorsement
B) qualification
C) indictment
D) oversight

> **Analysis:** "Should not be read as an ___" follows a concession (the historian acknowledges the theory is unsupported). The blank needs a word meaning "criticism" or "rejection" since she's distancing herself from attacking the methodology outright. "Qualification" here does NOT mean "credential" but rather "limitation" - which is the wrong direction. The historian is saying her critique of the evidence is NOT a criticism of the methodology.
>
> **A is wrong** - **tone mismatch** - endorsement is positive, the historian is not praising the methodology either.
>
> **B is wrong** - **secondary meaning trap** - "qualification" here could mean "limitation" but the sentence structure says "not read as an ___" - a limitation of methodology doesn't fit.
>
> **C is correct** - indictment (strong criticism). The historian says her assessment of insufficient evidence should NOT be read as an indictment of the methodology.
>
> **D is wrong** - **wrong meaning** - oversight means mistake or supervision, neither fits.

### 3.7 Complete-the-Text Format

**Example [Medium]:**

The company's quarterly report revealed that revenue had increased substantially, but the growth was not ___ across all divisions; the consumer electronics segment actually posted a decline.

A) negligible
B) consistent
C) unprecedented
D) uniform

> The semicolon after the blank sets up a clarifying clause: the consumer electronics segment declined. This means the blank's growth was NOT happening everywhere. "Not ___" = "not happening in all divisions."
>
> **A is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - negligible means insignificant; the opposite is "significant," which doesn't fit the contrast structure.
>
> **B is plausible** - but "not consistent" means "uneven," which is close but less precise than what the sentence is saying.
>
> **C is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - unprecedented means never seen before; "not unprecedented" would mean "seen before," not relevant to the contrast.
>
> **D is correct** - uniform means the same across all parts. "Not uniform" = varying across divisions, which the example of "decline in consumer electronics" directly supports.

### 3.8 Find-the-Meaning Format

**Example [Hard]:**

The archaeologist's interpretation of the site was not merely descriptive but genuinely ___; she synthesized fragmentary evidence into a coherent narrative of daily life that had previously eluded scholars.

As used in the text, what does the word "synthesized" most nearly mean?

A) fabricated
B) summarized
C) integrated
D) simplified

> The context says "synthesized fragmentary evidence into a coherent narrative." The prefix syn- means "together." The word means "put together" disparate pieces into a whole. The tone is positive (the synthesis "had previously eluded scholars").
>
> **A is wrong** - **connotation mismatch** - fabricated means made up falsely, negative tone.
>
> **B is wrong** - **precision error** - summarizing means restating briefly, not combining fragments into a new whole.
>
> **C is correct** - integrated means combining separate elements into a unified whole.
>
> **D is wrong** - **precision error** - simplifying means making easier, not combining.

### 3.10 Answer-Choice Analysis Pattern (Real-Style Questions)

**Question 1 [Easy] - modeled on the official Bao Phi item:**

In recommending Bao Phi's collection Sông I Sing, a librarian noted that pieces by the spoken-word poet don't lose their ___ nature when printed: the language has the same pleasant musical quality on the page as it does when performed by Phi.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

A) jarring
B) scholarly
C) melodic
D) personal

> **Step 1 - predict:** The colon after "printed" restates the idea: the language has a "pleasant musical quality." The blank must mean "musical" or "songlike."
>
> **Step 2 - evaluate:**
>
> **A is wrong** - **direction error** - jarring means disagreeable or upsetting, the opposite of "pleasant musical quality."
>
> **B is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - scholarly (academic, learned) has nothing to do with musical quality. It describes a different trait entirely.
>
> **C is correct** - melodic means having a pleasant musical quality, exactly what the restatement after the colon describes.
>
> **D is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - personal (private, individual) is a plausible trait of poetry, but it is not the trait the colon restates. The clue is "musical quality," not "self-expression."
>
> **Why this is an Easy question:** the restatement after the colon gives the answer away almost word for word ("melodic" = "pleasant musical quality"). The distractors fail on clear, easily named grounds.

---

**Question 2 [Medium] - secondary meaning in action:**

The following text is from F. Scott Fitzgerald's 1925 novel The Great Gatsby.

[Jay Gatsby] was balancing himself on the dashboard of his car with that resourcefulness of movement that is so peculiarly American - that comes, I suppose, with the absence of lifting work in youth and, even more, with the formless grace of our nervous, sporadic games. This quality was continually breaking through his punctilious manner in the shape of restlessness.

As used in the text, what does the word "quality" most nearly mean?

A) Characteristic
B) Standard
C) Prestige
D) Accomplishment

> **Step 1 - predict:** "This quality" refers back to "that resourcefulness of movement." The word means "trait" or "attribute."
>
> **Step 2 - evaluate:**
>
> **A is correct** - characteristic means a distinguishing trait or attribute. Gatsby's "resourcefulness of movement" is a trait that breaks through his manner.
>
> **B is wrong** - **secondary meaning trap** - "standard" means a level of quality or a requirement, implying Gatsby is meeting a criterion set by others. The passage describes an innate trait, not a benchmark.
>
> **C is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - prestige means high status or renown; the passage is describing a physical mannerism, not social standing.
>
> **D is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - accomplishment means something achieved; resourcefulness of movement is not an achievement.
>
> **Why this is a Medium question:** "quality" is one of the most common words in English, and its everyday meaning ("excellence") is a siren song. The correct answer requires recognizing that in this context "quality" = "trait." Distractor B is the sophisticated trap: "standard" is another quality-related meaning, but it is the wrong one.

---

**Question 6 [Very Hard] - two clauses, one contrast, one cause-effect:**

Despite the project's early promise, the researchers found that the data collected under uncontrolled conditions were too ___ to support the strong claims in the grant proposal; consequently, they revised the proposal to emphasize only the findings that could be replicated reliably.

A) variable
B) voluminous
C) preliminary
D) compelling

> **Step 1 - predict:** "Despite... early promise" sets up a negative outcome. The data were "too ___ to support the strong claims." The consequence: they revised the proposal to emphasize only replicable findings. So the data must have been unreliable or inconsistent - you cannot make strong claims from data that varies wildly between runs.
>
> **Step 2 - evaluate:**
>
> **A is correct** - variable means inconsistent, changing between measurements. Uncontrolled conditions produce variable data, which cannot support strong claims. The fix (emphasizing only replicable findings) confirms the problem was inconsistency.
>
> **B is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - voluminous means large in quantity. Too much data is not a reason strong claims fail; more data usually strengthens claims.
>
> **C is wrong** - **precision error** - preliminary means early-stage. Early data can be weak, but the sentence's logic is about reliability across replications, not about timing.
>
> **D is wrong** - **direction error** - compelling means convincing; data that are too convincing would not force a revision.
>
> **Why this is a Very Hard question:** the trap is that "preliminary" sounds like a natural reason to distrust data. But the second clause ("emphasize only the findings that could be replicated reliably") points specifically to inconsistency, not earliness. The most academic-sounding distractor is not the correct one; the word that matches the REPLICATION clue is.

## 4. A2.2 Text Structure and Purpose

---

### 4.1 What It Tests

Analyze how a text is organized and determine the rhetorical purpose of a passage as a whole or of a specific sentence/part within it. The official description: "Students will analyze the structure of a text or determine the main rhetorical purpose of a text." Two sub-skills are bundled here:

- **(a) Structural analysis:** what job does a sentence, paragraph, or section do in the text (introduce, qualify, contrast, exemplify, conclude)?
- **(b) Rhetorical purpose:** why was the text written (to inform, argue, illustrate, challenge, defend)?

Three question subtypes:
- **Main Purpose:** "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?"
- **Specific Function:** "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?"
- **Overall Structure:** "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"

---

### 4.2 Core Method

**For Main Purpose:**
1. Distinguish topic (WHAT it's about) from purpose (WHY written).
2. Predict a verb phrase: "The text argues that..." / "The text explains why..." / "The text challenges the view that..."
3. Match your prediction. The right answer uses an active verb (explain, argue, illustrate, challenge, qualify, defend, propose) + the actual content.
4. Check the first verb of each answer choice first. Purpose verbs are usually wildly different (argue vs. describe vs. compare), so the verb alone often eliminates two or three choices.
5. Beware the "also true" answer: a choice that describes something the text mentions but that is not the MAIN purpose.

**For Specific Function:**
1. Read the sentence in context (before and after).
2. Ask: "What job does this sentence do?" Options: introduce, contrast, exemplify, concede, qualify, conclude, contextualize, define.
3. Label the job with one verb before looking at choices.
4. Circle transition words, especially any inside the underlined sentence ("however," "but," "because," "for example" are function markers).
5. Match. Wrong answers describe a different job or describe content instead of function.

**For Overall Structure:**
1. Identify the order of rhetorical moves.
2. Look for structural signposts: "however," "for example," "therefore," "in contrast," "moreover."
3. Map the sequence: Claim -> Evidence -> Counterargument -> Rebuttal; Question -> Analysis -> Conclusion; Theory -> Example -> Implication; Paradox -> Question -> Explanation -> Conclusion.

---

### 4.3 The Topic vs. Purpose Distinction

This is the most important distinction in the entire A2.2 sub-domain, and it is the one the test exploits most often.

- **Topic** = WHAT the text is about (the subject). "This text is about reCAPTCHA." "This text is about Florence Price."
- **Purpose** = WHY the text was written (the goal). "This text explains how von Ahn's reCAPTCHA worked." "This text provides examples of Price's importance as a composer."

**The test:** wrong answers describe the topic; the right answer describes the purpose. A choice that says "a text about posture and cognition" is a topic answer. The purpose answer says what the author is DOING with that topic: explaining, challenging, cautioning, celebrating.

**Method:** after reading, force yourself to complete the sentence "The author wrote this in order to ___." Your answer must be a verb phrase, not a noun phrase. If your answer is a noun ("about bears"), you have named the topic, not the purpose.

---

### 4.4 The Content vs. Function Trap

The most common mistake on purpose questions: choosing an answer that describes WHAT the text says (content) instead of WHY the text says it (function/purpose). The one-line rule from PrepMaven's strategy guide: "If a sentence discusses a recent experiment, its function may be to provide evidence, challenge an assumption, or introduce a limitation" - the function is never merely "to discuss the experiment."

---

### 4.5 Tone Calibration: The Full Taxonomy

#### The Full Tone Table

| Tone | Definition | Signal Words | Correct Answer Verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cautious / Qualified | limits claims with hedging | may, might, suggests, likely, possibly, seems to, under certain conditions | suggest, propose, indicate, caution |
| Skeptical / Critical | questions or challenges a view | however, overlooks, fails to account for, unconvincing, doubtful | challenge, question, critique, cast doubt on |
| Objective / Neutral | presents facts without bias | according to, data indicate, researchers found, no emotional language | report, state, present, describe |
| Explanatory / Expository | clarifies a concept or process | this means, in other words, because, as a result, works as follows | explain, describe, define, illustrate |
| Persuasive / Advocacy | argues for a position or action | should, must, clearly, undoubtedly, it is time to | advocate, urge, argue, call for |
| Defensive / Rebuttal | responds to a criticism | critics argue... but, it is true that... nevertheless, the charge that | defend, rebut, counter, respond to |

**How to use this table on test day:** when two purpose answers both seem plausible, check which tone the passage actually uses, then pick the answer whose verb matches that tone. A passage full of "may" and "suggests" cannot have the purpose "to prove" or "to demonstrate conclusively" - the correct verb will be "to suggest" or "to caution." A passage full of "should" and "must" cannot have the purpose "to describe neutrally" - the correct verb will be "to advocate."

### 4.6 Distinguishing Two Plausible Purpose Answers Using Tone

**Example [Hard]:**

Some studies have suggested that posture can influence cognition, but we should not overstate this phenomenon. A case in point: In a 2014 study, Megan O'Brien and Alaa Ahmed had subjects stand or sit while making risky simulated economic decisions. Standing is more physically unstable and cognitively demanding than sitting; accordingly, O'Brien and Ahmed hypothesized that standing subjects would display more risk aversion during the decision-making tasks than sitting subjects did, since they would want to avoid further feelings of discomfort and complicated risk evaluations. But O'Brien and Ahmed actually found no difference in the groups' performance.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A) It presents the study by O'Brien and Ahmed to critique the methods and results reported in previous studies of the effects of posture on cognition.
B) It argues that research findings about the effects of posture on cognition are often misunderstood, as in the case of O'Brien and Ahmed's study.
C) It explains a significant problem in the emerging understanding of posture's effects on cognition and how O'Brien and Ahmed tried to solve that problem.
D) It discusses the study by O'Brien and Ahmed to illustrate why caution is needed when making claims about the effects of posture on cognition.

> **Step 1 - identify the tone:** the opening sentence is the tell: "we should not overstate this phenomenon." That is cautious/qualified language - a warning against overclaiming. The passage then uses the study as "a case in point" - an illustration of why caution is needed.
>
> **Step 2 - match the verb to the tone:** the correct purpose verb should be something like "caution" or "illustrate why caution is needed."
>
> **A is wrong** - **overstatement** - the passage does not critique the METHODS of previous studies; it says the phenomenon should not be overstated. Critiquing methods is a stronger claim than the text makes.
>
> **B is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - the passage does not say the O'Brien and Ahmed study was misunderstood; it says the phenomenon in general should not be overstated. The study is an example, not a victim of misunderstanding.
>
> **C is wrong** - **content vs. function error** - this describes the study's content (what the researchers hypothesized and found) rather than the text's purpose (to caution). The text is not primarily explaining the study; it is using the study to illustrate a cautionary point.
>
> **D is correct** - the opening sentence states the caution, and the study illustrates it. The verb "illustrate why caution is needed" matches the cautious tone exactly.
>
> **Trap named:** choices B and C are sophisticated. B sounds similar but shifts the object of the misunderstanding; C describes content instead of purpose. Tone (the "should not overstate" opening) is what separates D from the rest.

### 4.7 Difficulty Ladder

**Example [Hard] - Specific Function:**

Historians have long debated the causes of the collapse of the Bronze Age civilizations of the eastern Mediterranean. Some attribute the collapse to the mysterious "Sea Peoples" invasions, while others point to internal economic decline compounded by climate change. [The truth is almost certainly more complex than any single-factor explanation allows.] A combination of environmental pressures, trade disruptions, and population movements likely contributed to a system-wide failure.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence?

A) It introduces a new theory that challenges previous explanations.
B) It concedes that the debate may be impossible to resolve.
C) It qualifies the preceding discussion by suggesting that single-cause explanations are inadequate.
D) It summarizes the main arguments presented earlier in the text.

> **A is wrong** - **overstatement** - the sentence doesn't introduce a NEW theory; it comments on the nature of existing explanations.
>
> **B is wrong** - **meaning mismatch** - the sentence says the truth is complex, not that it's impossible to resolve.
>
> **C is correct** - the sentence says single-factor explanations can't capture the complexity (qualifying the debate just presented).
>
> **D is wrong** - **function error** - the sentence doesn't summarize arguments; it critiques their oversimplification.

---

**Example [Very Hard] - Overall Structure:**

The discovery of fossilized remains of a whale in the Atacama Desert of Chile seems paradoxical. How could a marine animal end up in one of the driest places on Earth? The answer lies in plate tectonics. Millions of years ago, the region was submerged under the Pacific Ocean. As the Nazca Plate subducted beneath the South American Plate, the Andes rose and what was once seafloor became arid high desert. The whale, therefore, was not transported to the desert; the desert rose around it.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

A) It presents a paradox, poses a question about it, explains the geological process that resolves it, and states the conclusion.
B) It introduces a scientific debate, presents evidence for one side, then argues that the debate is based on a misunderstanding.
C) It describes a discovery, lists competing theories to explain it, and then endorses one theory over the others.
D) It poses a rhetorical question, answers it with a historical narrative, and then acknowledges limitations in the explanation.

> **A is correct** - Paradox (whale in desert) -> Question (how?) -> Explanation (plate tectonics/Andes rise) -> Conclusion (desert rose around it).
>
> **B is wrong** - **structure mismatch** - no debate between competing theories.
>
> **C is wrong** - **structure mismatch** - no competing theories; the text offers one explanation.
>
> **D is wrong** - **structure mismatch** - the question is not merely rhetorical (it IS answered), and there is no acknowledgment of limitations.

---

**Example [Medium] - Main Purpose (real official style, reCAPTCHA):**

In 2007, computer scientist Luis von Ahn was working on converting printed books into a digital format. He found that some words were distorted enough that digital scanners couldn't recognize them, but most humans could easily read them. Based on that finding, von Ahn invented a simple security test to keep automated "bots" out of websites. The first version of the reCAPTCHA test asked users to type one known word and one of the many words scanners couldn't recognize. Correct answers proved the users were humans and added data to the book-digitizing project.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A) To discuss von Ahn's invention of reCAPTCHA
B) To explain how digital scanners work
C) To call attention to von Ahn's book-digitizing project
D) To indicate how popular reCAPTCHA is

> **Step 1 - predict:** the text tells a story: von Ahn noticed a problem (scanners can't read distorted words), used that insight to invent reCAPTCHA, and explains how the test works. Purpose: to explain/discuss the invention of reCAPTCHA.
>
> **A is correct** - the whole text is organized around the invention: the problem that motivated it, the insight, and how it works.
>
> **B is wrong** - **content error** - scanners are mentioned only as background; the text does not explain how they work.
>
> **C is wrong** - **scope error** - the book-digitizing project is one detail (the source of the data), not the subject of the text. The reCAPTCHA test is the subject.
>
> **D is wrong** - **fabrication** - the text never mentions how popular reCAPTCHA is.

### 4.8 The Structure Signpost Bank

Memorize which signpost points to which structural move. When you see one, you know what the next sentence is DOING before you read it:

| Signpost | Structural Move |
|---|---|
| however, but, yet, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other hand | Contrast / counterargument |
| for example, for instance, such as, a case in point | Exemplify / illustrate |
| therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence | Conclude / cause-effect |
| moreover, furthermore, in addition, also | Add / elaborate |
| although, despite, even though, while it is true that | Concede / qualify |
| in other words, that is, this means, namely | Define / restate |
| first, second, finally, subsequently, in 2019, after | Sequence / chronology |
| in fact, indeed, actually | Emphasize / correct a misconception |
| however, this claim overlooks, the evidence fails to show | Skeptical challenge |

## 5. A2.3 Cross-Text Connections

---

### 5.1 What It Tests

Draw connections between two short texts on related topics. This is the ONLY question type that presents two passages. The official description: "Students will draw reasonable connections between two texts on related topics." Each text is short (25-100 words), and they are always labeled Text 1 and Text 2.

Two question subtypes:
- **Agreement:** "Based on the texts, the authors of both passages would most likely agree on which statement?"
- **Response/Disagreement:** "How would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim made in Text 1?" (Variants: "How would the researchers in Text 2 most likely describe the view presented in Text 1?" or "Which choice best describes the relationship between the two texts?")

---

### 5.2 Core Method

**Step 1: Read the QUESTION first.** The question tells you whether you are hunting for agreement or disagreement. "Both authors would most likely agree" = agreement hunt. "How would Text 2 respond to Text 1" = disagreement hunt. This saves you from reading both texts looking for the wrong relationship.

**Step 2: Read Text 1** - identify the specific claim, not just the topic. Underline signal words that announce conclusions: "argued," "found," "concluded," "contends," "suggests." Summarize the claim in a few words. The main point is often in the last sentence.

**Step 3: Read Text 2** - identify its specific claim. For a response question, look for the sentence that pushes back against Text 1's claim (often introduced by "however," "but," "while," "although"). For an agreement question, look for the point where both texts overlap.

**Step 4: Compare** - do they agree, disagree, or partially overlap? Name the relationship: "Text 1 says X is true. Text 2 says Y is also true" or "Text 1 argues P; Text 2 challenges that by pointing out Q."

**Step 5: Predict** the relationship before looking at choices. For agreement questions, predict the lowest-common-denominator statement: the simplest claim that would be hard for either author to deny. For response questions, predict the exact point of divergence.

**Step 6: Evaluate choices** - eliminate ones that misrepresent either text. Run every choice through a "Text 1 test" and a "Text 2 test": if either author would disagree with the choice as written, it is wrong.

---

### 5.4 The "How Would Text 2 Respond" Format

The response format is a disagreement hunt with a specific structure. Text 1 makes a claim (often a strong one: "X is the primary cause of Y"). Text 2 typically:

1. **Concedes** something ("While X has certainly caused some Y...") - this concession exists to make Text 2 sound fair and to tempt you into thinking they agree.
2. **Then pushes back** ("...habitat loss has affected even more species").

The question asks how Text 2's author would RESPOND to Text 1's claim. The correct answer names the pushback - the specific point where Text 2's position diverges from Text 1's. The most common wrong answers:

- **Overstatement:** claiming Text 2 fully denies something it actually concedes.
- **Understatement:** claiming Text 2 mostly agrees when it actually challenges.
- **One-sided content:** attributing to Text 2 a position it never states.
- **The reversed relationship:** swapping which author holds which position (a real trap when the question names researchers rather than "author").

**Test day mantra for this format:** "Text 1 claims X. Text 2 says X, but...". The answer is whatever comes after Text 2's "but."

### 5.5 Difficulty Ladder

**Example [Easy] - Agreement:**

**Text 1**
School uniform policies reduce visible economic disparities among students and minimize distractions related to fashion competition. Research suggests that schools with uniform requirements report fewer incidents of bullying based on clothing.

**Text 2**
While uniforms may reduce certain social pressures, they do not address the root causes of bullying. Moreover, uniforms can suppress students' individual expression during a critical period of identity formation.

Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree that:

A) School uniforms eliminate bullying entirely.
B) School uniforms have some effect on the school social environment.
C) Student self-expression is more important than reducing bullying.
D) Uniforms are an ineffective policy for any school.

> **Step 1 - read the question:** agreement hunt.
>
> **Step 2 - summarize:** Text 1: uniforms reduce clothing-based bullying and disparities (positive). Text 2: uniforms may reduce some social pressures but don't fix root causes and suppress expression (critical).
>
> **Step 3 - find the overlap:** both texts acknowledge uniforms CHANGE the social environment; they just evaluate that change differently.
>
> **A is wrong** - **overstatement** - neither text claims uniforms eliminate bullying.
>
> **B is correct** - Text 1 says uniforms reduce certain problems; Text 2 acknowledges uniforms reduce social pressures. Both agree uniforms affect the social environment, even if they evaluate that effect differently. This is the lowest-common-denominator statement.
>
> **C is wrong** - **one-sided** - Text 1 does not prioritize self-expression over bullying reduction.
>
> **D is wrong** - **overstatement** - Text 1 shows effectiveness in some areas; Text 2 critiques but doesn't call uniforms completely ineffective.

**Example [Medium] - Response:**

**Text 1**
The decline of amphibian populations worldwide is primarily driven by the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis. This pathogen has caused population collapses in hundreds of species across every continent except Antarctica, making it the most devastating infectious disease ever documented in vertebrates.

**Text 2**
While the chytrid fungus has certainly caused significant amphibian declines, habitat loss and climate change have affected even more species. The fungus tends to thrive in cool, moist environments, and climate change is actually reducing suitable habitat for the pathogen in many regions, complicating the picture of amphibian extinction risk.

How would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim in Text 1 that chytrid fungus is the primary driver of amphibian declines?

A) By agreeing that the fungus is the most important factor
B) By arguing that habitat loss and climate change affect more species overall
C) By denying that the fungus has caused any significant declines
D) By claiming that the fungus is actually beneficial for amphibians

> **Step 1 - read the question:** response/disagreement hunt. The claim to respond to: chytrid fungus is the PRIMARY driver.
>
> **Step 2 - summarize Text 1:** the fungus is the most important cause.
>
> **Step 3 - summarize Text 2:** the fungus caused significant declines, BUT habitat loss and climate change affected even more species. Text 2 concedes (sentence 1) then pushes back (the "but").
>
> **Step 4 - predict:** Text 2 would say the fungus is important but NOT the primary driver.
>
> **A is wrong** - **direction error** - Text 2 explicitly says habitat loss and climate change have affected "even more species."
>
> **B is correct** - Text 2 directly challenges the "primary driver" claim by pointing to other factors.
>
> **C is wrong** - **overstatement** - Text 2 acknowledges the fungus "has certainly caused significant amphibian declines."
>
> **D is wrong** - **fabrication** - nothing in Text 2 suggests the fungus is beneficial.

**Example [Very Hard] - Response (real official style, zooplankton):**

**Text 1**
What factors influence the abundance of species in a given ecological community? Some theorists have argued that historical diversity is a major driver of how diverse an ecological community eventually becomes: differences in community diversity across otherwise similar habitats, in this view, are strongly affected by the number of species living in those habitats at earlier times.

**Text 2**
In 2010, a group of researchers including biologist Carla Caceres created artificial pools in a New York forest. They stocked some pools with a diverse mix of zooplankton species and others with a single zooplankton species and allowed the pool communities to develop naturally thereafter. Over the course of four years, Caceres and colleagues periodically measured the species diversity of the pools, finding - contrary to their expectations - that by the end of the study there was little to no difference in the pools' species diversity.

Based on the texts, how would Caceres and colleagues (Text 2) most likely describe the view of the theorists presented in Text 1?

A) It is largely correct, but it requires a minor refinement in light of the research team's results.
B) It is not compelling as a theory regardless of any experimental data collected by the research team.
C) It may seem plausible, but it is not supported by the research team's findings.
D) It probably holds true only in conditions like those in the research team's study.

> **Step 1 - read the question:** response hunt. The view to respond to: historical diversity drives later community diversity.
>
> **Step 2 - summarize Text 1:** early species diversity determines later diversity (the "historical diversity" hypothesis).
>
> **Step 3 - summarize Text 2:** the experiment found that starting with many species vs. one species made little to no difference in the end - "contrary to their expectations." The researchers EXPECTED the historical-diversity view to be right and it failed.
>
> **Step 4 - predict:** Text 2's researchers would say the hypothesis sounds reasonable but their data did not support it.
>
> **A is wrong** - **understatement** - the findings fundamentally challenge the hypothesis; "minor refinement" is far too weak.
>
> **B is wrong** - **overstatement** - "contrary to their expectations" shows the researchers initially believed the hypothesis was plausible; they did not dismiss it as uncompelling before testing.
>
> **C is correct** - the researchers expected the view to hold ("contrary to their expectations"), found it did not, so they would describe it as plausible-sounding but unsupported by their findings.
>
> **D is wrong** - **direction error** - the findings undermine the hypothesis; they do not suggest it holds in the study's conditions (the study conditions are where it FAILED).
>
> **Trap named:** B and D are the calibrated traps. B overstates by claiming the theory was never compelling, contradicting "contrary to their expectations." D reverses the logic by suggesting the hypothesis works in the study's own conditions, when the study is precisely where it failed.

## 10. Quick-Reference Cheat Sheet

### A2.1 Words in Context

- **Cover choices. Predict your own word first.**
- Match BOTH definition and connotation.
- "Although/despite/however" = look for the OPPOSITE of the other clause.
- Secondary meanings: test the word in the blank to make sure it fits.
- **Denotation** = dictionary definition. **Connotation** = emotional tone. Both must match.
- After choosing, plug the word IN and read the sentence. If it sounds wrong, it is wrong.
- Context clue types: definition, restatement, contrast, comparison, cause-effect, example.

### A2.2 Text Structure and Purpose

- **Topic = WHAT. Purpose = WHY.**
- For specific function: label with ONE verb before looking at choices.
- Wrong answers often describe content instead of function.
- Look for structural signposts (however, for example, therefore).
- **Main purpose answers start with "To" + a verb.**
- Check the first verb of each choice before reading the rest.
- Two plausible answers? Let TONE decide: match the author's stance (cautious, skeptical, objective, explanatory, persuasive, defensive) to the purpose verb.

### A2.3 Cross-Text Connections

- Read the QUESTION first: agreement or disagreement?
- Read each text for its SPECIFIC claim, not just the general topic.
- **Agreement:** hunt the lowest-common-denominator statement. The simplest answer is often correct.
- **Disagreement:** find Text 1's claim, then find Text 2's "but."
- **Predict the relationship before looking at choices.**
- The correct answer is almost NEVER pure agreement or pure disagreement - it's usually partial/qualified.
- Eliminate choices that misrepresent either text. Run each choice through BOTH texts.
- Absolute words (always, never, entirely, all) are red flags.

### Process of Elimination Rules

- If an answer choice misrepresents what the text says, eliminate it.
- If an answer choice describes a different rhetorical function than what the sentence performs, eliminate it.
- If an answer choice has the wrong connotation (too positive, too negative, too extreme), eliminate it.
- If an answer choice describes content instead of function, eliminate it.
- If an answer choice is true of only ONE text, eliminate it (cross-text).
- If an answer choice is "also true" but not the MAIN point, eliminate it (purpose).

### 6.7 High-Utility Words Added for Deep Study (with Example Sentences)

These 36 additional words appear regularly in official Words in Context questions. For each, know the definition, one example sentence, and the trap that makes it hard.

**1. alleviate** - to make (pain or a problem) less severe.
*Example:* "The new drug alleviated the patients' symptoms within hours, though it did not cure the underlying disease."
**Trap:** alleviate reduces severity; it does NOT eliminate or cure. Answer choices that say "remove entirely" are too strong.

**2. bolster** - to support or strengthen.
*Example:* "The discovery of a second fossil site bolstered the team's claim that the species had migrated across the continent."
**Trap:** bolster = strengthen an argument or position. It is positive; it does NOT mean "challenge" or "weaken."

**3. capricious** - given to sudden, unpredictable changes of mood or behavior.
*Example:* "The region's capricious weather made long-range forecasting nearly impossible."
**Trap:** capricious = unpredictable; it is not the same as "harsh" or "severe."

**4. circumvent** - to find a way around (an obstacle or rule).
*Example:* "By registering the vessel in another country, the company circumvented local safety regulations."
**Trap:** circumvent = get around; it is not the same as "obey" or "enforce."

**5. contentious** - causing argument or controversy.
*Example:* "The use of standardized testing remains one of the most contentious issues in education policy."
**Trap:** contentious = debatable/controversial; it is not the same as "decisive" or "settled."

**6. cursory** - hasty and therefore not thorough.
*Example:* "A cursory glance at the data suggested no pattern, but a detailed analysis revealed a clear trend."
**Trap:** cursory is negative (superficial); it is NOT the same as "careful" or "quick but accurate."

**7. debunk** - to expose the falseness of (a belief or claim).
*Example:* "The researcher's replication study debunked the widely reported claim that the treatment boosted memory."
**Trap:** debunk = prove false; it is not the same as "question" or "qualify" (both weaker).

**8. deplete** - to use up the supply or resources of.
*Example:* "Decades of overfishing have depleted the cod population to a fraction of its historical size."
**Trap:** deplete = reduce toward exhaustion; it does not mean "eliminate completely" unless the context says so.

**9. deter** - to discourage (someone) from doing something.
*Example:* "The threat of steep fines did little to deter companies from dumping waste into the river."
**Trap:** deter = discourage; it is not the same as "prevent" (deterrence reduces attempts; prevention stops outcomes).

**10. discern** - to perceive or recognize something with effort.
*Example:* "Through the fog, the crew could barely discern the outline of the lighthouse."
**Trap:** discern = perceive with difficulty; it is not the same as "imagine" or "invent."

**11. disseminate** - to spread (information or ideas) widely.
*Example:* "The institute's goal was to disseminate the findings beyond academic journals to practicing teachers."
**Trap:** disseminate = spread widely; it is not the same as "collect" or "conceal."

**12. eclectic** - deriving ideas or style from a broad range of sources.
*Example:* "The chef's eclectic menu drew on Japanese, Italian, and Mexican techniques."
**Trap:** eclectic = mixed from many sources; it is not the same as "traditional" or "uniform."

**13. elicit** - to draw out (a response or reaction).
*Example:* "The comedian's remark elicited laughter from even the most serious members of the audience."
**Trap:** elicit = draw out; it is not the same as "suppress" or "ignore."

**14. ephemeral** - lasting for a very short time.
*Example:* "The flower's beauty is ephemeral, fading within a single day of blooming."
**Trap:** ephemeral = short-lived; it is not the same as "fragile" (though they often co-occur, fragility is about breakability, not duration).

**15. equitable** - fair and impartial.
*Example:* "The committee sought an equitable distribution of resources across the district's schools."
**Trap:** equitable = fair; it is not the same as "equal" (equal means identical amounts; equitable means fair amounts).

**16. eschew** - to deliberately avoid using or engaging with.
*Example:* "The minimalist architect eschewed ornament in favor of clean geometric forms."
**Trap:** eschew = deliberately avoid; it is not the same as "embrace" or "tolerate."

**17. exhaustive** - complete and thorough, considering every possibility.
*Example:* "After an exhaustive search of the archives, the historian found no record of the disputed treaty."
**Trap:** exhaustive = extremely thorough; it is NOT the same as "exhausting" (tiring), though the words are related.

**18. facilitate** - to make (an action or process) easier.
*Example:* "The new online portal facilitated the exchange of documents between the two agencies."
**Trap:** facilitate = make easier; it does not mean "perform" or "cause." The person facilitating is helping, not doing.

**19. fastidious** - very attentive to detail; hard to please.
*Example:* "A fastidious editor, she rejected any manuscript containing a single inconsistent date."
**Trap:** fastidious = meticulous (can be positive); it is not the same as "fussy" in a purely negative sense unless context says so.

**20. fortuitous** - happening by chance, especially in a lucky way.
*Example:* "The fortuitous arrival of a second research vessel allowed the team to continue after their equipment failed."
**Trap:** fortuitous = lucky accident; it is NOT the same as "fortunate" in the sense of deserved good outcomes, and it definitely does not mean "planned."

**21. futile** - incapable of producing any useful result.
*Example:* "Attempts to revive the dying language proved futile once the last fluent speakers passed away."
**Trap:** futile = hopeless/useless; it is not the same as "difficult" (difficult things can still succeed).

**22. hamper** - to hinder or impede.
*Example:* "Heavy rainfall hampered the rescue effort, slowing vehicles on the flooded roads."
**Trap:** hamper = hinder; it is not the same as "prevent" (hampering slows; preventing stops).

**23. imminent** - about to happen.
*Example:* "The evacuation order came as a storm of unprecedented size was imminent."
**Trap:** imminent = about to happen; it is not the same as "eminent" (distinguished) - a classic spelling/meaning confusion pair.

**24. inherent** - existing in something as a permanent, essential characteristic.
*Example:* "The researcher acknowledged the inherent limitations of studying behavior in a laboratory setting."
**Trap:** inherent = built-in/essential; it is not the same as "obvious" or "temporary."

**25. innocuous** - harmless or unlikely to offend.
*Example:* "The question seemed innocuous, but it triggered a surprisingly hostile response."
**Trap:** innocuous = harmless; it is not the same as "irrelevant" (harmless things can still matter).

**26. judicious** - having or showing good judgment.
*Example:* "A judicious use of the budget allowed the museum to acquire three major works in a single year."
**Trap:** judicious = wise/careful; it is not the same as "generous" (generous spending is not necessarily wise).

**27. meticulous** - showing great attention to detail; very careful and precise.
*Example:* "The conservator's meticulous restoration took four years and required a microscope."
**Trap:** meticulous = careful with details; it is not the same as "slow" (though it can be) or "obsessive" in a negative sense.

**28. mitigate** - to make (something bad) less severe, serious, or painful.
*Example:* "Planting trees along the riverbank helped mitigate the effects of seasonal flooding."
**Trap:** mitigate = reduce severity; it is NOT the same as "eliminate." If the passage says the problem continues but lessens, mitigate is correct; if it disappears, the word is too weak.

**29. obsolete** - no longer produced or used; out of date.
*Example:* "The invention of digital cameras made film-based photography largely obsolete for consumers."
**Trap:** obsolete = outdated/unused; it is not the same as "unpopular" (something can be popular and still outdated).

**30. pervasive** - spreading widely throughout an area or group.
*Example:* "The pervasive use of smartphones has changed how teenagers form friendships."
**Trap:** pervasive = widespread; it is not the same as "powerful" or "permanent."

**31. preclude** - to prevent from happening; make impossible.
*Example:* "The terms of the contract precluded the company from competing in the same market for five years."
**Trap:** preclude = make impossible; it is stronger than "discourage" or "deter."

**32. proficient** - competent or skilled in doing something.
*Example:* "Within a year, the student became proficient enough in Spanish to conduct interviews."
**Trap:** proficient = skilled; it is not the same as "fluent" (fluent implies near-native ease; proficient implies competence).

**33. prolific** - producing much output; highly productive.
*Example:* "A prolific writer, she published a novel nearly every year for three decades."
**Trap:** prolific = highly productive; it is not the same as "popular" (a prolific author may be little read).

**34. reticent** - not revealing one's thoughts or feelings readily.
*Example:* "Reticent about his wartime experiences, he rarely spoke of them even with close friends."
**Trap:** reticent = reserved/quiet; it is NOT the same as "reluctant" in the sense of refusing to act (though the two are often confused; reticence is about speech, reluctance about action).

**35. robust** - strong and healthy; vigorous (of data: reliable and substantial).
*Example:* "The findings were robust across multiple samples, suggesting they were not an artifact of one dataset."
**Trap:** robust (of evidence) = strong/reliable; it is not the same as "conclusive" (robust evidence can still leave questions open).

**36. viable** - capable of working successfully; feasible.
*Example:* "Solar power became a viable alternative to diesel once battery storage costs fell."
**Trap:** viable = workable; it is not the same as "ideal" (a viable option is merely feasible, not necessarily the best).
