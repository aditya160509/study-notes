# DIGITAL SAT — MASTER SYLLABUS
### Mapped 1:1 to College Board's *Assessment Framework for the Digital SAT Suite* (v3.01, Aug 2024) — the actual exhaustive spec, not the sampled Skills Insight document.

This replaces the earlier syllabus format. Structure follows the Framework's own hierarchy exactly:
**Section → Content Domain → Skill/Knowledge Testing Point (official name) → sub-points → worked explanation.** Where the Framework's own language is quoted, it's marked *(AF)*. Everything else is explanatory expansion.

# PART A — READING AND WRITING SECTION

Official construct: *"literacy achievement relative to core college and career readiness requirements... comprehension, rhetoric, and language use."*
54 questions (50 operational + 4 pretest), 2 modules, 32 min each, 64 min total. ~30% of questions are in-context (word count constrained, $\leq$ 50 words for most). Multiple-choice, four-option discrete questions — one passage per question.

Four content domains, each domain = one *claim* about what a student can do. Testing points are the officially named sub-skills within each domain — this is the exhaustive list, not a sample.

---

## DOMAIN A1: INFORMATION AND IDEAS [~26%, 12–14 questions]

**Claim (AF):** *"Students will use comprehension, analysis, and reasoning skills and knowledge as well as what is stated and implied in texts (including in any accompanying informational graphics) to locate, interpret, evaluate, and integrate information and ideas."*

### A1.1 Central Ideas and Details
**Official description:** *"Students will determine the central idea of a text and/or interpret the key details supporting that idea."*
- Two distinct sub-tasks bundled under one testing point: (a) identify the *central idea* (the thesis/main point), (b) interpret specific *details* that support it. Questions can test either independently.
- Method: read for what the passage is fundamentally arguing or demonstrating, not just what happens in it.
- Trap: answer choices that are true, passage-supported details but are either too narrow (a supporting detail dressed as the main idea) or too broad (an overreach beyond the text's actual claim).

### A1.2 Command of Evidence
**Official description:** *"Students will determine the evidence in a text that best supports a specified claim or point."* Two officially separate sub-testing-points:

**A1.2a — Textual:** *"Students will determine the textual evidence (e.g., a fact, detail, or example from a text) that best supports a specified claim or point."*
- Method: identify the *exact* claim in the question stem first, then find the choice that most directly and specifically supports THAT claim — not just any true detail from the passage.
- Trap: distractors are usually real details that support a related-but-different claim.

**A1.2b — Quantitative:** *"Students will determine the quantitative evidence (i.e., data from an informational graphic) that best supports a specified claim or point."*
- Method: read axis labels and units on the graph/table BEFORE reading answer choices.
- Trap: choices citing a real, correctly-read data point that doesn't actually support the specific claim asked about.

### A1.3 Inferences
**Official description:** *"Students will draw reasonable inferences based on explicit and/or implicit information and ideas in a text."*
- Method: treat as a mini logic proof — the correct inference is the only one that *must* follow from stated facts, not merely a plausible-sounding extension.
- Trap: "sounds smart" answers requiring outside knowledge or an unstated extra assumption not backed by the text.

### A1.4 Interpreting Uncertainty in Scientific Writing
Not a separate Framework testing point — this is a recurring pattern within A1.2–A1.3 on science passages. College Board regularly uses hedging language in scientific texts, and questions test whether students recognize the strength (or tentativeness) of a claim.
- Key hedging signal words to identify: *suggests, indicates, may, might, likely, appears, consistent with, implies, could, seems, possibly, presumably.*
- A claim hedged with *"suggests"* is weaker than one with *"demonstrates"* — the correct inference or evidence answer must match the claim's actual strength.
- Trap: answer choices that overstate certainty (replacing "suggests" with "proves," or "may" with "will"). The SAT frequently rewards the answer that preserves the author's original hedging.

**Note:** the Framework's Table A33 does NOT list "quantitative inference" or "graph-only" as a separate testing point beyond what's above — inference and evidence together cover both textual and graphic material.

## DOMAIN A2: CRAFT AND STRUCTURE [~28%, 13–15 questions — largest single domain]

**Claim (AF):** *"Students will use comprehension, vocabulary, analysis, synthesis, and reasoning skills and knowledge to use and determine the meaning of high-utility academic words and phrases in context, evaluate texts rhetorically, and make supportable connections between multiple topically related texts."*

### A2.1 Words in Context
**Official description:** *"Students will determine the meaning of a high-utility academic word or phrase in context or use such vocabulary in a contextually appropriate way."*
- Two directions tested: (a) determine meaning given context, (b) supply the correct word/phrase for a given context — same skill, different direction.
- "High-utility academic vocabulary" = tier-two words: common across many text types/subjects, rare in everyday speech (e.g., *ambivalent, mitigate, ubiquitous, corroborate, candid, pervasive*).
- **Denotation vs. connotation** — a recurring trap: answer choices may share a similar dictionary definition (denotation) but carry different attitudes (connotation). Example: *slender* (positive/neutral), *thin* (neutral), *skinny* (negative/critical). All three can mean "not thick," but the SAT expects the choice whose connotation matches the author's tone.
- Method: cover the choices, predict your own word from context clues, THEN match, checking both definition AND tone.
- Trap: near-synonym distractors that don't fit the exact tone/logic; negation traps (watch "although," "despite," "however" signaling the blank should contrast with a nearby idea).

### A2.2 Text Structure and Purpose
**Official description:** *"Students will analyze the structure of a text or determine the main rhetorical purpose of a text."*
- Two sub-skills bundled: (a) structural analysis (how is this text/sentence organized, what job does a piece of it do), (b) purpose (why did the author write this — inform, argue, illustrate, challenge).
- **Tone calibration** — a recurring sub-skill within Purpose questions. The SAT tests recognition of these rhetorical stances:
  - *Cautious / qualified* — limits claims with hedging ("may," "suggests," "under certain conditions")
  - *Skeptical / critical* — questions or challenges a prevailing view
  - *Objective / neutral* — presents facts without apparent bias
  - *Explanatory / expository* — clarifies a concept or process
  - *Persuasive / advocacy* — argues for a position or course of action
  - *Defensive / rebuttal* — responds to a criticism
- Distinguishing these is often the difference between two plausible-sounding purpose answers.
- Method for structure/sentence-level: ask "what job is this sentence doing for the argument" (introducing, qualifying, contrasting, exemplifying, concluding) rather than summarizing content.
- Method for purpose/passage-level: distinguish topic (what it's about) from purpose (why written).
- Trap: confusing "what a sentence says" with "what job it's doing"; choosing an answer describing content instead of purpose.

### A2.3 Cross-Text Connections
**Official description:** *"Students will draw reasonable connections between two texts on related topics."*
- Method: identify each author's specific claim separately first, then compare only on the exact point of overlap the question asks about.
- Agreement between authors can be:
  - *Complete* — both authors assert the same claim
  - *Partial* — they agree on some points but diverge on others (most common on the SAT)
  - *Qualified* — one author agrees but with conditions or reservations
  - *Indirect* — the agreement or disagreement is implied rather than stated outright
- Trap: assuming total agreement or total disagreement between two authors, when usually they align on some points and differ narrowly on one specific claim.

## DOMAIN A3: EXPRESSION OF IDEAS [~20%, 8–12 questions]

**Claim (AF):** *"Students will use revision skills and knowledge to improve the effectiveness of written expression in accordance with specified rhetorical goals."*

### A3.1 Rhetorical Synthesis
**Official description:** *"Students will strategically integrate information and ideas on a topic to form an effective sentence achieving a specified rhetorical aim."*
- Unique to the digital SAT (didn't exist in this form on paper test) — student is given bullet-point facts and a stated goal, must select the choice that best combines facts *in service of that goal*.
- Method: read the stated goal FIRST, treat bullets as raw ingredients; the correct answer combines facts specifically to hit that goal, not just accurately.
- Trap: multiple choices are all factually accurate; only one matches the specific goal stated in the prompt. Always re-read the goal last, right before selecting.

### A3.2 Transitions
**Official description:** *"Students will determine the most effective transition word or phrase to logically connect information and ideas in a text."*

Full transition taxonomy to know cold:

| Category | Function | Common Words |
|---|---|---|
| **Sequence** | Order in time or steps | *then, subsequently, first/second/third, next, afterward, later, previously, finally* |
| **Contrast** | Direct opposition | *however, conversely, in contrast, on the other hand, nevertheless, nonetheless, yet, whereas* |
| **Concession** | Admit a point before pivoting | *granted, admittedly, of course, certainly, to be sure, although, despite this* |
| **Cause-Effect** | One thing produces another | *therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, accordingly, because, since* |
| **Addition** | Extend or reinforce | *furthermore, moreover, in addition, additionally, also, besides, indeed* |
| **Emphasis** | Strengthen or clarify | *indeed, in fact, notably, particularly, specifically, above all* |
| **Example** | Illustrate a general point | *for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, namely* |
| **Result / Exception** | Logical outcome or deviation | *as a result, therefore, otherwise, instead, rather, alternatively* |

- Trap: two transitions both grammatically fine, but only one matches the exact logical relationship — contrast vs. concession is the most commonly confused pair (concession admits a point before pivoting; contrast just opposes). Cause-effect vs. sequence is another frequent trap.
- Method: delete the transition, read the two surrounding claims, identify the logical relationship with your own word, THEN match to the table.

## DOMAIN A4: STANDARD ENGLISH CONVENTIONS [~26%, 11–15 questions]

**Claim (AF):** *"Students will use editing skills and knowledge to make text conform to core conventions of Standard English sentence structure, usage, and punctuation."*

This is the domain your existing A4 manual covers. **The Framework names exactly two testing points, each with named sub-points — this is the full official taxonomy, nothing more, nothing less:**

### A4.1 BOUNDARIES
**Official description:** *"Students will edit text to ensure that sentences are conventionally complete."*

**A4.1a — Between Sentences:** *"Students will use contextually appropriate punctuation to properly mark the end of a sentence."*
- = period, semicolon (as period-equivalent), question mark — the boundary between two full sentences/independent clauses.
- Maps to your existing 1.1 (IC/DC cover test), 1.2 (period vs. semicolon elimination logic).

**A4.1b — Within Sentences:** *"Students will coordinate clauses within a sentence or elements in a series using appropriate punctuation and, in some cases, a conjunction or conjunctive adverb; incorporate supplementary information (e.g., appositives, parentheticals) using appropriate punctuation; and recognize circumstances in which no punctuation is needed to set off sentence elements."*
- This single official sub-point actually bundles THREE things your manual already separates out:
  1. Clause/list coordination with conjunctions or conjunctive adverbs → your 1.2, 1.3, 1.7
  2. Supplementary info via appositives/parentheticals → your 1.5 (essential/non-essential, matching pairs)
  3. **Recognizing when NO punctuation is needed** → this is explicitly named as its own skill, and it's the officially-confirmed sample-question type: *"the reason...falling is that..."* — no comma/colon/semicolon between subject and verb no matter how long the subject clause is. This is the exact "over-punctuation" trap flagged earlier. Also covers: no comma before a compound predicate that isn't a second independent clause (e.g., "She wrote the report and submitted it early" — no comma, since "submitted it early" isn't its own IC).

  **→ This is your one confirmed real gap: an explicit "when to use NO punctuation" sub-section, covering (i) never separate a long subject from its verb regardless of subject length/complexity, and (ii) never comma-split a compound predicate that shares one subject.**

Colons (A4.4 in your manual) and dashes-as-colons fall under "Within Sentences" too (elaboration/list-introduction), even though the Framework doesn't name "colon" separately — it's absorbed into the general "coordinate clauses...using appropriate punctuation."

### A4.2 FORM, STRUCTURE, AND SENSE
**Official description:** *"Students will edit text to conform to conventional usage."*

The Framework names **exactly six** sub-testing-points here. This is the complete official list — nothing else is separately named:

**A4.2a — Subject-Verb Agreement:** *"Students will ensure agreement in number between a subject and a verb."*
→ maps directly to your 2.1. Fully covered, including inserted-phrase traps, indefinite pronouns, inverted order, collective nouns.

**A4.2b — Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement:** *"Students will ensure agreement in number between a pronoun and its antecedent."*
→ maps to your 2.3. Covered, including singular "they" acceptance and ambiguous-referent traps.
- **Expanded coverage for this testing point:**
  - *Number agreement* — singular pronoun for singular antecedent, plural for plural (including indefinite pronouns: *everyone...his or her*)
  - *Reference clarity* — the antecedent must be unambiguous. If two possible antecedents exist (e.g., "Maria told Jenna that she won"), the sentence is ungrammatical under this testing point.
  - *Distance* — a pronoun far from its antecedent creates ambiguity; the closer the referent, the clearer the sentence.
  - *Dummy pronouns* — *it* and *there* used as expletives/subject placeholders ("It is raining," "There are three options") — not tested as agreement errors, but knowing they have no antecedent prevents false positives.
- **Minor gap:** the official phrasing is number-agreement specific — doesn't separately name "ambiguity" as its own tested point, though it's a real trap type within this category. Your treatment is fine; just note ambiguity-testing is a sub-case of this point, not a separate one.

**A4.2c — Verb Finiteness:** *"Students will use verbs and verbals (i.e., gerunds, participles, infinitives) in contextually appropriate ways."*
- **This is a genuine gap in your manual as a named category.** "Verb finiteness" = distinguishing when a sentence needs a truly finite (conjugated, subject-agreeing) verb versus when a non-finite verbal form (gerund "-ing" as noun, participle "-ing/-ed" as modifier, infinitive "to ___") is grammatically required instead.
- **The four verbal forms tested:**
  - *Finite verb* — conjugated for tense and person, can serve as the main verb of a clause: *runs, ran, is running, has broken, will leave.* Every independent clause needs exactly one.
  - *Gerund* — *-ing* form used as a noun: "Running is good exercise." Cannot serve as the main verb.
  - *Participle* — *-ing* (present) or *-ed/-en* (past) as adjective: "The broken vase sat on the shelf." A participle alone cannot power a clause.
  - *Infinitive* — *to* + base form, used as noun/adjective/adverb: "She wants to leave." Not a finite verb.
- **Decision framework:**
  1. Identify the subject of the clause containing the blank.
  2. Does that subject already have a finite verb? If YES, the blank needs a verbal (participle, gerund, or infinitive). If NO, the blank needs a finite verb.
  3. Check for commas around the blank — a phrase set off by commas between a subject and its existing verb is almost always a participial phrase (verbal), not a finite verb.
- Examples:
  - *"The report, having been reviewed twice, ___ approved."* → needs a finite verb ("was") because the clause has no other conjugated verb.
  - *"Her goal is ___ [to finish / finishing] the marathon."* → infinitive vs. gerund choice after certain verbs is directly tested.
  - *"The committee reviewing the proposal"* is a fragment — *reviewing* alone is not a finite verb. Needs "The committee is reviewing..."
- Example of the actual AF sample question style: choosing between "Rabinal Achí tells the story..." (finite verb, correct) vs. fragment/dangling alternatives — this was literally the Framework's own sample question, and it's filed under Form/Structure/Sense, not Boundaries, because the core issue is verbal form choice, not punctuation.
- Your manual's participial-phrase material (1.6) and modifier material (2.5) circles this concept but never names "finite vs. non-finite verb form choice" as its own decision point. **Recommend adding a short section: "when the sentence needs a real conjugated verb vs. when a gerund/participle/infinitive is correct," with the fragment-avoidance angle emphasized** (a sentence with only a participle and no finite verb anywhere is an incomplete sentence — this is actually a Boundaries-adjacent issue that the Framework files under FSS).

**A4.2d — Verb Tense and Aspect:** *"Students will use contextually appropriate tenses and aspects of verbs."*
→ maps to your 2.2. Well covered (tense consistency, past perfect sequencing, irregular participles). "Aspect" specifically = progressive/perfect/perfect-progressive distinctions (has done vs. was doing vs. had been doing) — your worked examples 3 and 4 already hit this implicitly; could be named more explicitly but substance is there.

**A4.2e — Subject-Modifier Placement:** *"Students will place modifying elements in sentences (e.g., participles) in contextually appropriate ways."*
→ maps to your 2.5 (dangling/misplaced modifiers). Fully covered, and this was literally the second official sample question in the Framework (Rabinal Achí example) — your worked examples 1–4 match this exactly.
- **Expanded modifier subtypes to teach:**
  - *Dangling modifier* — the subject being modified is missing from the main clause: "Walking to school, the rain started." (Who was walking? Not the rain.)
  - *Misplaced modifier* — placed next to the wrong noun: "She served cookies to the children on paper plates." (The children aren't on paper plates.)
  - *Squinting modifier* — could modify either what comes before or after: "Students who practice often improve." (Does "often" modify "practice" or "improve"?)
  - *Ambiguous modifier* — unclear which of two possible nouns it refers to.

**A4.2f — Genitives and Plurals:** *"Students will make contextually appropriate choices among singular, plural, singular possessive, and plural possessive nouns and pronouns and among possessive determiners (its, their, your), contractions (it's, they're, you're), and adverbs (there)."*
→ maps to your 2.4. Covered (it's/its, who's/whose, plural vs. possessive apostrophes). One small officially-named item your manual doesn't separately flag: **"there" as an adverb vs. "their"/"they're"** — a three-way homophone confusion (their/there/they're), not just the two-way pairs you listed. Easy one-line addition.

### WHAT A4.2 OFFICIALLY DOES **NOT** NAME AS A TESTING POINT

This is the important finding from the real spec, as opposed to guessing from Skills Insight's partial sample: your A4 manual contains substantial, well-built content on topics that **are not separately named anywhere in the Framework's exhaustive taxonomy.** These are:

- **Parallel structure** — not named as its own testing point. It may occasionally surface as an instance of "Verb Finiteness" (parallel verbals in a list) but isn't a standalone official category. *Nonetheless, it appears frequently enough in practice that it should be taught.* Covers parallel: verbs, nouns, phrases, clauses, and comparison structures.
  - Example: "She likes swimming, hiking, and **to bike**" → should be "**biking**" for parallelism.
  - Comparisons must be structurally parallel: "The weather in New York is colder than **Miami**" → should be "**that of Miami**" or "**in Miami**."
- **Adjective vs. adverb** (2.7) — not named. Still worth teaching: "She did **good**" → "She did **well**."
- **Comparatives and superlatives** (2.8) — not named. Still tested: *more...than, less...than, as...as, same...as, different from, rather than, between X and Y.*
- **Subject vs. object pronoun case / who vs. whom** (2.9) — not named. Note "Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement" is about number agreement only, not case.
- **Quotation punctuation** (1.8) — not explicitly named under Boundaries, though it could be read as a Within-Sentences punctuation instance.
- **Coordination/subordination logical fit** (2.10) — not named as SEC; this concept actually belongs more naturally under Transitions (A3.2) since it's about logical relationship, not grammar.

This doesn't mean these topics never appear on real tests — test-prep consensus and released practice tests do show occasional questions matching these patterns. But they are **not part of the official exhaustive taxonomy** the way Subject-Verb Agreement or Verb Finiteness are. Practically: keep this content (it's good, low-cost insurance), but understand it sits outside the core six-category spine when you're prioritizing study time. The six official FSS categories plus the two Boundaries categories are where guaranteed question density lives.

# PART B — MATH SECTION

**Construct (AF):** *"math achievement relative to core college and career readiness requirements."*
44 questions total (22 per module — 20 operational + 2 pretest), 2 modules, 35 min each (70 min total), calculator allowed throughout (built-in Desmos graphing calculator + approved handheld). ~30% of questions are in-context. Question format: ~75% multiple-choice (4 options), ~25% student-produced response (SPR).

Four content domains = four claims. Below: full official testing-point breakdown per domain (SAT column; PSAT variants differ slightly in whether higher-order interpretive items appear).

## DOMAIN B1: ALGEBRA [~35%, 13–15 questions]
**Claim (AF):** *"analyze, fluently solve, interpret, and create linear equations and inequalities as well as analyze and fluently solve systems of equations using multiple techniques."*

Five officially named testing points:

**B1.1 Linear equations in one variable** — create/use to solve problems in context; identify/create equation from context; interpret a constant/variable/factor/term/solution in context; solve making strategic use of algebraic structure; determine conditions for no/unique/infinite solutions; fluently solve.
- **Add: identity vs. conditional equation.** The SAT tests the distinction:
  - *Identity* — true for all values (e.g., $2(x+3) = 2x + 6$). Infinite solutions. The two sides are algebraically equivalent.
  - *Conditional equation* — true only for specific values (e.g., $2x + 3 = 7$). One solution.
  - *Contradiction* — true for no value (e.g., $2(x+3) = 2x + 7$). No solution. The two sides simplify to different constants.
- The "no solution / infinite solutions in ONE variable" case isn't separately worked in your manual — your condition-counting method (B1.4) is built for two-variable systems. The one-variable version needs its own treatment.

**B1.2 Linear functions** — create/use to solve problems; identify/create function modeling a relationship; interpret input/output pair or constant/term in context; interpret the graph in context; connect table/algebraic/graph representations (in and out of context); evaluate function for input or find input from output; write rule given two input-output pairs or one pair + rate of change.
→ your B1.2/B1.3 combined cover this well (slope-intercept, point-slope, context interpretation).

**B1.3 Linear equations in two variables** — same structure as above but for full two-variable equations (not just functions): create/use; identify/create to model a constraint; interpret solution/constant/term in context; interpret graph of $Ax+By=C$; connect representations; write equation given two points, one point + slope, or one point + parallel/perpendicular line.
→ your B1.2 and B1.6 cover slope forms and parallel/perpendicular; the $Ax+By=C$ standard-form interpretation angle is implicit but not explicitly drilled — minor addition worth making.

**B1.4 Systems of two linear equations in two variables** — create/use to solve problems in context; identify/create to model constraints; solve using algebraic structure; determine conditions for no/unique/infinite solutions; connect algebraic/graph representations in and out of context; fluently solve.
→ your B1.4. Fully covered including the slope-comparison shortcut.
- System types to explicitly name: linear + linear (core), and as a sub-pattern, systems that model real-world constraints with inequalities.

**B1.5 Linear inequalities in one or two variables** — create/use; interpret constant/solution; interpret a point in the xy-plane in terms of the solution set (solid/dashed boundary, shading direction, testing a point).
→ your B1.5 covers the sign-flip rule and basic graphing; **add the "interpret a point relative to an inequality's solution region" angle** — this is explicitly named in the AF and isn't drilled in your manual.

**Note:** The AF also tests solving linear inequalities fluently (including compound inequalities) and interpreting them in context as constraints.

## DOMAIN B2: ADVANCED MATH [~35%, 13–15 questions]
**Claim (AF):** *"...analyzing, fluently solving, interpreting, and creating equations, including absolute value, quadratic, exponential, polynomial, rational, radical, and other nonlinear equations, as well as analyzing and fluently solving systems of linear and nonlinear equations in two variables."*

The Framework names **three** official testing points. Your existing B2.1–B2.6 cover the substance well; the expansion below maps each official point to worked content:

**B2.1 Equivalent expressions** — rewrite algebraic expressions in equivalent forms: factoring, expanding, combining fractions, rewriting radicals as exponents, completing the square, polynomial long division.
→ Your factoring toolkit covers this. Add explicit naming: equivalent forms of quadratic (standard, vertex, factored), rational expression simplification, and radical-to-exponent conversion.

**B2.2 Nonlinear equations in one variable and systems of equations in two variables** — solve and create: quadratic, radical, rational, absolute value, exponential; extraneous-root checking; linear + nonlinear systems (linear + quadratic, linear + circle, linear + absolute value).
→ Your B2.2, B2.3, B2.4, B2.6 cover these. **Add explicit system-type naming:** linear + quadratic, linear + circle, linear + absolute value — each has a distinct solving approach.
- **Piecewise functions** — not their own chapter, but they appear. A function defined by different rules over different intervals. SAT tests evaluation at a given input and, occasionally, graph interpretation.

**B2.3 Nonlinear functions** — all function concepts applied to nonlinear relationships:
- **Domain and range** (especially restrictions from square roots, denominators, context)
- **Maximum and minimum** (vertex of parabola, graph identification)
- **Increasing / decreasing** intervals
- **End behavior** (what happens as $x \to \infty$ or $x \to -\infty$ for polynomials and exponentials)
- **Graph features** (intercepts, asymptotes for rational/exponential, symmetry)
- **Transformations** (shifts, reflections, stretches — applied to any function type)
- **Polynomial specifics:**
  - *Factor Theorem* — $f(a)=0$ iff $(x-a)$ is a factor
  - *Remainder Theorem* — $f(a)$ = remainder when $f(x)$ is divided by $(x-a)$
  - *Zero multiplicity* — a factor $(x-a)^k$ has multiplicity $k$; odd multiplicity crosses the x-axis, even multiplicity touches and turns
  - *Graph behavior near roots* — single root (straight through), double root (bounces), triple root (s-curve through)
- **Exponential function interpretation:**
  - *Growth factor* vs. *decay factor* ($b > 1$ vs. $0 < b < 1$ in $f(x)=a \cdot b^x$)
  - *Percent interpretation* — a growth factor of 1.08 = 8% increase per unit; a decay factor of 0.92 = 8% decrease per unit
  - *Doubling / halving* — $f(x)=a \cdot 2^{x/d}$ doubles every $d$ units; $f(x)=a \cdot (1/2)^{x/h}$ halves every $h$ units

Your existing worked methods for factoring, quadratic formula/discriminant, exponential/radical/rational/absolute-value equations with extraneous-root checking, systems, graph features/transformations, solving for a variable of interest — all align. No major named gap from the AF's sub-bullets; the additions above are specificity improvements, not new domain coverage.

## DOMAIN B3: PROBLEM-SOLVING AND DATA ANALYSIS [~15%, 5–7 questions]
**Claim (AF):** *"apply quantitative reasoning about ratios, rates, and proportional relationships; understand and apply unit rate; and analyze and interpret one- and two-variable data."*

**B3.1 Ratios, rates, proportional relationships, and units** — your existing coverage plus:
- **Unit analysis / dimensional analysis** — SAT increasingly hides math inside compound units (miles per hour, dollars per square foot), unit conversion (feet→inches, hours→minutes), square/cubic units (area/volume unit conversions). Many mistakes are unit mistakes, not math mistakes.
- **Density** — mass/volume, population/area — appears as a rates sub-case.

**B3.2 Percentages** — your coverage is solid. Add: percent increase/decrease, repeated percent change, error margin percentage.

**B3.3 One-variable data: distributions and measures of center and spread**
- Mean, median, mode, range, IQR, standard deviation (interpretation: which data set has a larger standard deviation? No calculation required).
- Shape of distribution (symmetric, skewed left, skewed right), effect of outliers on mean vs. median.

**B3.4 Two-variable data: models and scatterplots** — your coverage is solid.

**B3.5 Probability and conditional probability**
- Organize by type: **independent** events, **dependent** events, **conditional** probability, **complements**, **mutually exclusive** events.
- Two-way tables: calculating conditional probability $P(A|B)$ from row/column totals.
- "Given that" phrasing — the condition is always the denominator.

**B3.6 Inference from sample statistics and margin of error** — your coverage plus:
- **Sampling bias** — a non-representative sample invalidates generalization to the full population.
- **Random sampling** → enables generalization to the population.
- **Random assignment** → enables causal claims (treatment vs. control).
- **Correlation vs. causation** — a correlation does NOT imply causation without random assignment.
- **Experimental design** — distinguish observational studies from experiments; identify confounding variables.

**B3.7 Evaluating statistical claims: observational studies and experiments** — your coverage is solid.

## DOMAIN B4: GEOMETRY AND TRIGONOMETRY [~15%, 5–7 questions]
**Claim (AF):** *"solve problems that focus on perimeter, area, and volume; angles, triangles, and trigonometry; and circles."*

**B4.1 Area and perimeter** — your coverage is solid. Add coordinate-geometry connections: area of a polygon given vertices, distance between points.

**B4.2 Volume and surface area** — your coverage is solid. Note: SAT primarily tests volume; surface area is less common.

**B4.3 Lines, angles, and triangles** — your coverage plus:
- **Coordinate geometry** (explicitly tested though cross-cutting):
  - *Distance formula* — $d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$
  - *Midpoint formula* — $(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2})$
  - *Slope* as rate of change in context
  - *Parallel lines* (same slope), *perpendicular lines* (negative reciprocal slopes)
- **Transformations:**
  - *Rotation* (90°, 180°, 270° about the origin)
  - *Reflection* (over x-axis, y-axis, $y=x$)
  - *Translation* (horizontal/vertical shift)
  - *Dilation* (scaling about a point)

**B4.4 Right triangles and trigonometry** — your coverage plus:
- **SOHCAHTOA** — sine, cosine, tangent as ratios of sides
- **Inverse trig** — using $\sin^{-1}$, $\cos^{-1}$, $\tan^{-1}$ to find angles
- **Angle of elevation / angle of depression** — standard application setups
- **Special triangles:** 30-60-90 ($1:\sqrt{3}:2$), 45-45-90 ($1:1:\sqrt{2}$) — both are explicitly testable without a calculator

**B4.5 Circles** — your coverage plus:
- **Equation of a circle in the coordinate plane:** $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$
- Completing the square to find center and radius from expanded form $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$

# PART C — DIGITAL SAT STRATEGY APPENDIX

Not syllabus content — practical test-taking skills specific to the digital format. These don't appear in the Framework but directly affect score outcomes.

### C.1 Bluebook App Tools
- **Answer eliminator** — cross out wrong choices visually (reduces cognitive load on hard questions).
- **Mark for review** — flag questions to return to within the same module. Use strategically: if stuck >60s, mark and move on.
- **Annotation / highlighting** — highlight key clauses in R&W passages. Reduces re-reading time.
- **Timer** — visible per-module countdown. No time carries between modules.

### C.2 Desmos Graphing Calculator Strategy
The built-in Desmos calculator is available for all 44 math questions. Strategic use:
- **Graphing** — solve systems by finding intersection points visually; find roots/zeros of quadratics and polynomials.
- **Regression** — type a table of points and get the best-fit line or curve ($y_1 \sim mx_1 + b$).
- **Checking** — verify algebraic work by comparing graphs of original and transformed equations.
- **When NOT to use Desmos** — simple arithmetic, single-step algebra, or questions where Desmos would take longer than mental math. Over-reliance slows students down.

### C.3 Adaptive Module Strategy
- **Module 1 is the gateway.** Performance determines whether Module 2 is harder (access to full score range) or easier (score ceiling ~590–650 on R&W, ~590–650 on Math).
- **Estimated routing thresholds** (based on analysis of released tests): ~18–19/27 correct on R&W M1, ~14–16/22 on Math M1 → harder Module 2.
- **Accuracy over speed.** Careless errors on Module 1 are more damaging than on the paper SAT because they lock you out of the higher-scoring path.
- **Score ceiling:** students routed to the easier Module 2 cannot achieve top scores regardless of perfect performance on that module. The harder Module 2 is the only path to the 700–800 range.
- **No backward navigation across modules.** Once you submit Module 1, you cannot return. Budget time per module carefully.

### C.4 Reference Sheet
- A formula reference sheet is available during the Math section (area, volume, Pythagorean theorem, special right triangles, circle formulas).
- In v3.01 (Aug 2024), the reference sheet layout was updated for readability. Know what's on it so you don't waste time deriving formulas that are already provided.
