## 1. Theory

### 1.1 The nuclear model of the atom

The modern model of the atom has three key features:

- A small, dense, **positively charged nucleus** at the centre, containing **protons** and **neutrons** (together called **nucleons**).
- **Electrons** occupy the space around the nucleus in a cloud of energy levels; they are much less massive than nucleons.
- Most of the atom is **empty space**: the nucleus has a radius of about $10^{-15}~\text{m}$ to $10^{-14}~\text{m}$, while the atom has a radius of about $10^{-10}~\text{m}$.

**Sizes and density:**

- Proton radius $\approx 0.8 \times 10^{-15}~\text{m}$.
- Nuclear radius $\approx 10^{-15}$ to $10^{-14}~\text{m}$.
- Atomic radius $\approx 10^{-10}~\text{m}$.
- Nuclear density $\approx 10^{17}$ to $10^{18}~\text{kg m}^{-3}$, roughly $10^{14}$ times the density of water.

**Why the nucleus does not collapse:** the electrostatic repulsion between protons is overcome by the **strong nuclear force**, which is attractive between nucleons at very short range.

**Worked example:** A gold nucleus has a radius of $7.0 \times 10^{-15}~\text{m}$ and a gold atom has a radius of $1.4 \times 10^{-10}~\text{m}$. Estimate the ratio of the volume of the atom to the volume of the nucleus.

1. Volume $\propto r^3$, so the ratio is $\left(\frac{1.4 \times 10^{-10}}{7.0 \times 10^{-15}}\right)^3$.
2. The radius ratio is $2.0 \times 10^4$.
3. Volume ratio $= (2.0 \times 10^4)^3 = 8.0 \times 10^{12}$.

**Answer:** about $8 \times 10^{12}$; the atom is mostly empty space.

### 1.2 Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment

**Apparatus:** a source of alpha-particles in a lead container with a narrow aperture, a very thin gold foil (about $10^{-6}~\text{m}$ thick), and a movable detector (a scintillation screen with a microscope, or a GM tube). The apparatus was evacuated so that alpha-particles were not absorbed by air.

**Observations:**

- Most alpha-particles passed straight through the foil with little or no deflection.
- Some were deflected through small angles.
- About 1 in 20 000 were deflected through more than $90^\circ$, some almost straight back.

**Conclusions:**

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most alpha-particles pass straight through | Most of the atom is **empty space** |
| A few are deflected through large angles | There is a concentrated region of **positive charge** and **mass** — the nucleus |
| Very few are back-scattered | The nucleus is **tiny** compared with the atom, and contains most of the mass |
| Alpha-particles are repelled, not attracted | The nucleus is **positively charged** |

**Explanation:** an alpha-particle is positive, so it experiences a **repulsive electrostatic force** from a positive nucleus. The closer it passes to the nucleus, the stronger the force and the greater the deflection. A head-on approach produces a deflection of almost $180^\circ$. The nucleus is much more massive than the alpha-particle, so it barely moves; the alpha-particle does most of the deflecting.

**Why the plum-pudding model failed:** if positive charge were spread throughout the atom, the electric field would be too weak and diffuse to produce large deflections. Alpha-particles would pass through with only tiny deflections, and back-scattering would not occur.

**Worked example:** Explain why a thicker gold foil would increase the fraction of alpha-particles that are back-scattered.

1. A thicker foil contains more layers of nuclei.
2. An alpha-particle is more likely to pass close to a nucleus.
3. A close approach produces a large repulsive force and therefore a large deflection.

**Answer:** greater chance of a close encounter with a nucleus, so more large-angle scattering.

### 1.3 Nuclide notation

A nuclide is represented as:

$${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$$

where:

- $A$ = **nucleon number** (mass number) = number of protons + number of neutrons.
- $Z$ = **proton number** (atomic number) = number of protons.
- $N$ = **neutron number** = $A - Z$.
- X = chemical symbol of the element.

For a **neutral atom**, the number of electrons equals $Z$. An **ion** has gained or lost electrons, so its electron number is not equal to $Z$.

**Examples:**

- ${}^{1}_{1}\text{H}$: 1 proton, 0 neutrons, 1 electron.
- ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$: 2 protons, 2 neutrons, 2 electrons (an alpha-particle is the nucleus, without electrons).
- ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$: 6 protons, 8 neutrons, 6 electrons.
- ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$: 92 protons, 146 neutrons, 92 electrons.

**Worked example:** Determine the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of ${}^{210}_{82}\text{Pb}$.

1. Protons $= Z = 82$.
2. Neutrons $= A - Z = 210 - 82 = 128$.
3. Electrons $= Z = 82$ (neutral atom).

**Answer:** 82 protons, 128 neutrons, 82 electrons.

### 1.4 Isotopes

**Isotopes** are nuclei of the **same element** with the **same number of protons** but **different numbers of neutrons**.

- They have the same $Z$ but different $A$.
- They have the same chemical properties, because chemistry depends on the electron arrangement (and hence on $Z$).
- They have different nuclear properties (stability, type of decay, mass).
- They may have slightly different physical properties such as density and boiling point.

**Examples:**

- Hydrogen: ${}^{1}_{1}\text{H}$ (protium), ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H}$ (deuterium), ${}^{3}_{1}\text{H}$ (tritium).
- Carbon: ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ and ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$.
- Uranium: ${}^{235}_{92}\text{U}$ and ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$.

**Worked example:** Two nuclei are ${}^{235}_{92}\text{U}$ and ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$. State one similarity and two differences.

1. Similarity: same number of protons (92), so both are uranium.
2. Difference: different numbers of neutrons (143 and 146).
3. Difference: different nucleon numbers (235 and 238) and different nuclear stability.

**Answer:** same $Z$, different $N$ and $A$.

### 1.5 Forces in the nucleus

Two forces are important inside the nucleus:

| Force | Nature | Range | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strong nuclear force** | Attractive between all nucleons (proton–proton, neutron–neutron, proton–neutron) | Very short, about $10^{-15}~\text{m}$ | Holds the nucleus together; overcomes proton–proton repulsion |
| **Electrostatic (Coulomb) force** | Repulsive between protons | Long range, acts throughout the nucleus | Tries to push the nucleus apart |

The strong force is **attractive** at separations of about $10^{-15}~\text{m}$, but becomes **repulsive** at even smaller separations, preventing nucleons from collapsing into one point. It is **charge-independent**: it acts equally between any pair of nucleons.

**Why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons:** the strong force has a short range, so each nucleon only attracts its nearest neighbours, while the electrostatic repulsion acts on every proton in the nucleus. In large nuclei, extra neutrons increase the number of strong-force attractions without adding electrostatic repulsion, helping to stabilise the nucleus. All nuclei with $Z > 83$ are unstable and radioactive.

**Worked example:** Explain why the nucleus of ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$ contains more neutrons than protons.

1. There are 92 protons, all repelling each other electrostatically.
2. The strong force is short-range, so each nucleon only attracts nearby nucleons.
3. Extra neutrons provide additional strong-force attraction without adding repulsion, increasing stability.

**Answer:** neutrons help to hold the nucleus together against proton repulsion.

### 1.6 Fundamental particles: hadrons and leptons

Subatomic particles are divided into two families:

- **Hadrons:** particles affected by the strong nuclear force. Examples: protons, neutrons, pions, kaons. Hadrons are **not** fundamental; they are made of quarks.
  - **Baryons:** hadrons made of **three quarks**. Examples: proton (uud), neutron (udd).
  - **Mesons:** hadrons made of a **quark–antiquark pair**. Example: $\pi^+$ = (u$\bar{\text{d}}$).
- **Leptons:** particles **not** affected by the strong nuclear force. Examples: electron, positron, neutrino, antineutrino, muon. Leptons are fundamental (as far as is known).

**Quarks:** the up (u), down (d) and strange (s) quarks have fractional charges:

| Quark | Charge |
|---|---|
| up (u) | $+\frac{2}{3}e$ |
| down (d) | $-\frac{1}{3}e$ |
| strange (s) | $-\frac{1}{3}e$ |
| antiquark ($\bar{\text{u}}$, $\bar{\text{d}}$, $\bar{\text{s}}$) | opposite sign |

**Proton** = uud: charge $= +\frac{2}{3} + \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = +1e$.

**Neutron** = udd: charge $= +\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = 0$.

**Worked example:** Show that the quark composition of a neutron gives zero charge.

1. Neutron = udd.
2. Charge $= +\frac{2}{3}e + (-\frac{1}{3}e) + (-\frac{1}{3}e)$.
3. Charge $= 0$.

**Answer:** udd gives zero net charge.

### 1.7 The three types of ionising radiation

Radioactive decay is the **spontaneous and random** emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation from an unstable nucleus.

| Property | Alpha ($\alpha$) | Beta-minus ($\beta^-$) | Beta-plus ($\beta^+$) | Gamma ($\gamma$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ | Electron ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ | Positron ${}^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$ | Electromagnetic photon |
| Charge | $+2e$ | $-e$ | $+e$ | 0 |
| Relative mass | 4 | $\frac{1}{1840}$ | $\frac{1}{1840}$ | 0 |
| Speed | $\approx 0.05c$ ($\sim 10^7~\text{m s}^{-1}$) | Up to $\approx 0.99c$ | Up to $\approx 0.99c$ | $c = 3.0 \times 10^8~\text{m s}^{-1}$ |
| Ionising power | Strongest | Moderate | Moderate | Weakest |
| Penetrating power | Least: stopped by paper or a few cm of air | More: stopped by a few mm of aluminium | Similar to $\beta^-$, but annihilates quickly | Most: reduced by cm of lead, never completely stopped |
| Deflection in electric field | Toward negative plate | Toward positive plate | Toward negative plate | None |
| Deflection in magnetic field | One direction | Opposite direction | Same direction as $\alpha$ | None |

**Alpha decay:** the nucleus emits an alpha-particle (2 protons + 2 neutrons). $A$ decreases by 4 and $Z$ decreases by 2.

**Beta-minus decay:** a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. $A$ is unchanged and $Z$ increases by 1.

**Beta-plus decay:** a proton changes into a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino. $A$ is unchanged and $Z$ decreases by 1.

**Gamma emission:** an excited nucleus loses energy as a gamma photon. $A$ and $Z$ are unchanged.

**Why neutrinos are needed:** beta-particles are emitted with a **range of energies**, not a single value. If only the electron were emitted, energy and momentum would not be conserved. A nearly massless, uncharged particle — the neutrino or antineutrino — carries away the missing energy and momentum.

**Worked example:** Compare the ionising and penetrating powers of alpha and gamma radiation.

1. Alpha is highly ionising because it is charged, massive and slow, so it interacts strongly with atoms and creates many ion pairs over a short distance.
2. Alpha is therefore stopped quickly (paper or a few cm of air), so it is least penetrating.
3. Gamma is uncharged and travels at the speed of light, so it interacts weakly and creates few ion pairs.
4. Gamma is therefore the most penetrating, requiring several cm of lead to reduce its intensity significantly.

**Answer:** alpha: most ionising, least penetrating; gamma: least ionising, most penetrating.

### 1.8 Decay equations and conservation laws

In every nuclear decay:

- **Nucleon number $A$ is conserved.**
- **Proton number $Z$ (and hence charge) is conserved.**
- **Mass–energy is conserved** (mass may be converted to kinetic energy; see Chapter 31).

**Alpha decay:**

$${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\text{Y} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

**Beta-minus decay:**

$${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z+1}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}_e$$

**Beta-plus decay:**

$${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z-1}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + \nu_e$$

**Gamma emission:**

$${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}^{*} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} + \gamma$$

**Worked example:** Radon-222 (${}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn}$) decays by alpha emission. Write the decay equation.

1. Alpha emission: $A$ decreases by 4, $Z$ decreases by 2.
2. New $A = 222 - 4 = 218$; new $Z = 86 - 2 = 84$.
3. Element with $Z = 84$ is polonium (Po).

**Answer:** ${}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow {}^{218}_{84}\text{Po} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$.

**Worked example:** Carbon-14 (${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$) decays by beta-minus emission. Write the equation.

1. $A$ stays 14; $Z$ increases by 1 to 7.
2. Element with $Z = 7$ is nitrogen (N).
3. Emit an electron and an antineutrino.

**Answer:** ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}_e$.

**Worked example:** A nucleus ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ emits an alpha-particle followed by two beta-minus particles. Determine the final $A$ and $Z$.

1. Alpha: $A \rightarrow A-4$, $Z \rightarrow Z-2$.
2. First beta-minus: $Z \rightarrow Z-1$.
3. Second beta-minus: $Z \rightarrow Z$.

**Answer:** final $A = A-4$, final $Z = Z$; the element changes, but the proton number returns to its original value.

### 1.9 Behaviour in electric and magnetic fields

**Electric field:** the force on a charged particle is along the field (for positive charges) or opposite to it (for negative charges).

- Alpha ($+2e$): deflected toward the **negative** plate; small deflection because of its large mass.
- Beta-minus ($-e$): deflected toward the **positive** plate; much larger deflection because its mass is about $1/7000$ of an alpha-particle.
- Beta-plus ($+e$): deflected toward the **negative** plate, in the same direction as alpha, but with a large deflection.
- Gamma (uncharged): **undeflected**.

**Magnetic field:** the force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, so the path curves. Use **Fleming's left-hand rule** for a positive charge (current direction = velocity direction); for a negative charge, reverse the force direction.

- Alpha and beta-plus curve one way; beta-minus curves the opposite way.
- Gamma is undeflected.
- The radius of curvature is $r = \frac{mv}{qB}$: for the same speed, the lighter beta-particle curves much more than the alpha-particle.

**Worked example:** A mixture of alpha, beta-minus and gamma radiation passes through a uniform electric field between two plates. Describe the path of each.

1. Alpha: deflected slightly toward the negative plate.
2. Beta-minus: deflected strongly toward the positive plate.
3. Gamma: straight line, undeflected.

**Answer:** as above; the beta-minus deflection is largest because its mass is smallest.

### 1.10 Penetration, absorption and range

**Ionisation** is the removal of electrons from atoms, producing positive ions and free electrons. The radiation loses kinetic energy with each ionisation, so it slows down and eventually stops.

- **Alpha:** very dense ionisation; range only a few cm in air; stopped by paper or skin. Inside the body, however, alpha emitters are dangerous because all their energy is deposited in a small volume.
- **Beta:** moderate ionisation; range of a few mm in aluminium; can penetrate skin but is stopped by a few mm of metal or several mm of perspex.
- **Gamma:** weak ionisation; intensity decreases exponentially with thickness; never completely absorbed, but several cm of lead or metres of concrete reduce it to a safe level.

**Inverse relationship:** the more strongly ionising the radiation, the less penetrating it is, because it loses energy quickly.

**Background radiation:** even without a source, a detector records a small count rate from cosmic rays, rocks, radon gas and medical/industrial sources. This must be subtracted from measurements (see Chapter 31 for practical work).

**Worked example:** Explain why alpha radiation is more dangerous inside the body than outside.

1. Outside, alpha is stopped by the dead layer of skin, so it does not reach living cells.
2. Inside, the alpha-particle is in direct contact with living tissue.
3. It is highly ionising, so it deposits a large amount of energy in a small volume, causing significant cell damage.

**Answer:** high ionising power and short range mean concentrated energy deposition in tissue.

### 1.11 The electronvolt

The **electronvolt (eV)** is the energy transferred when an electron moves through a potential difference of $1~\text{V}$.

$$1~\text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}~\text{J}$$

Useful conversions:

$$1~\text{keV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-16}~\text{J}, \qquad 1~\text{MeV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-13}~\text{J}$$

**Worked example:** A gamma photon has an energy of $2.5~\text{MeV}$. Express this in joules.

1. $2.5~\text{MeV} = 2.5 \times 10^6~\text{eV}$.
2. $E = 2.5 \times 10^6 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$.
3. $E = 4.0 \times 10^{-13}~\text{J}$.

**Answer:** $4.0 \times 10^{-13}~\text{J}$.

### 1.12 The weak interaction and beta decay

Beta decay is caused by the **weak interaction** (weak nuclear force), not the strong force. At the quark level:

**Beta-minus:** one down quark changes into an up quark:

$$\text{d} \rightarrow \text{u} + \text{e}^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

Since a neutron is udd and a proton is uud, this changes a neutron into a proton.

**Beta-plus:** one up quark changes into a down quark:

$$\text{u} \rightarrow \text{d} + \text{e}^+ + \nu_e$$

Since a proton is uud and a neutron is udd, this changes a proton into a neutron.

**Worked example:** State the quark change in beta-minus decay and the force responsible.

1. A down quark changes to an up quark.
2. The weak interaction is responsible.

**Answer:** d → u; weak interaction.

## 2. Definition Sheet

> **Nucleon:** a proton or a neutron in a nucleus.

> **Nucleon number ($A$):** the total number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus.

> **Proton number ($Z$):** the number of protons in a nucleus; it determines the element.

> **Neutron number ($N$):** the number of neutrons in a nucleus, $N = A - Z$.

> **Nuclide:** a particular combination of protons and neutrons in a nucleus, specified by $A$ and $Z$.

> **Isotopes:** nuclei of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

> **Ion:** an atom or molecule that has gained or lost one or more electrons, so it has a net charge.

> **Alpha-particle:** a helium-4 nucleus, consisting of two protons and two neutrons.

> **Beta-minus particle:** an electron emitted from a nucleus when a neutron changes into a proton.

> **Beta-plus particle (positron):** the antiparticle of the electron, emitted when a proton changes into a neutron.

> **Gamma radiation:** high-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted by an excited nucleus as it loses energy.

> **Neutrino:** a nearly massless, uncharged lepton emitted in beta-plus decay (electron neutrino).

> **Antineutrino:** the antiparticle of the neutrino, emitted in beta-minus decay.

> **Hadron:** a particle that experiences the strong nuclear force; made of quarks.

> **Lepton:** a fundamental particle that does not experience the strong nuclear force (e.g. electron, neutrino).

> **Quark:** a fundamental particle that makes up hadrons; the up and down quarks make protons and neutrons.

> **Strong nuclear force:** the short-range attractive force between nucleons that holds the nucleus together.

> **Weak interaction:** the force responsible for beta decay, acting on quarks and leptons.

> **Ionisation:** the removal of electrons from atoms by radiation, producing ion pairs.

> **Electronvolt (eV):** the energy gained or lost by an electron when it moves through a potential difference of $1~\text{V}$; $1~\text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}~\text{J}$.

## 4. Methods (Question Types & Methods)

### 1. Count protons, neutrons and electrons

**Method:**
1. Protons $= Z$ (lower number).
2. Neutrons $= A - Z$.
3. Electrons $= Z$ for a neutral atom; adjust for the charge if it is an ion.
4. Check: $A = Z + N$.

**Traps:** reading $A$ and $Z$ the wrong way round; forgetting that an alpha-particle has no electrons; giving the electron number as $A$.

### 2. Identify isotopes

**Method:**
1. Compare $Z$: same $Z$ means the same element.
2. Compare $A$: different $A$ means different isotopes.
3. Check that $N = A - Z$ differs.

**Traps:** saying "same number of nucleons" (that would be the same nuclide, not isotopes); confusing isotopes with ions.

### 3. Complete a decay equation

**Method:**
1. Write the equation with the unknown nuclide/particle.
2. Balance $A$ on both sides.
3. Balance $Z$ on both sides.
4. Identify the element from $Z$ using the periodic table.
5. Add the neutrino/antineutrino for beta decay.

**Traps:** forgetting the antineutrino in $\beta^-$; using a proton instead of a neutron in $\beta^+$; changing $A$ in beta decay (it does not change); losing charge conservation.

### 4. Determine the type of decay from $A$ and $Z$ changes

**Method:**
1. $\Delta A = -4$, $\Delta Z = -2$: alpha.
2. $\Delta A = 0$, $\Delta Z = +1$: beta-minus.
3. $\Delta A = 0$, $\Delta Z = -1$: beta-plus.
4. $\Delta A = 0$, $\Delta Z = 0$: gamma.

**Traps:** beta-plus and electron capture both decrease $Z$; if a neutrino is shown, it is beta-plus.

### 5. Explain Rutherford scattering

**Method:**
1. Most pass straight through → mostly empty space.
2. Some deflected → positive charge concentrated somewhere.
3. Few back-scattered → tiny, massive, positive nucleus.
4. Use the words "repulsive electrostatic force" and "closer approach → greater force".

**Traps:** saying the nucleus attracts alpha-particles (it repels them); saying electrons cause the large deflections (they are too light).

### 6. Compare ionising radiations

**Method:**
1. State charge and mass.
2. Link charge/mass/speed to ionising power.
3. Link ionising power to range/penetration (inverse relationship).
4. State the absorber needed.

**Traps:** saying gamma is "not ionising" (it is, but weakly); saying alpha has the greatest range; confusing penetration with ionising power.

### 7. Quark calculations

**Method:**
1. Write the quark composition (proton uud, neutron udd).
2. Add the charges in units of $e$.
3. Check the total equals the known charge.
4. For beta decay, identify which quark changes: d → u ($\beta^-$) or u → d ($\beta^+$).

**Traps:** using $+1/3$ for down; forgetting that antiquarks have opposite charge; thinking a beta-particle is a quark (it is a lepton).

### 8. Convert eV and MeV to joules

**Method:**
1. Convert MeV to eV by multiplying by $10^6$.
2. Multiply by $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$.
3. Check the power of ten: nuclear energies are typically $10^{-13}$ to $10^{-12}~\text{J}$.

**Traps:** using $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ as the MeV-to-J conversion directly; forgetting the $10^6$.

## 5. Solved Examples

### Example 1: Nuclide composition

**Question:** Determine the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in ${}^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$.

**Method:**
1. Protons $= 26$.
2. Neutrons $= 56 - 26 = 30$.
3. Electrons $= 26$ (neutral atom).

**Answer:** 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 26 electrons.

### Example 2: Isotope identification

**Question:** Which of ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$, ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ and ${}^{14}_{7}\text{N}$ are isotopes of each other?

**Method:**
1. Isotopes have the same $Z$.
2. ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ and ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ both have $Z = 6$.
3. ${}^{14}_{7}\text{N}$ has $Z = 7$, so it is a different element.

**Answer:** the two carbon nuclides are isotopes.

### Example 3: Alpha decay

**Question:** ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$ decays by alpha emission. Write the equation.

**Method:**
1. $A: 226 - 4 = 222$.
2. $Z: 88 - 2 = 86$.
3. $Z = 86$ is radon (Rn).

**Answer:** ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$.

### Example 4: Beta-minus decay

**Question:** ${}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr}$ decays by beta-minus emission. Write the equation.

**Method:**
1. $A$ stays 90.
2. $Z: 38 + 1 = 39$.
3. $Z = 39$ is yttrium (Y).
4. Emit an electron and an antineutrino.

**Answer:** ${}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr} \rightarrow {}^{90}_{39}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}_e$.

### Example 5: Beta-plus decay

**Question:** ${}^{23}_{12}\text{Mg}$ decays by beta-plus emission. Write the equation.

**Method:**
1. $A$ stays 23.
2. $Z: 12 - 1 = 11$.
3. $Z = 11$ is sodium (Na).
4. Emit a positron and a neutrino.

**Answer:** ${}^{23}_{12}\text{Mg} \rightarrow {}^{23}_{11}\text{Na} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + \nu_e$.

### Example 6: Missing particle

**Question:** Complete ${}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{1}_{1}\text{p} + \text{?} + \bar{\nu}_e$.

**Method:**
1. $A: 1 = 1 + A + 0$, so $A = 0$.
2. $Z: 0 = 1 + Z + 0$, so $Z = -1$.
3. The particle with $A = 0$, $Z = -1$ is an electron.

**Answer:** ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ (a beta-minus particle).

### Example 7: Quark charge

**Question:** Show that a proton has charge $+e$ using the quark model.

**Method:**
1. Proton = uud.
2. Charge $= +\frac{2}{3}e + \frac{2}{3}e - \frac{1}{3}e$.
3. Charge $= +1e$.

**Answer:** uud gives $+e$.

### Example 8: Quark change

**Question:** State the quark change in beta-plus decay.

**Method:**
1. Beta-plus: proton → neutron.
2. Proton is uud, neutron is udd.
3. One up quark becomes a down quark.

**Answer:** u → d, with emission of a positron and a neutrino.

### Example 9: Energy conversion

**Question:** A beta-particle has kinetic energy $1.2~\text{MeV}$. Calculate its energy in joules.

**Method:**
1. $1.2~\text{MeV} = 1.2 \times 10^6~\text{eV}$.
2. $E = 1.2 \times 10^6 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$.
3. $E = 1.92 \times 10^{-13}~\text{J}$.

**Answer:** $1.9 \times 10^{-13}~\text{J}$.

### Example 10: Alpha then beta

**Question:** ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$ emits an alpha-particle, then a beta-minus particle. Determine the final $A$ and $Z$.

**Method:**
1. Alpha: $A = 238 - 4 = 234$; $Z = 92 - 2 = 90$.
2. Beta-minus: $A = 234$; $Z = 90 + 1 = 91$.

**Answer:** $A = 234$, $Z = 91$ (protactinium).

### Example 11: Number of decays

**Question:** ${}^{210}_{82}\text{Pb}$ decays to ${}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb}$. How many alpha and beta-minus particles are emitted?

**Method:**
1. $\Delta A = 210 - 206 = 4$, so one alpha-particle.
2. One alpha changes $Z$ by $-2$, but the final $Z$ is the same.
3. Two beta-minus decays each increase $Z$ by 1, restoring $Z$.

**Answer:** one alpha and two beta-minus particles.

### Example 12: Nuclear density ratio

**Question:** The radius of a nucleus is about $10^{-15}~\text{m}$ and the radius of an atom is about $10^{-10}~\text{m}$. Show that the density of nuclear matter is about $10^{15}$ times the density of the atom.

**Method:**
1. Volume ratio $= (10^{-10}/10^{-15})^3 = (10^5)^3 = 10^{15}$.
2. Density $= m/V$; almost all the mass is in the nucleus.
3. Nuclear density / atomic density $= (m/V_{\text{nucleus}})/(m/V_{\text{atom}}) = V_{\text{atom}}/V_{\text{nucleus}} = 10^{15}$.

**Answer:** the ratio is about $10^{15}$.

## 6. Exam-Style Questions (Full Solutions)

### Question 1

In Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment, most alpha-particles passed straight through a thin gold foil, but a small fraction were deflected through angles greater than $90^\circ$. (a) State what this shows about the structure of the atom. (b) Explain why the alpha-particles are deflected. (c) Explain why the foil is made very thin.

**Solution:**
1. (a) The atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny, massive, positively charged nucleus at the centre containing most of the mass.
2. (b) The alpha-particle is positive and experiences a repulsive electrostatic force from the positive nucleus. The closer the approach, the stronger the force and the greater the deflection; a head-on collision gives back-scattering.
3. (c) A thin foil ensures that an alpha-particle is likely to pass through without being absorbed, and reduces the chance of multiple scattering, so the deflection is due to a single close encounter with a nucleus.

**Answer:** as above.

### Question 2

Complete the following equations and state the type of decay:
(a) ${}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow \text{?} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$
(b) ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + \text{?}$
(c) ${}^{22}_{11}\text{Na} \rightarrow {}^{22}_{10}\text{Ne} + \text{?}$

**Solution:**
1. (a) $A = 218$, $Z = 84$: ${}^{218}_{84}\text{Po}$; alpha decay.
2. (b) $A = 0$, $Z = -1$: ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ (plus $\bar{\nu}_e$); beta-minus decay.
3. (c) $A = 0$, $Z = +1$: ${}^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$ (plus $\nu_e$); beta-plus decay.

**Answer:** (a) ${}^{218}_{84}\text{Po}$, alpha; (b) ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}_e$, beta-minus; (c) ${}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + \nu_e$, beta-plus.

### Question 3

Compare alpha, beta-minus and gamma radiation in terms of (a) charge, (b) ionising power, (c) penetrating power and (d) deflection in an electric field.

**Solution:**
1. (a) Alpha $+2e$; beta-minus $-e$; gamma 0.
2. (b) Alpha is most ionising (charged, massive, slow); beta is intermediate; gamma is least ionising.
3. (c) Alpha is least penetrating (paper/few cm air); beta is intermediate (few mm aluminium); gamma is most penetrating (cm of lead).
4. (d) Alpha deflects toward the negative plate; beta-minus toward the positive plate and much more strongly; gamma is undeflected.

**Answer:** as above.

### Question 4

A nucleus ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ decays by alpha emission followed by two beta-minus emissions. (a) Write the final nucleon and proton numbers. (b) State how the element changes. (c) Explain which conservation laws are obeyed.

**Solution:**
1. (a) Alpha: $A-4$, $Z-2$. Two beta-minus: $Z-2+2 = Z$; $A$ stays $A-4$.
2. (b) The final nucleus has the same $Z$ as the original but a different $A$, so it is a different isotope of the same element.
3. (c) Nucleon number is conserved ($A \rightarrow A-4+4$); proton number is conserved at each step when the emitted particles' charges are included; mass–energy is also conserved.

**Answer:** (a) $A-4$, $Z$; (b) same element, different isotope; (c) $A$, $Z$ and mass–energy.

### Question 5

Describe the quark model of the proton and neutron. Hence explain what happens in beta-minus decay.

**Solution:**
1. Proton = uud; charge $+\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{3} = +1$.
2. Neutron = udd; charge $+\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{3} = 0$.
3. In beta-minus decay, a neutron changes into a proton: one down quark changes into an up quark.
4. The nucleus emits an electron and an electron antineutrino: d → u + e$^-$ + $\bar{\nu}_e$.

**Answer:** proton uud, neutron udd; beta-minus is d → u with electron and antineutrino emission.

### Question 6

Explain why (a) alpha radiation is stopped by paper but gamma radiation is not, and (b) beta radiation is deflected more than alpha radiation in the same electric field.

**Solution:**
1. (a) Alpha particles are highly ionising because of their large charge and mass, so they lose energy rapidly in a short distance and are stopped by a few sheets of paper. Gamma photons are uncharged and interact weakly, so they can pass through paper and need dense, thick material such as lead to attenuate them.
2. (b) The electric force on a particle is $F = qE$. The beta-particle has a smaller charge magnitude than an alpha-particle, but its mass is about $1/7000$ as large. The acceleration $a = F/m$ is therefore much larger for the beta-particle, so it is deflected much more.

**Answer:** as above.

## 7. Cheat Sheet

**Nuclide notation:**
- ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$: $A$ = protons + neutrons, $Z$ = protons, $N = A-Z$.
- Neutral atom: electrons $= Z$.

**Decay changes:**
- Alpha: $A-4$, $Z-2$.
- Beta-minus: $A$ unchanged, $Z+1$, emit e$^-$ + $\bar{\nu}_e$.
- Beta-plus: $A$ unchanged, $Z-1$, emit e$^+$ + $\nu_e$.
- Gamma: $A$, $Z$ unchanged.

**Quarks:**
- u = $+2/3$, d = $-1/3$, s = $-1/3$.
- Proton uud, neutron udd.
- Beta-minus: d → u; beta-plus: u → d.

**Radiation ranking:**
- Ionising: $\alpha > \beta > \gamma$.
- Penetrating: $\gamma > \beta > \alpha$.
- Alpha: paper; beta: few mm Al; gamma: cm Pb.

**Rutherford:**
- Most straight → empty space.
- Few large deflections → tiny, massive, positive nucleus.
- Repulsive electrostatic force.

**Energy:**
- $1~\text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}~\text{J}$; $1~\text{MeV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-13}~\text{J}$.

## 8. Common Exam Mistakes

1. **Reading $A$ and $Z$ the wrong way round.** $A$ is the top (nucleon) number, $Z$ the bottom (proton) number.
2. **Saying isotopes have different proton numbers.** They have the **same** $Z$ and different $N$.
3. **Forgetting the antineutrino in beta-minus decay.** A beta-minus equation without $\bar{\nu}_e$ is incomplete in modern notation.
4. **Changing $A$ in beta decay.** Beta decay leaves the nucleon number unchanged; only $Z$ changes.
5. **Putting an electron in the nucleus.** Beta-particles are created at the moment of decay when a neutron changes into a proton; they were not orbiting inside the nucleus.
6. **Saying the nucleus attracts alpha-particles.** Both are positive, so the force is **repulsive**.
7. **Saying electrons cause the large deflections.** Electrons are far too light; the nucleus provides the concentrated positive charge and mass.
8. **Saying gamma is not ionising.** Gamma is weakly ionising; it is just far less ionising than alpha or beta.
9. **Confusing ionising power with penetrating power.** They are inversely related: the most ionising radiation is the least penetrating.
10. **Forgetting that alpha is dangerous inside the body.** Short range means concentrated energy deposition in tissue, even though it cannot penetrate skin.
11. **Using the wrong quark charge for down.** Down is $-1/3$, not $+1/3$.
12. **Treating a beta-particle as a hadron.** Beta-particles are leptons (electrons/positrons), not quarks.
