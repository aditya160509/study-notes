# Chapter 26: Magnetic fields and electromagnetism - CAIE A Level Physics (9702) Study Notes

Exam-focused revision notes for Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, covering magnetic fields and electromagnetism as taught in Chapter 26 of the coursebook. Everything here is what the exam can actually ask, nothing more. All definitions are worded to match mark schemes, and every question type below has appeared in real 9702 papers (mainly Paper 4 structured questions and Paper 1 multiple choice). Every theory section ends with a worked example, so you can see the formula being used the moment it is introduced.

## 1. Theory

### 1.1 Magnetic fields and field lines

A magnetic field is a **field of force**: it is a region of space where a magnetic pole (or a moving charge, or a current-carrying wire) experiences a force. You can make a magnetic field in two ways: with a permanent magnet, or with an electric current. All magnetic fields are actually created by **moving charges** - even in a permanent magnet, the field comes from electrons circulating within the atoms of the material (each electron acts as a tiny current loop). In a ferrous material like iron these tiny fields line up and combine into a strong field; in non-magnetic materials they cancel out.

We represent magnetic fields with **field lines**. Three rules:

- Field lines come **out of north poles** and go **into south poles**.
- The direction of a field line at any point shows the direction of the force that a 'free' magnetic north pole would experience there.
- The field is **strongest where the field lines are closest together** (the separation of the lines is a measure of the field's strength).

The field pattern of a bar magnet looks like a set of curved lines leaving the N pole and returning to the S pole. Two like poles repel (their lines push apart between them), two unlike poles attract (lines run straight through between them). In a **uniform field** the lines are parallel and equally spaced - you get one between the flat poles of two magnets facing each other.

**Worked example:** A plotting compass is placed at three points around a bar magnet. At which point does the compass needle point most strongly along the field direction, and why?

1. The compass needle aligns with the local field direction (it is a small free north pole).
2. The field is strongest where the field lines are closest together - close to the poles.
3. So the needle points most strongly (and the field is strongest) nearest the poles, where the lines bunch up.

**Answer:** nearest the poles, because that is where the field lines are closest together and the field is strongest.

**Worked example:** Two bar magnets are placed with their N poles facing each other, 5 cm apart. Describe the field between them and the force on each magnet.

1. Between two like poles the field lines push away from each other - they bulge out sideways rather than running straight across.
2. A free north pole between them would be pushed away from both N poles, so the field direction points outwards towards the gap.
3. Each magnet experiences a repulsive force, pushing the magnets apart.

**Answer:** the field lines between the two N poles curve away from the central line, and the magnets repel each other.

### 1.2 Fields from currents: wire, solenoid, flat coil, electromagnet

A current-carrying wire has a circular magnetic field around it, centred on the wire. The field lines are **concentric circles**; further from the wire they are drawn further apart, showing the field gets weaker with distance. Reversing the current reverses the direction of the field.

**Direction rules** (know both, they are slightly different):

- **Right-hand grip rule (solenoid/coil):** grip the coil with your right hand, fingers curling in the direction of the current. Your thumb points in the direction of the field lines **inside** the coil - towards the north pole.
- **Right-hand rule (straight wire):** grip the wire with your right hand, thumb pointing in the direction of the (conventional) current. Your fingers curl around in the direction of the field lines.
- Looking end-on at a solenoid: if the current is **clockwise** (as you look at it), that end is a **south pole**; **anticlockwise** means a **north pole**.

A **solenoid** is a long coil of wire. Its field pattern looks like that of a bar magnet - field lines emerge from a north pole at one end, run outside and return to the south pole at the other. Winding the coil concentrates the field. You can greatly increase the strength of the field by adding a core of a ferrous material (iron): the iron rod becomes magnetised by the current, adding its own strong field. A **flat circular coil** has a similar field to a short solenoid. An **electromagnet** is simply a solenoid (often with an iron core) whose field can be switched on and off with the current.

**Worked example:** A solenoid is wound so that, viewed from the left end, the current goes anticlockwise. Identify the poles and the direction of the field inside.

1. Viewing end-on, anticlockwise current means a north pole (the rule: anticlockwise = N, clockwise = S).
2. Field lines inside a solenoid run from the south pole towards the north pole, so inside the coil the field runs from right to left.
3. Outside the coil the lines leave the N pole and return to the S pole.

**Answer:** the left end is a north pole; inside the solenoid the field points towards the left end.

**Worked example:** A long straight wire carries a current of 2.0 A vertically upwards. In which direction do the field lines circle the wire, and what happens if the current is reversed?

1. Use the right-hand rule for a straight wire: thumb points up (current direction), fingers curl around the wire.
2. Viewed from above, the fingers curl anticlockwise, so the field circles anticlockwise (viewed from above).
3. Reversing the current makes the field circle in the opposite direction - clockwise viewed from above.

**Answer:** anticlockwise viewed from above; reversing the current reverses the field direction.

### 1.3 Force on a current-carrying conductor ($F = BIL$)

A current-carrying wire is surrounded by its own magnetic field. When it is placed in an **external** magnetic field, the two fields interact and the wire experiences a **force**. This is the **motor effect** - the basis of electric motors. The direction of the force is given by **Fleming's left-hand (motor) rule**: hold the thumb and first two fingers of your left hand mutually at right angles:

- **thuMb** - direction of Motion (the force)
- **First finger** - direction of the external Field
- **seCond finger** - direction of the Conventional current

A useful picture: think of the magnetic field lines as elastic bands. The field of the current and the field of the magnets combine; on one side of the wire the lines are squashed together (stronger field), and the 'elastic bands' push the wire out towards the weaker side.

**When is there a force?** A conductor experiences a force only when it **cuts across** magnetic field lines. If the wire is **parallel** to the field, there is **no force**. The maximum force occurs when the wire is at **right angles** to the field.

The size of the force is given by:

$$F = BIL$$

- $F$ is the force on the conductor, in newtons (N).
- $B$ is the magnetic flux density of the external field, in tesla (T).
- $I$ is the current in the conductor, in amperes (A).
- $L$ is the length of the conductor inside the field, in metres (m).

This equation is only valid when the field is at right angles to the current.

**Worked example:** A current of 0.20 A flows in a wire of length 2.50 m placed at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 0.06 T. Calculate the force on the wire. (Coursebook Question 5)

1. The wire is at right angles to the field, so use $F = BIL$ directly.
2. Substitute: $F = 0.06 \times 0.20 \times 2.50 = 0.03~\text{N}$.

**Answer:** $F = 0.03~\text{N}$.

**Worked example:** A 20 cm length of wire is placed at right angles to a magnetic field. When a current of 1.5 A flows in the wire, a force of 0.015 N acts on it. Determine the flux density of the field. (Coursebook Question 6)

1. Convert the length: $L = 20~\text{cm} = 0.20~\text{m}$.
2. Rearrange $F = BIL$ for $B$: $B = \frac{F}{IL}$.
3. Substitute: $B = \frac{0.015}{1.5 \times 0.20} = 0.05~\text{T}$.

**Answer:** $B = 0.05~\text{T}$.

**Worked example:** A wire of length 50 cm carrying a current lies at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 5.0 mT. If $10^{18}$ electrons pass a point in the wire each second, what current flows, and what force acts on the wire? ($e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}~\text{C}$) (Coursebook Question 7)

1. Current is charge per unit time: $I = \frac{Ne}{t} = 10^{18} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 0.16~\text{A}$.
2. Convert length and flux density: $L = 0.50~\text{m}$, $B = 5.0 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$.
3. Use $F = BIL$: $F = 5.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.16 \times 0.50 = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$.

**Answer:** $I = 0.16~\text{A}$; $F = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$.

### 1.4 Magnetic flux density (the tesla, and $F = BIL \sin\theta$)

The **strength** of a magnetic field is called its **magnetic flux density**, symbol $B$. Think of it as the number of magnetic field lines passing through a region per unit area. By analogy with gravitational field strength $g = \frac{F}{m}$ and electric field strength $E = \frac{F}{Q}$, the magnetic flux density is defined in terms of the force on a current-carrying conductor placed at **right angles** to the field:

> The magnetic flux density at a point in space is the force experienced per unit length by a long straight conductor carrying unit current and placed at right angles to the field at that point.

$$B = \frac{F}{IL}$$

The unit of magnetic flux density is the **tesla** (T):

> The magnetic flux density is 1 T when a wire carrying a current of 1 A placed at right angles to the magnetic field experiences a force of 1 N per metre of its length.

So $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{N A}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}$.

**At an angle other than 90°:** when the conductor cuts across the field at an angle $\theta$, only the component of $B$ at right angles to the current matters. That component is $B\sin\theta$, giving:

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

- $\theta$ is the angle between the magnetic field and the current (or the conductor).
- When $\theta = 90°$, $\sin\theta = 1$ and you get $F = BIL$ (maximum).
- When $\theta = 0°$ (wire parallel to the field), $\sin\theta = 0$ and there is no force.

The component of $B$ parallel to the current, $B\cos\theta$, does not contribute to the force. The force is always at right angles to both the current and the field.

**Worked example:** A conductor OC of length 0.20 m lies at an angle of 25° to a magnetic field of flux density 0.050 T. Calculate the force on the conductor when it carries a current of 400 mA. (Coursebook Worked Example 2)

1. Convert the current: $I = 400~\text{mA} = 0.40~\text{A}$.
2. The wire is not at right angles to the field, so use $F = BIL\sin\theta$: $F = 0.050 \times 0.40 \times 0.20 \times \sin 25°$.
3. $\sin 25° = 0.423$, so $F = 0.050 \times 0.40 \times 0.20 \times 0.423 = 1.7 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$.
4. Direction: at 90° to both the field and the current (use the left-hand rule for the exact direction).

**Answer:** $F = 1.7 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$, acting perpendicular to both the field and the current.

**Worked example:** A wire of length 50 cm carrying a current of 2.4 A lies at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 5.0 mT. Calculate the force on the wire. (Coursebook Question 10)

1. Convert: $L = 0.50~\text{m}$, $B = 5.0 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$.
2. At right angles, $F = BIL$: $F = 5.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 2.4 \times 0.50 = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$.

**Answer:** $F = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$.

### 1.5 Measuring magnetic flux density (current balance and Hall probe)

**Current balance (top-pan balance method):** place the magnet arrangement on a top-pan balance and zero the reading. A wire carrying a current $I$ passes through the uniform field between the magnet poles, with its length $L$ in the field measured with a ruler. The wire experiences a force $F = BIL$ (upwards or downwards); by Newton's third law the magnets are pushed the other way, and the balance shows the force as a change in mass. The force is found from $F = mg$ (the change in mass $\times g = 9.81~\text{m s}^{-2}$), then:

$$B = \frac{F}{IL}$$

A version with pivots and counterweights (a proper **current balance**) works the same way: add small weights until the frame balances again; the added weight equals the magnetic force. You can also use the arrangement to show that the force is directly proportional to the current (vary $I$, watch the balance reading change proportionally).

**Hall probe:** a thin strip of semiconductor which gives a voltage proportional to the magnetic flux density through its flat face. Hold it so the field lines pass at right angles through the face; rotating it so the field passes through at an angle reduces the reading. It is sensitive enough to measure the Earth's magnetic field: hold it aligned with the field for a maximum reading, rotate through 180° and the change in reading is twice the field's contribution. (How it works is covered in Chapter 27.)

**Worked example:** In a current balance, a current of 0.50 A flows and a counterweight of mass 0.02 g is needed to restore balance. The section of the conductor in the field is 5.0 cm long. What is the flux density of the field? (Coursebook Question 9)

1. Convert: $m = 0.02~\text{g} = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}~\text{kg}$, $L = 5.0~\text{cm} = 0.050~\text{m}$.
2. The magnetic force equals the weight of the counterweight: $F = mg = 2.0 \times 10^{-5} \times 9.81 = 1.96 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$.
3. Use $B = \frac{F}{IL}$: $B = \frac{1.96 \times 10^{-4}}{0.50 \times 0.050} = 7.8 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$.

**Answer:** $B = 7.8 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$.

### 1.6 Forces between currents (parallel wires attract, anti-parallel repel)

Every current has a magnetic field around it. Put two currents near each other and their fields interact, producing a force on each wire. The rule:

- **Parallel currents (same direction) attract** each other.
- **Anti-parallel currents (opposite directions) repel** each other.

This is the opposite of electric charges (like charges repel) and opposite magnetic poles (N-S attract). It is the basis of the SI definition of the ampere: the ampere is defined through the magnetic force between two parallel wires.

**Explaining the force - way 1 (the fields):** draw the fields of the two wires. For anti-parallel currents, the field lines between the wires run the same way and add up - the lines between the wires are squashed together, making an extra-strong field there, and the 'elastic band' picture pushes the wires apart. For parallel currents, the fields between the wires oppose and cancel out, so the lines outside are stronger and the wires are pushed together.

**Explaining the force - way 2 (the motor effect):** take wire 1's field; wire 2 carries a current across it. From the field of wire 1, the field at wire 2 is at right angles to wire 2's current, so wire 2 experiences a $BIL$ force, with direction given by Fleming's left-hand rule - towards wire 1. By Newton's third law there is an equal and opposite force on wire 1, towards wire 2. The two forces are an action-reaction pair.

**Worked example:** Two flat circular coils are set up side by side, connected in series so the same current flows around each in the same direction (as seen from the space between them). Will the coils attract or repel? (Coursebook Question 13)

1. As electromagnets: each flat coil behaves like a short solenoid. With the current circulating in the same sense as seen from the gap, the two facing faces are the same type of pole (both north or both south).
2. Like poles repel, so as electromagnets the coils repel.
3. Considering the parallel-current rule: look at the nearest sides of the two coils. The current in the side of coil A nearest the gap flows in the opposite direction to the current in the side of coil B nearest the gap (one goes up, the other down). Anti-parallel currents repel.
4. Both explanations agree: the coils repel.

**Answer:** the coils repel. As electromagnets the facing ends are like poles; and the nearest sides of the two coils carry anti-parallel currents, which repel. (Note the trick: 'the same direction' is judged from between the coils - viewed from the same outside end, the currents would be opposite and the answer would flip.)

### 1.7 Relating SI units (the tesla in base units)

The SI system has seven **base units**: m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd. All other units are **derived units**, deducible from the base units by replacing quantities in formulae with their units:

- Newton: from $F = ma$, $1~\text{N} = 1~\text{kg m s}^{-2}$.
- Joule: from $W = Fd$, $1~\text{J} = 1~\text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-2}$.
- Watt: from $P = \frac{W}{t}$, $1~\text{W} = 1~\text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-3}$.
- Coulomb: from $Q = It$, $1~\text{C} = 1~\text{A s}$.
- Volt: from $V = \frac{W}{Q}$, $1~\text{V} = 1~\text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-3}\text{A}^{-1}$.
- **Tesla**: from $B = \frac{F}{IL}$, $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{N A}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1} = 1~\text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$.

The ampere is itself a base unit; the tesla is defined in terms of the amp, the metre and the newton (and hence in terms of kg, m, s, A).

**Worked example:** Show from $F = BIL$ that the tesla can be written as $\text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$.

1. Rearrange $F = BIL$ for $B$: $B = \frac{F}{IL}$.
2. Replace each quantity by its unit: $F$ in N, $I$ in A, $L$ in m, so $1~\text{T} = \frac{\text{N}}{\text{A m}} = \text{N A}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}$.
3. Replace the newton: $1~\text{N} = \text{kg m s}^{-2}$, so $1~\text{T} = \text{kg m s}^{-2} \times \text{A}^{-1} \times \text{m}^{-1} = \text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$.

**Answer:** $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$.

### 1.8 Comparing magnetic, electric and gravitational fields

All three fields share these features: action at a distance; strength decreasing with distance from the source; representation by field lines (direction shows the direction of the force, density shows relative strength). Each is defined as force per unit of the relevant 'test' quantity - force per unit mass ($g$), force per unit positive charge ($E$), force per unit current per unit length ($B$).

The forces are wildly different in size:

- Force between two 1 kg masses 1 m apart: $6.7 \times 10^{-11}~\text{N}$ (gravity is by far the weakest).
- Force between two charges of 1 C placed 1 m apart: $9.0 \times 10^9~\text{N}$ (electric is by far the strongest).
- Force per metre between two wires carrying 1 A placed 1 m apart: $2.0 \times 10^{-7}~\text{N}$.

For an electron orbiting a proton in a hydrogen atom, the electrical force is about $10^{39}$ times the gravitational force - so electric forces dominate for small charged objects. But over large distances with large masses (planets, stars), gravity dominates. In modern physics, electric and magnetic fields are two parts of one combined electromagnetic field; gravitational fields are a different thing.

**Worked example:** A proton and an electron in a hydrogen atom are separated by about $5 \times 10^{-11}~\text{m}$. Without calculating exact values, explain why the electric force between them is enormously larger than the gravitational force.

1. The electric force is proportional to the product of the charges (about $1.6 \times 10^{-19}~\text{C}$ each) divided by $r^2$; the gravitational force is proportional to the product of the masses (about $10^{-27}$ and $10^{-31}~\text{kg}$) divided by $r^2$.
2. The charges are only about $10^{19}$ times smaller than 1 C, but the masses are about $10^{27}$ and $10^{31}$ times smaller than 1 kg - the mass product is smaller by a factor of about $10^{58}$ more than the charge product.
3. The ratio of electric to gravitational force is therefore enormous (about $10^{39}$), as the coursebook states.

**Answer:** because the electric force depends on charge (which is relatively large for these particles) while the gravitational force depends on their tiny masses - the charge-to-mass imbalance makes the electric force about $10^{39}$ times larger.

## 2. Definition Sheet

Definitions worded to match 9702 mark schemes. The exact phrasing matters: examiners award marks for the key phrases in bold.

1. **Magnetic field** - a region of space in which a magnetic pole (or a moving charge or current-carrying conductor) experiences a force. All magnetic fields are produced by **moving charges**.
2. **Magnetic field lines** - lines used to represent a magnetic field; the direction of a line at a point shows the direction of the force on a free north pole placed there, and the closeness of the lines shows the field's strength. Lines come out of north poles and go into south poles.
3. **Magnetic flux density, $B$** - the magnetic flux density at a point in space is **the force experienced per unit length by a long straight conductor carrying unit current placed at right angles to the field** at that point. Unit: tesla (T).
4. **Tesla (definition)** - the magnetic flux density is **1 T when a wire carrying a current of 1 A placed at right angles to the magnetic field experiences a force of 1 N per metre** of its length. $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{N A}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1} = 1~\text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$.
5. **Motor effect** - the force experienced by a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field (because the field of the current interacts with the external field).
6. **Fleming's left-hand rule (motor rule)** - if the thumb and first two fingers of the **left** hand are held mutually at right angles, with the **First** finger pointing in the direction of the magnetic **Field** and the se**C**ond finger in the direction of the **Current**, then the thu**M**b points in the direction of the **Motion** (force).
7. **Right-hand grip rule** - grip a coil with the right hand, fingers curling in the direction of the current; the thumb points in the direction of the magnetic field lines inside the coil (towards the north pole).
8. **Right-hand rule (straight wire)** - grip a wire with the right hand, thumb pointing in the direction of the conventional current; the fingers curl around in the direction of the circular magnetic field lines.
9. **Uniform magnetic field** - a field in which the flux density is the same (in magnitude and direction) at every point; represented by parallel, equally spaced field lines (e.g. between two flat opposite poles).
10. **Electromagnet** - a solenoid (often with a soft-iron core) whose magnetic field is produced by an electric current and can be switched on and off.
11. **Current balance** - an apparatus that measures magnetic flux density by weighing (balancing) the magnetic force $F = BIL$ on a current-carrying conductor, using $B = \frac{F}{IL}$.
12. **Hall probe** - a device containing a thin strip of semiconductor that gives a voltage proportional to the magnetic flux density passing at right angles through its face; used to measure $B$.
13. **Parallel currents rule** - two parallel conductors carrying currents in the **same direction attract**; carrying currents in **opposite directions repel**.

## 3. Formula Sheet

| Quantity | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Force on a conductor in a field | $F = BIL$ | Only when the field is at right angles to the current |
| Force at an angle | $F = BIL\sin\theta$ | $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the current |
| Magnetic flux density | $B = \frac{F}{IL}$ | Force per unit length per unit current, at right angles |
| Weight from a current balance | $F = mg$ | $\Delta m \times g$; the magnetic force equals the change in balance reading |

| Current from charge flow | $I = \frac{Ne}{t}$ | $N$ electrons of charge $e$ passing per second |
| Force per metre between two long parallel wires | $\frac{F}{L} = BI$ | Wire 1's field at wire 2: $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\frac{I}{x}$ |

**Constants you need:** electron charge $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}~\text{C}$, $g = 9.81~\text{m s}^{-2}$, and the field a distance $x$ (m) from a long straight wire carrying current $I$ (A) is $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\frac{I}{x}$.

**Exam fact:** on the 9702 data sheet, the force equations are given as $F = BIL$ and $F = BQv$ (the latter is the force on a moving charge, Chapter 27). The $F = BIL\sin\theta$ form and the wire field $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\frac{I}{x}$ are not listed - derive them from $F = BIL$ and the definitions when you need them.

## 4. Methods (Question Types)

**Method 1: Sketching or describing magnetic field patterns**

- For a straight wire: concentric circles centred on the wire; spacing increases with distance (weaker field). Use the right-hand rule for direction - thumb = current, fingers = field.
- For a solenoid or flat coil: pattern like a bar magnet - lines leave one end (N), curve round outside, enter the other end (S); inside the coil the lines are parallel and close together (strong uniform field).
- Reversing the current reverses every arrow. Doubling the current doesn't change the shape, only the strength (lines drawn closer together).

**Traps:** forgetting that the field inside a solenoid runs from S to N (the same direction as the thumb); drawing the field of a wire as a dipole (it has no poles); reversing only some of the arrows when the current reverses.

**Method 2: Finding the force on a straight conductor ($F = BIL$)**

1. Check the angle: if the wire is at right angles to the field, use $F = BIL$; if at angle $\theta$, use $F = BIL\sin\theta$; if parallel, the force is zero.
2. Convert everything to SI: cm to m, mA to A, mT to T.
3. Substitute and give the unit (N). State the direction using Fleming's left-hand rule (or say 'at right angles to both field and current').

**Traps:** using the full wire length instead of the length inside the field; using $\sin\theta$ when the angle given is between the field and the wire but measured the wrong way (the angle must be between the field direction and the current direction); forgetting that a wire parallel to the field feels no force at all.

**Method 3: Measuring $B$ with a current balance**

1. Find the magnetic force from the balance: $F = mg$, where $m$ is the change in reading (or the balancing mass) and $g = 9.81~\text{m s}^{-2}$.
2. Measure the length $L$ of wire actually inside the field.
3. Use $B = \frac{F}{IL}$.

**Traps:** using the whole mass on the balance instead of the change; forgetting that the magnets experience the reaction force (Newton's third law), not the wire's own weight; giving the answer in mT when the working used T.

**Method 4: Direction questions (which way does the wire move / which way does the force act)**

1. Identify the direction of the external magnetic field (N to S).
2. Identify the direction of the conventional current.
3. Apply Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion.
4. For a force ON a wire crossing a field: check whether the current is into or out of the page (the $\times$/$\bullet$ convention).

**Traps:** using the right hand (Fleming's rule is left-hand for motors); using electron flow instead of conventional current; confusing the force direction with the field direction (they are always perpendicular).

**Method 5: Torque on a motor coil**

1. Force on one side: $F = BIL$ for each side at right angles to the field.
2. The two opposite sides feel equal and opposite forces - a couple. Torque = force $\times$ perpendicular separation of the two forces.
3. Maximum torque when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field (sides at right angles to the field); zero torque when the coil face is perpendicular to the field.

**Traps:** using the coil area instead of the side length in $F = BIL$; halving the separation instead of using the full distance between the two forces; saying 'more turns makes the motor faster' without noting that torque increases with each extra turn ($N$ times the single-coil torque).

**Method 6: Forces between parallel wires**

1. Parallel currents (same direction) attract; anti-parallel currents (opposite directions) repel.
2. To find the size of the force, treat one wire's field as the external field acting on the other wire: $F = BIL$ where $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\frac{I}{x}$ at distance $x$.
3. By Newton's third law the forces on the two wires are equal and opposite.

**Traps:** saying opposite currents attract (they repel - the opposite of electric charges); forgetting the factor of 2.0 in the wire field formula; using the wire separation as the length $L$.

**Method 7: SI base-unit questions (show that $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$)**

1. Start from a formula you know, e.g. $B = \frac{F}{IL}$.
2. Replace every quantity by its base unit: N, A, m.
3. Replace the newton: $1~\text{N} = 1~\text{kg m s}^{-2}$, and simplify.

**Traps:** writing T = N m A (missing the inverse); forgetting that the ampere is a base unit; mixing up $\text{kg m s}^{-2}$ (N) with $\text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-2}$ (J).

**Method 8: Top-pan balance / lifting-wire problems**

1. For the wire to just lift off: the magnetic force equals the wire's weight, $BIL = mg$.
2. For a balance reading change: the magnetic force on the wire is $F = \Delta m \cdot g$ (the balance measures the reaction force, which changes by the magnetic force).
3. Work out the direction from the left-hand rule, then decide whether the reading increases or decreases.

**Traps:** ignoring the direction of the force when deciding if the balance reading goes up or down; using the wire's total length when only part of it is in the field; forgetting $g$ when converting mass to force.

## 5. Solved Examples

Real exam-style questions from the end of Chapter 26, with their mark allocations, plus the book's worked examples. The format for every example: Question / Method / Answer / What the examiner wants.

### Example 1: When is there a force? - End-of-chapter Q1, [1] + [1] marks

**Question:** A current-carrying wire is placed in a uniform magnetic field. **a** When does the wire experience no force due to the magnetic field? **b** When does the wire experience the maximum force?

**Method:** The force depends on the angle between the current and the field. There is no force when the conductor is parallel to the field lines; the force is maximum when the conductor cuts the field at right angles.

**Answer:** **a** when the wire (current) is parallel to the magnetic field lines. **b** when the wire is at right angles (90°) to the magnetic field.

**What the examiner wants:** the phrase 'parallel to the field' for zero force and 'at right angles' / 'perpendicular' for maximum force. Saying 'when the current is at 90°' without mentioning the field loses the point - the angle is between the current direction and the field direction.

### Example 2: Scaling the force - End-of-chapter Q2, [2] + [2] + [2] marks

**Question:** A current-carrying conductor placed at right angles to a uniform magnetic field experiences a force of $4.70 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$. Determine the force on the wire when, separately: **a** the current is increased by a factor of 3.0; **b** the magnetic flux density is halved; **c** the length of the wire in the field is reduced to 40% of its original length.

**Method:** With the wire at right angles to the field, $F = BIL$, so $F$ is proportional to $I$, to $B$ and to $L$. Scale the original force by the appropriate factor in each part.

**Answer:**
- **a** $F = 3.0 \times 4.70 \times 10^{-3} = 1.41 \times 10^{-2}~\text{N}$.
- **b** $F = \frac{1}{2} \times 4.70 \times 10^{-3} = 2.35 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$.
- **c** $F = 0.40 \times 4.70 \times 10^{-3} = 1.88 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$.

**What the examiner wants:** proportional reasoning marks. Each part is a direct scaling of $F = BIL$ - no need to invent values of $B$, $I$ or $L$. The final force is still at right angles to the field, so $F = BIL$ (not $BIL\sin\theta$) applies throughout.

### Example 3: Force at an angle - End-of-chapter Q3, [3] + [1] marks

**Question:** A copper wire carrying a current of 1.2 A has 3.0 cm of its length placed in a uniform magnetic field, as shown in Figure 26.26. The force experienced by the wire is $3.8 \times 10^{-3}~\text{N}$ when the angle between the wire and the magnetic field is 50°. The current is downwards into the paper. **a** Calculate the magnetic field strength. **b** What is the direction of the force experienced by the wire?

**Method:**
1. The wire is at 50° to the field, so use $F = BIL\sin\theta$ rearranged: $B = \frac{F}{IL\sin\theta}$.
2. Convert: $L = 3.0~\text{cm} = 0.030~\text{m}$.
3. Substitute: $B = \frac{3.8 \times 10^{-3}}{1.2 \times 0.030 \times \sin 50°} = \frac{3.8 \times 10^{-3}}{1.2 \times 0.030 \times 0.766} = 0.138~\text{T}$.
4. Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule, with the field direction as drawn and current into the paper; the force is at right angles to both the current and the field.

**Answer:** **a** $B = 0.14~\text{T}$ (2 s.f.). **b** the force acts at right angles to the wire and to the field, in the direction given by the left-hand rule (perpendicular to the plane containing the wire and the field).

**What the examiner wants:** the $\sin\theta$ must be included (the most common error is using $F = BIL$ here); the angle used must be the angle between the wire and the field. Direction marks come from naming the left-hand rule and quoting the mutual-perpendicular property, not from guessing a compass point off the diagram.

### Example 4: Fields and forces between two strips - End-of-chapter Q4, [2] + [4] marks

**Question:** Figure 26.27 shows a view from above of two long, parallel strips of aluminium foil A and B, carrying a current. **a** On a copy of the diagram, draw the magnetic field around and between the two strips. **b** State and explain the direction of the forces caused by the current in the strips.

**Method:**
1. Each strip has concentric circular field lines around it; use the right-hand rule for the arrow direction (thumb = current, fingers = field).
2. The current is in the same direction in both strips (as in Figure 26.27), so the fields between the strips oppose and cancel; the lines outside are stronger.
3. Parallel currents attract: the force on A is towards B, and the force on B is towards A (action-reaction pair).

**Answer:** **a** concentric circles around each strip, arrows following the right-hand rule; between the strips the arrows oppose (fields cancel), outside they add. **b** the strips attract - the forces point towards each other, because parallel currents attract (or: each strip is in the other's field, and $F = BIL$ with the left-hand rule pulls them together).

**What the examiner wants:** both explanation styles are acceptable - field-line argument (fields cancel between, strengthen outside, 'elastic band' picture pushes them together) or the $BIL$ force on one wire in the other's field. The examiner specifically rewards 'parallel currents attract' stated with the direction of the force on each strip.

### Example 5: Compasses around a wire - End-of-chapter Q5, [2] + [2] + [2] marks

**Question:** Figure 26.28 shows a wire XY which carries a constant direct current. Plotting compass R, placed alongside the wire, points due north. Compass P is placed below the wire and compass Q is placed above the wire. **a** State the direction of the current in the wire. **b** State in which direction compass P points. **c** State in which direction compass Q points if the current in the wire is reversed.

**Method:**
1. Compass R points north, so the field at R (to the side of the wire) points north. Use the right-hand rule for a straight wire: thumb in the current direction, fingers curl in the field direction.
2. If the current flows upwards (from Y to X), the field circles anticlockwise viewed from above: at the east side of the wire the field points north - matching R. So the current flows from Y to X.
3. With the current up, the field at a point below the wire (P) points west; with the current reversed (now downwards), the field at a point above the wire (Q) circles clockwise viewed from above, so it points east.

**Answer:** **a** the current flows upwards, from Y to X. **b** P points west. **c** Q points east.

**What the examiner wants:** the current direction must be justified (via the right-hand rule matching compass R's north-pointing field). The compass needle always aligns with the local field direction - it points in the direction of the field at that point, which for P and Q is found by curling the fingers around the wire in the correct sense.

### Example 6: Rectangular frame in a uniform field - End-of-chapter Q6, [3] + [1] + [2] + [2] marks

**Question:** Figure 26.29 shows a rectangular metal frame PQRS (sides 4.0 cm and 7.0 cm) placed in a uniform magnetic field of flux density $4.5 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$. The current in the frame is 2.5 A. **a** Calculate the force experienced by side PQ of the frame. **b** Suggest why side QR does not experience a force. **c** Describe the motion of the frame immediately after the current is switched on. **d** Calculate the maximum torque (moment) exerted about an axis parallel to side PQ.

**Method:**
1. Side PQ is 4.0 cm long, at right angles to the field, so $F = BIL$: $F = 4.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 2.5 \times 0.040 = 4.5 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$.
2. Side QR carries its current parallel to the magnetic field lines, so $\sin\theta = 0$ and there is no force.
3. The force on PQ is opposite to the force on RS (equal and opposite, forming a couple), so the frame rotates about an axis through the other two sides.
4. Maximum torque about an axis parallel to PQ: only the force on RS acts about this axis (PQ lies on the axis). $F = BIL = 4.5 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$, separation = 7.0 cm, so torque $= F \times d = 4.5 \times 10^{-4} \times 0.070 = 3.2 \times 10^{-5}~\text{N m}$.

**Answer:** **a** $F = 4.5 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$. **b** because side QR is parallel to the magnetic field (the current in it is parallel to the field lines), so there is no force on it. **c** the frame rotates (about an axis parallel to QR / through the centres of PQ and RS) because the forces on PQ and RS form a couple. **d** maximum torque $= 3.2 \times 10^{-5}~\text{N m}$.

**What the examiner wants:** the length used in part (a) is the side in the field at right angles to it (4.0 cm, not 7.0 cm). Part (b) rewards the word 'parallel' - 'no force because the current is parallel to the field'. Part (d) wants torque = force on the far side $\times$ perpendicular distance between the sides; the force on PQ itself contributes nothing about an axis through PQ.

### Example 7: Current balance with a paper tape - End-of-chapter Q7, [1] + [2] + [3] + [1] marks

**Question:** Figure 26.30 shows a current-carrying wire frame placed between a pair of Magnadur magnets on a yoke; a pointer is attached to the wire. A current of 8.5 A in the wire causes the pointer to move vertically upwards. A small paper tape attached to the pointer brings it back to its initial position; the paper's mass is 60 mg. The section of the wire between the poles has a length of 5.2 cm. **a** State the direction of the magnetic field. **b** Calculate the force on the wire due to the magnetic field when it carries a current of 8.5 A. **c** Calculate the magnetic flux density between the poles. **d** Describe what happens to the frame if low-frequency alternating current passes through the wire.

**Method:**
1. The field is between the N and S poles of the magnets, so it is horizontal, at right angles to the wire, running from the north pole to the south pole.
2. The magnetic force on the wire equals the weight of the paper tape: $F = mg = 60 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.81 = 5.9 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$.
3. $B = \frac{F}{IL} = \frac{5.9 \times 10^{-4}}{8.5 \times 0.052} = 1.33 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$.
4. With alternating current, the direction of the force reverses every half-cycle, so the pointer vibrates up and down at the supply frequency (twice per cycle the force changes direction - net effect: oscillation about the initial position).

**Answer:** **a** horizontal, from the north pole to the south pole (at right angles to the wire). **b** $F = 5.9 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N}$. **c** $B = 1.3 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$ (2 s.f.). **d** the pointer oscillates up and down (vibrates) as the force on the wire alternates in direction.

**What the examiner wants:** part (b) is a Newton's-third-law balance: the tape's weight exactly balances the magnetic force, so $F = mg$. Part (c) needs the length in the field (5.2 cm, not the whole frame). Part (d): the force alternates direction with the current, so the wire vibrates at the a.c. frequency - a steady deflection is not possible with a.c.

### Example 8: Demonstrating $F \propto I$, and a lifting wire - End-of-chapter Q8, [5] + [1] + [3] marks

**Question:** **a** The size of the force acting on a wire carrying a current in a magnetic field is proportional to the size of the current. With the aid of a diagram, describe how this can be demonstrated in a school laboratory. **b** At a certain point on the Earth's surface the horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field is $1.6 \times 10^{-5}~\text{T}$. A piece of wire 3.0 m long and of weight 0.020 N lies in an east-west direction on a laboratory bench. When a large current flows in the wire, the wire just lifts off the surface of the bench. **i** State the direction of the current in the wire. **ii** Calculate the minimum current needed to lift the wire from the bench.

**Method:**
1. **a** Set up a wire (or a section of it) at right angles to the field between the poles of a strong magnet, with the magnet on a top-pan balance. Connect the wire, an ammeter and a variable resistor in series with a power supply. Vary the current, and for each value record the current and the change in the balance reading (which gives the force via $F = mg$). Plot force against current: a straight line through the origin shows $F \propto I$.
2. **b i** For the wire to lift, the magnetic force must act upwards, against the weight. With the field pointing north (horizontal component) and the force upwards, the left-hand rule gives: First finger = Field (north), thuMb = Motion (up), so the seCond finger points east - the current flows from west to east (towards the east). (Cross-product check: $F = IL \times B$, with east $\times$ north $=$ up, confirms it.)

3. **b ii** Just lifting off means the magnetic force equals the weight: $BIL = mg$, so $I = \frac{mg}{BL} = \frac{0.020}{1.6 \times 10^{-5} \times 3.0} = \frac{0.020}{4.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 4.2 \times 10^{2}~\text{A}$.

**Answer:** **a** (description as Method 1; diagram showing magnet on top-pan balance, wire through the field, ammeter and variable resistor in series; straight-line graph of $F$ against $I$ through the origin). **b i** the current flows from west to east (eastwards). **b ii** $I = 4.2 \times 10^{2}~\text{A}$ (420 A).

**What the examiner wants:** part (a) is a 5-mark practical: named apparatus (top-pan balance or current balance, ammeter, rheostat/variable resistor), the wire at right angles to the field, force found from the balance reading, repeated readings, and the straight-line-through-origin conclusion. Part (b) needs the cross-product/left-hand logic spelled out (field north + force up $\Rightarrow$ current east), and the calculation uses the FULL 3.0 m of wire in the field.

### Example 9: Top-pan balance and the magnets - End-of-chapter Q9, [2] + [2] + [2] + [2] marks

**Question:** Figure 26.31 shows a fixed horizontal wire passing centrally between the poles of a permanent magnet placed on a top-pan balance. With no current, the balance records 102.45 g. When a current of 4.0 A flows in the wire, the balance records 101.06 g. **a** Explain why the reading decreases when the current is switched on. **b** State and explain the direction of the current flow in the wire. **c** The length of the wire in the magnetic field is 5.0 cm. Calculate the average magnetic flux density between the poles. **d** Sketch a graph, with balance reading on the vertical axis and current on the horizontal axis, to show how the balance reading changes when the current is altered.

**Method:**
1. The wire is fixed. The magnetic force on the wire is downwards; by Newton's third law the magnets experience an equal force upwards, so the force on the balance decreases - the reading falls by the magnetic force.
2. Field direction: from the N pole to the S pole (horizontal, left to right in the figure). Force on the wire: downwards. Left-hand rule (First finger = field, thuMb = motion of the wire = down): the seCond finger points into the paper, so the current flows downwards into the paper.
3. The decrease in balance reading corresponds to the magnetic force: $\Delta m = 102.45 - 101.06 = 1.39~\text{g} = 1.39 \times 10^{-3}~\text{kg}$. $F = \Delta m \cdot g = 1.39 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.81 = 1.36 \times 10^{-2}~\text{N}$. Then $B = \frac{F}{IL} = \frac{1.36 \times 10^{-2}}{4.0 \times 0.050} = 0.068~\text{T}$.
4. Graph: a straight line starting at 102.45 g at $I = 0$ and falling linearly as $I$ increases (force $\propto$ current). (For reversed current the line rises instead.)

**Answer:** **a** the wire experiences a downward magnetic force, so (Newton's third law) the magnets are pushed upwards, reducing the force on the balance. **b** current flows downwards into the paper (left-hand rule: field left-to-right, force on wire downwards). **c** $B = 0.068~\text{T}$. **d** straight line of negative slope through (0, 102.45 g) - the reading decreases linearly with current.

**What the examiner wants:** part (a) must mention Newton's third law (action-reaction between wire and magnets) - 'the wire pushes the magnet up' alone loses the mark. Part (b) wants the left-hand rule applied with both field and force directions stated. Part (c) is the standard $\Delta m g / IL$ route; part (d) wants a straight line (linear proportionality), not a curve.

### Example 10: Definition, stacked wires, force per unit length - End-of-chapter Q10, [3] + [4] + [3] marks

**Question:** **a** Define magnetic flux density and explain the similarity with the definition of electric field strength. **b** Two thin horizontal wires are placed in a north-south direction, one on a bench, the other held 3.0 cm directly above the first. **i** When equal currents flow in the two wires, the force exerted on the bench by the lower wire decreases. Explain why, and say what you can about the directions of the currents. **ii** The magnetic flux density $B$ at a distance $x$ from a long straight wire carrying current $I$ is $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\frac{I}{x}$. When the current in each wire is 4.0 A, calculate the force per unit length on one wire due to the current in the other.

**Method:**
1. **a** Magnetic flux density at a point: the force per unit length on a long straight conductor carrying unit current placed at right angles to the field at that point. Electric field strength is force per unit positive charge - both are defined as force per unit 'test' quantity, measured by a test object at the point.
2. **b i** The lower wire presses on the bench less hard, so it feels a net upward magnetic force; the force between the wires is therefore attractive, which happens when the currents are in the same direction (parallel currents attract).
3. **b ii** Field of one wire at the other: $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7} \times \frac{4.0}{0.030} = 2.67 \times 10^{-5}~\text{T}$. Force per unit length on the other wire: $\frac{F}{L} = BI = 2.67 \times 10^{-5} \times 4.0 = 1.07 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N m}^{-1}$.

**Answer:** **a** definition as in the Definition Sheet; similarity - both fields are defined as force per unit quantity (per unit charge for $E$, per unit current per unit length for $B$) on a test object placed at the point. **b i** the magnetic force between the wires is upward on the lower wire (attractive), so the currents flow in the same direction. **b ii** $\frac{F}{L} = 1.1 \times 10^{-4}~\text{N m}^{-1}$ (2 s.f.).

**What the examiner wants:** the definition must say 'force per unit length' and 'unit current' and 'at right angles' - missing any of the three costs a mark. The similarity mark is for 'force per unit (test) quantity' phrasing. Part (b i) links the reduced bench force to an upward magnetic force on the lower wire, hence attraction, hence same-direction currents. Part (b ii) uses $x = 3.0~\text{cm} = 0.030~\text{m}$ and the force-per-unit-length form - a common slip is using $x$ in cm.

## 6. Cheat Sheet

**The one-page summary - everything you must have in your head before the exam.**

**Field patterns**
- Wire: concentric circles, thumb = current, fingers = field.
- Solenoid/coil: like a bar magnet; inside the coil the field is uniform (parallel lines), running S to N.
- End-on: clockwise = S pole, anticlockwise = N pole.
- Field lines: leave N, enter S; strength shown by line spacing.

**The force**
- $F = BIL$ when the wire is at right angles to the field.
- $F = BIL\sin\theta$ for an angle $\theta$ between wire and field.
- No force when the wire is parallel to the field.
- Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule - First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion (left hand!).

**The definition that always comes up**
- Magnetic flux density: force per unit length on a straight conductor carrying unit current at right angles to the field. $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{N A}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1} = 1~\text{kg A}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$.

**Measuring B**
- Current balance: $F = mg$ from the balance change, then $B = \frac{F}{IL}$.
- Hall probe: voltage $\propto B$ through its face; must be at right angles to the field.

**Parallel wires**
- Same direction: attract. Opposite directions: repel.
- Wire field at distance $x$: $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\frac{I}{x}$.

**Motors**
- Torque = force on one side $\times$ separation of the sides (a couple).
- Max torque: plane of coil parallel to the field. Zero torque: plane perpendicular to the field.
- More torque: more turns, bigger current, stronger field, bigger coil.

**Numbers to remember**
- $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}~\text{C}$, $g = 9.81~\text{m s}^{-2}$.
- Earth's horizontal field component is about $1.6 \times 10^{-5}~\text{T}$ (as used in Q8).
- Electric vs gravitational force on an electron: ratio about $10^{39}$.

**Flow for any force-on-wire calculation:** angle check ($90°$, $\theta$, or parallel) $\rightarrow$ convert to SI (cm to m, mA to A, mT to T) $\rightarrow$ substitute $\rightarrow$ unit $\rightarrow$ direction via left-hand rule.

## 7. Common Exam Mistakes

1. **Using the right hand with Fleming's rule.** The left-hand rule is for motors (force on a current). The right hand is only for field direction (grip rules). Mixing them up flips every direction answer.
2. **Using $F = BIL$ at an angle.** If the wire is at 50° to the field you must use $F = BIL\sin\theta$. The most common wrong answer in the whole chapter.
3. **Using the whole wire length.** Only the length of wire actually inside the magnetic field counts as $L$. A 3 m wire with 5.0 cm between the poles uses $L = 0.050~\text{m}$.
4. **Forgetting to convert units.** 3.0 cm = 0.030 m, 400 mA = 0.40 A, 5.0 mT = $5.0 \times 10^{-3}~\text{T}$. A force in newtons is impossible without SI.
5. **Saying opposite currents attract.** Parallel currents attract, anti-parallel currents repel - the exact opposite of electric charges and magnetic poles.
6. **Answering a definition question with the formula only.** 'The magnetic flux density is $B = \frac{F}{IL}$' is not the definition. You must say: force per unit length, unit current, at right angles to the field.
7. **Forgetting $g$ in current-balance questions.** The balance gives a mass; the force is $F = mg$. Using the mass in grams as if it were a force loses the mark.
8. **Giving the field direction instead of the force direction.** The force is always at right angles to both the field and the current. The question asks for the force direction - answer with the left-hand rule, not 'N to S'.
9. **Drawing a solenoid's outside field wrongly.** Inside the coil the field runs S to N (the same way the thumb points); outside it runs N to S. Both halves are needed.
10. **Saying a wire parallel to the field 'feels a small force'.** It feels NO force - $\sin 0° = 0$. There is no such thing as a small force here; it is exactly zero.
11. **Torque confusion.** Torque is force $\times$ perpendicular separation of the couple - not force $\times$ area, and not force $\times$ half the separation. Also, maximum torque occurs with the coil plane parallel to the field, not perpendicular.
12. **Skipping the direction in calculation questions.** Many force questions carry a direction mark. If you only give the magnitude, you can't get full marks. State 'at right angles to both field and current' at minimum.
13. **Newton's third law slips.** When the wire is fixed and the magnets are on the balance, the balance reading changes by the magnetic force on the WIRE (the magnets feel the equal reaction). Attributing the change to the wire's own weight loses the marks.
14. **Base-unit sloppiness.** $1~\text{T} = 1~\text{N A}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}$, not $\text{N A m}$. Work from $B = \frac{F}{IL}$ every time and you can't go wrong.
